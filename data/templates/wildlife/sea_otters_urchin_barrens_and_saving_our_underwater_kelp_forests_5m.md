If I asked you to picture a great forest, you would probably think of giant redwoods or the Amazon jungle. You probably wouldn't picture putting on a wetsuit and jumping into the freezing cold water off the coast of {{ argument_coastal_region_name }}.

Yet right beneath the waves lies a forest just as tall, just as lush, and even faster-growing than the trees on land: the giant kelp forest. In the spring, these golden underwater towers can grow {{ argument_daily_kelp_growth_statistic }} every single day, sheltering {{ argument_kelp_forest_species_list }}.

And standing guard over this entire underwater jungle is the cutest, furriest bodyguard in the ocean: the {{ argument_target_otter_species }}.

It sounds crazy that a floating ball of fur holding hands with its friends could hold up an entire forest -- until you see what happens when they disappear.

In {{ argument_collapse_year }}, along {{ argument_affected_coastline_name }}, the {{ argument_target_otter_species }} vanished from several bays after {{ argument_decline_cause_phrase }}. Within months, the lush golden kelp forests were completely gone. In their place was a desolate, rocky wasteland covered in millions of purple spiky balls: {{ argument_sea_urchin_species }}.

Here is the secret of how the kelp forest works. {{ argument_sea_urchin_species }} love to eat the bottom of kelp stalks. Normally, {{ argument_target_otter_species }} keep them in check. Because a {{ argument_target_otter_species }} doesn't have thick blubber like a whale, it has to eat {{ argument_daily_food_intake_percentage }} of its own body weight every single day just to stay warm in the icy water. And its favorite snack is {{ argument_sea_urchin_species }}.

When the {{ argument_target_otter_species }} disappeared, the urchins marched out of the rock cracks like an army of spiky lawnmowers and chewed the forest down to bare stone.

Even worse, once the urchins eat the last blade of kelp, they don't die off. Instead, they go into a dormant state -- what divers call zombie urchins. Empty of food inside, they can sit on the bare rocks for years, snapping up every tiny baby kelp spore before it can ever grow back. Even when a few {{ argument_target_otter_species }} returned, they ignored the zombie urchins because they were basically empty shells with no calories inside.

To jump-start the forest again in {{ argument_restoration_bay_name }}, our team of volunteer divers teamed up with local fishermen from {{ argument_local_fishing_community }}. We dove down and manually cleared {{ argument_cleared_area_statistic }} of zombie urchins so young kelp could take root.

Once the kelp grew back and the remaining urchins fattened up again, the {{ argument_target_otter_species }} moved right back in and took over their old job as full-time forest rangers.

Today, {{ argument_restoration_bay_name }} is a booming underwater jungle again, soaking up {{ argument_carbon_absorption_rate }} more carbon per acre than forests on land and protecting our beaches from winter storm waves. Sometimes, the best way to save a giant forest is to protect the little creature floating on top of it. Thank you.
