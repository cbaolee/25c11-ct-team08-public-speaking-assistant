There is an old legend told around evening fires across the savannas of {{ argument_savanna_region_name }}. It says that when the Creator first planted the {{ argument_target_baobab_species }}, the tree kept walking around and complaining so much that the Creator lost patience, pulled the tree out of the ground, and stuffed it back into the earth upside down -- with its roots sticking straight up into the sky and its head buried in the dirt.

Stand in front of an ancient {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} during the dry season when its leaves have dropped, and you will swear that legend is true. Its massive, bottle-shaped trunk -- glowing silvery-pink in the sunset and reaching {{ argument_trunk_circumference_statistic }} around -- is topped by gnarled, twisted branches that look exactly like a root system reaching for the clouds.

Across {{ argument_savanna_region_name }} and {{ argument_island_baobab_region }}, these trees aren't just plants; they are living villages, water reservoirs, and thousand-year-old monuments that anchor the entire dryland world.

How does a tree grow to the size of an apartment building in a thorny savanna where not a drop of rain falls for {{ argument_dry_season_months }} months of the year?

If you took a chainsaw to an oak or mahogany tree, the wood inside would be hard, dense, and dry. The trunk of a {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} is completely different: it is made of soft, spongy, fibrous tissue that works like a giant living water tank.

When the brief monsoon rains arrive in {{ argument_rainy_season_month }}, the tree's shallow root system -- which spreads outward underground farther than the tree is tall -- sucks up rainwater at lightning speed. A single ancient {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} can store over {{ argument_water_storage_liters_statistic }} liters of pure water inside its swollen trunk. During the worst droughts, the trunk actually shrinks and expands by {{ argument_trunk_shrinkage_centimeters }} centimeters like a breathing chest as the tree sips from its internal reservoir!

And even if an elephant or a storm rips a huge chunk of bark off the outside, a {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} doesn't die like normal trees do when they are girdled; it is one of the only trees on Earth that can regrow brand-new bark right over an open wound.

Wait until the sun goes down over {{ argument_savanna_region_name }}, and watch a blooming {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} come alive.

All day long, its large green flower buds hang upside down on long stalks like Christmas ornaments, tightly closed. Then, just as twilight falls, the buds pop open so fast you can literally watch the petals unfurl with the naked eye in {{ argument_flower_opening_minutes }} minutes -- releasing a musky, sour-sweet scent into the night breeze.

That scent is an open dinner invitation for the night shift: {{ argument_nocturnal_pollinator_bat_or_lemur }} and hawk moths, which dive face-first into the giant white pom-pom flowers to lap up nectar and carry pollen across miles of dark savanna before the flowers wilt at sunrise the very next morning.

Six months later, those flowers turn into fuzzy, hard-shelled pods packed with dry white fruit pulp that has {{ argument_vitamin_c_multiplier }} times more Vitamin C than an orange, {{ argument_calcium_multiplier }} times more calcium than milk, and more antioxidants than blueberries.

And who opens those hard pods and plants the seeds? The largest land animals on Earth: {{ argument_savanna_megafauna_disperser }} and baboons, which swallow the seeds whole and plant them in piles of manure {{ argument_dispersal_distance_km }} kilometers away.

In fact, in places like {{ argument_island_baobab_region }}, several species of giant {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} evolved to be dispersed by {{ argument_extinct_giant_lemur_or_bird_species }} that went extinct a thousand years ago -- leaving these ancient trees as "evolutionary ghosts" waiting for human hands to help scatter their seeds today!

Because {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} don't have normal annual tree rings you can count with a magnifying glass, scientists used accelerator mass spectrometry radiocarbon dating on wood samples from inside their hollow trunks. They found that the oldest living {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} are between {{ argument_min_ancient_age_years }} and {{ argument_max_ancient_age_years }} years old.

Over the past {{ argument_monitoring_years }} years, however, our research team and international colleagues documented something deeply alarming: {{ argument_dying_elders_count }} of the oldest, largest {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} in {{ argument_southern_savanna_region }} suddenly collapsed and died within a single decade.

They weren't killed by an infection or cut down by loggers. They died because Southern Africa is warming at {{ argument_regional_warming_rate }} the global average, and prolonged, multi-year droughts combined with extreme heat prevented these colossal elders from refilling their internal water tanks enough to support their massive crowns.

At the same time, in {{ argument_fenced_park_or_deforested_region }}, young baby {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} are struggling to grow up -- either cleared by slash-and-burn agriculture in {{ argument_island_baobab_region }} or chewed down as saplings by over-concentrated herds of elephants trapped inside fenced reserves during dry years.

How do we make sure the upside-down giants survive the twenty-first century? By working directly with the rural women and farmers who live beneath their branches.

In {{ argument_community_project_country }}, our team partnered with {{ argument_womens_cooperative_count }} rural women's cooperatives to turn the sustainable harvest of wild baobab fruit into an engine for conservation.

Think about the beauty of harvesting fruit from a {{ argument_target_baobab_species }}: you don't cut the tree down; you don't even pick the fruit off the branches! You simply wait until the ripe pods naturally dry on the tree and fall to the ground on their own.

By helping village women process that fallen wild fruit powder to international food-safety standards and selling it directly to fair-trade buyers for {{ argument_annual_community_revenue_statistic }} a year, every single mature {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} tree became a living savings account paying school fees and buying solar panels for the village.

And to make sure there are giant trees for their great-great-grandchildren, every cooperative runs a Community Baobab Nursery -- growing thousands of seedlings inside thorny protective cages until their trunks are thick enough to survive browsers, and wrapping vulnerable old trees in wire mesh to prevent bark stripping during droughts.

Inside the hollow trunk of one two-thousand-year-old {{ argument_target_baobab_species }} in {{ argument_famous_hollow_tree_village }}, thirty people can stand together in the cool shade out of the midday sun. For millennia, these gentle wooden giants have sheltered us, watered us, and fed us. Now it is our turn to shelter them. Thank you.
