It was a lost commercial gillnet -- {{ argument_net_length_statistic }} of heavy-duty braided nylon mesh that had snagged on the rocks during a storm {{ argument_estimated_abandonment_years }} earlier and snapped off from a fishing boat.

As we swam closer, the scene broke our hearts. Tangled inside the mesh were the skeletons of {{ argument_trapped_skeleton_count }} large fish, dozens of dead crabs that had crawled in to eat the fish and gotten trapped themselves, a drowned {{ argument_entangled_marine_mammal_or_turtle }}, and right near the top edge, a young {{ argument_rescued_animal_species }} still alive, thrashing exhaustedly with nylon twine cutting deep into its flipper.

We spent the next forty minutes with our dive shears carefully cutting that {{ argument_rescued_animal_species }} free and watching it swim up for a breath of air.

While we were cutting it loose, a chilling thought hit me: if our team hadn't happened to dive on that exact rock on that exact morning, that net would have kept catching and killing animals all by itself for the next {{ argument_plastic_persistence_centuries }} years.

When most of us hear about ocean plastic pollution, we picture floating plastic grocery bags, soda bottles, and drinking straws washing up on a beach. And don't get me wrong -- single-use consumer plastics are a massive problem that we have to stop at the source.

Yet when you look at the plastic that is actually most lethal to large marine wildlife -- whales, dolphins, seals, sea turtles, and sharks -- the number-one killer by far isn't a straw or a shopping bag. It is abandoned, lost, or discarded fishing gear, what divers and fishermen call ghost gear.

Every year, roughly {{ argument_annual_ghost_gear_tonnage }} tons of fishing nets, longlines, and crab pots are lost or dumped into the world's oceans. Up until the {{ argument_synthetic_net_transition_decade }}, fishing nets were woven out of natural hemp, cotton, or flax. If a fisherman lost a net in a storm back then, ocean bacteria ate the natural fibers and the net rotted away in a few months.

Today, almost every net on Earth is made of virtually indestructible synthetic nylon, polyethylene, and polypropylene. It doesn't rot. It doesn't rust.

Instead, a lost net follows a deadly, repeating loop on the seafloor: it catches a fish; the dead fish attracts scavengers like crabs, octopuses, and seals; the scavengers get tangled and die; their bodies rot away; the lightweight net floats back up off the bottom; and the cycle starts all over again.

Globally, ghost gear entangles and kills an estimated {{ argument_annual_marine_mammal_entanglements }} marine mammals and millions of fish and seabirds every single year, while also costing fishermen themselves up to {{ argument_lost_fishery_catch_percentage }} of their potential catch because ghost traps are killing the very crabs and fish they are trying to harvest to feed their families.

When I started {{ argument_ghost_gear_initiative_name }} in {{ argument_initiative_founding_year }}, I made a promise that we would never point fingers at fishermen as the bad guys.

Have you ever met a fisherman who wants to lose a {{ argument_commercial_net_cost_statistic }} fishing net? Of course not! For an artisanal or commercial fishing captain in {{ argument_fishing_port_name }}, losing a net is a massive financial blow.

When we interviewed hundreds of captains across {{ argument_surveyed_ports_count }} ports, we found that {{ argument_accidental_loss_percentage }} of ghost gear isn't thrown overboard on purpose. It happens for three reasons: sudden severe weather forces crews to cut gear loose to keep their boat from capsizing; gear snags on uncharted underwater rocks or shipwrecks; or two different fishing boats accidentally run over each other's lines in crowded fishing grounds at night.

And on the rare occasions when worn-out nets were dumped at sea, it was almost always because ports charged fishermen expensive landfill fees by the pound to throw old nets away on land, or had zero disposal bins on the dock at all.

To tackle this crisis, we built a four-part strategy that cleans up the mess of the past while preventing the ghost nets of tomorrow.

First, we had to find and remove the thousands of tons of ghost nets already sitting on reefs and seafloors across {{ argument_target_cleanup_region }}. Underwater cleanup is dangerous, technical work; a heavy waterlogged net can weigh {{ argument_waterlogged_net_weight_tons }} tons, and if a diver gets tangled while cutting it in strong current, it can be fatal.

Working alongside commercial fishermen during their off-season -- paying them a fair daily wage to use their boats and heavy hydraulic winches for conservation -- we combined side-scan sonar and underwater robotic drones (ROVs) to map ghost nets on the bottom before a single diver splashes in.

Once our trained diver teams rig heavy-duty inflatable lift bags to the net and pop it safely off the coral without breaking the reef, the fishing boat's winch hauls the monster net onto the deck.

Over the past {{ argument_cleanup_campaign_years }} years, our coalition of divers and fishing crews in {{ argument_target_cleanup_region }} has pulled {{ argument_recovered_gear_tonnage_statistic }} tons of ghost nets and {{ argument_recovered_traps_count }} lost crab traps out of the sea -- saving an estimated {{ argument_saved_marine_animals_estimate }} marine animals from a slow death.

Once you haul {{ argument_recovered_gear_tonnage_statistic }} tons of tangled, barnacle-covered nylon onto a dock in {{ argument_fishing_port_name }}, you face the next big question: what do you do with it? If you just truck it to an open landfill or burn it, you haven't solved the pollution problem.

Here is the secret hidden inside commercial fishing nets: the Nylon-6 used to make professional gillnets and purse seines is some of the highest-grade, strongest polymer plastic on the planet.

By setting up free net-collection bins at {{ argument_participating_harbors_count }} harbors and paying local women's cooperatives in {{ argument_coastal_community_name }} to sort, wash, and shred old and recovered nets, we created a circular supply chain. That shredded nylon is melted down into pure regenerated pellets that partner brands turn into {{ argument_recycled_products_list }}.

Today, fishermen in {{ argument_fishing_port_name }} don't dump torn nets at sea; they bring them back to the dock where they actually earn gear-buyback credits toward new, safer equipment.

Even with free recycling on every dock, storms will still happen and crab pots will still occasionally get lost. How do we stop a lost trap from killing for five hundred years?

First, we worked with marine engineers and fishery regulators in {{ argument_regulated_fishery_region }} to mandate a remarkably simple, fifty-cent fix on every crab and lobster pot: biodegradable escape panels.

One side of the wire or plastic trap is sewn shut with untreated natural cotton twine or fastened with a biodegradable polymer clip designed to dissolve in seawater after {{ argument_dissolution_timeframe_days }}. While the fisherman is actively checking the trap every few days, it works normally. If the line snaps in a winter storm and the trap is lost on the bottom, the clip dissolves in a month, the door swings wide open, and every trapped crab, lobster, and octopus walks right out free.

Second, our material science partners are now field-testing next-generation biopolymer gillnets made from bacteria-fermented PHA plastics that have the exact same breaking strength as nylon while fishing, but naturally break down into harmless water and carbon dioxide within {{ argument_biopolymer_breakdown_years }} if lost on the seafloor.

Finally, we are bringing transparency to the high seas by embedding tiny, low-cost RFID microchips and acoustic pingers into commercial fishing gear.

When a net has an electronic license plate tied to its owner, two things happen: if a captain loses a net in a storm, they can use an onboard sonar receiver to ping its exact location on the bottom and haul it back up -- saving themselves thousands of dollars. And if an illegal fishing vessel deliberately dumps gear inside a marine reserve, marine police know whose boat it came from.

Nobody wants an ocean full of immortal traps -- not divers, not consumers, and certainly not the fishermen whose livelihoods depend on a living sea.

When we team up with coastal communities, redesign our gear with nature in mind, and pull the ghosts of the past out of the water, we give the ocean's wildlife back the freedom to swim unharmed. Thank you.
