We spend billions of dollars pointing giant radio telescopes into deep space, hoping that one day we will make contact with an intelligent alien life form.

I have some good news that could save space agencies a lot of money: the aliens are already here. You just need a mask, a snorkel, and a ticket to {{ argument_dive_site_location }} to meet one face-to-face.

I am talking about the {{ argument_target_octopus_species }}. Think about our evolutionary family tree for a second. The last time humans and octopuses shared a common ancestor was {{ argument_common_ancestor_age_statistic }} ago -- and that ancestor was a tiny, flat worm with no eyes and no brain at all.

That means evolution built big, smart brains twice on planet Earth: once in creatures with backbones like us, and once in a soft-bodied clam cousin with eight arms and blue blood.

When I first started diving in {{ argument_dive_site_location }} in {{ argument_study_start_year }}, I thought I was observing {{ argument_target_octopus_species }}. Within ten minutes, it was completely obvious that she was observing me.

To understand how an octopus experiences the ocean, forget everything you know about how your own body works. You have one central brain locked inside a hard skull. An octopus has roughly {{ argument_neuron_count_statistic }} neurons -- about the same as a {{ argument_mammal_comparison_animal }} -- but two-thirds of its brain isn't even in its head.

Instead, its nervous system is spread out across its eight arms. Each arm has its own mini-brain that can touch, smell, taste, and make local decisions without asking the central brain for permission. And its skin? Even though the octopus is colorblind, its skin is packed with millions of tiny color-changing sacs called chromatophores and light-sensing proteins. In less than {{ argument_camouflage_speed_seconds }}, it can morph its color, pattern, and 3D skin texture to look exactly like {{ argument_mimicry_examples_list }}.

For a long time, skeptics asked: "Sure, they have cool skin, but are they actually thinking?" Then we watched what wild {{ argument_target_octopus_species }} were doing on the open sandy bottom of {{ argument_tropical_bay_name }}.

Out on the flat sand, a soft-bodied octopus with no shell is an easy lunch for {{ argument_marine_predator_list }}. While diving there, we filmed an octopus rummaging through garbage on the seafloor until it found two discarded coconut shell halves.

It didn't just hide under one right then and there. It stacked the two bowls together like nesting Tupperware, tucked them underneath its body, and walked stiff-legged on the tips of its arms across {{ argument_carrying_distance_statistic }} of open seafloor -- carrying them for future use. Later, when a predator swam near, it pulled the two halves together into a tight armored ball and hid inside.

In biology, carrying an object not because you need it right now, but because you know you will need it later, is the textbook definition of foresight and tool use.

And in aquariums and rescue centers, their personalities are unmistakable. Some are shy; some are curious; and some are downright mischievous. We have documented individuals learning how to unscrew childproof jars from the inside, squirting jet-streams of water specifically at one annoying aquarium light to short-circuit the bulb so they can sleep in the dark, and playing with floating pill bottles in the current the way a puppy plays with a ball.

Here is the most haunting mystery about the {{ argument_target_octopus_species }}: with all that brainpower, curiosity, and tool-making skill, how long do you think they live?

Most species live for only {{ argument_octopus_lifespan_years }}.

Further, they have zero social life and zero parenting. After a mother lays her {{ argument_egg_count_statistic }} eggs inside a rocky den, she stops eating entirely. She spends the last {{ argument_brooding_duration_weeks }} of her life gently blowing fresh oxygen over her eggs and guarding them from crabs, starving herself so she dies just as her babies hatch and drift away into the current alone.

Think about what that means. A baby human or a baby chimpanzee is smart because its parents and grandparents spend years teaching it how to use tools and find food. A baby {{ argument_target_octopus_species }} never meets its mother. Every single individual has to figure out how to hunt, how to disguise itself, and how to outsmart predators completely from scratch in twenty-four months.

Over the past decade, as global fish stocks have declined, commercial fishing fleets in {{ argument_fishing_region_name }} have turned heavily toward trapping {{ argument_target_octopus_species }}, and industrial companies have begun proposing high-density underwater octopus farms.

Imagine packing thousands of solitary, high-strung, hyper-intelligent problem-solvers into barren underwater cages with nothing to do. Because of the behavioral research done by divers and neurobiologists over the last {{ argument_research_decade_count }} years, countries like {{ argument_welfare_law_country }} have officially recognized cephalopods as sentient beings in animal welfare law, and lawmakers in {{ argument_banning_jurisdiction_name }} recently passed the world's first pre-emptive ban on industrial octopus farming.

You don't have to look like a human or walk on two legs to have a rich inner life.

Next time you look out over the ocean, remember that down in the kelp and coral reefs, eight-armed minds are watching, learning, and solving puzzles in ways we are only beginning to imagine. Thank you.
