# How a Moss Builds Its Own Pickling Jar

How on earth does a plant with no roots, no wood, and no branches beat every tree on the planet at storing carbon?

Think about what happens when a leaf falls off a tree in a normal forest. Within a year, bacteria, fungi, and earthworms eat that dead leaf and breathe **{{ decomposition_carbon_return_percentage }} of its carbon** right back out into the sky as carbon dioxide.

**{{ target_moss_species }}** breaks that rule completely. It is a master **ecosystem engineer** that literally pickles its own dead body so nothing can ever eat it.

First, {{ target_moss_species }} acts like a super-absorbent sponge. Its leaves are made mostly of giant, empty dead cells with tiny pores that can soak up and hold **{{ water_holding_capacity_multiplier }} times the moss's own dry weight in rainwater** -- raising the water table until the ground is completely waterlogged and zero oxygen can get down into the soil.

Second, the living moss actively exchanges ions with the rainwater, pumping out hydrogen ions until the bog water becomes **as acidic as {{ acidity_comparison_liquid }}** -- while releasing special phenolic compounds called *sphagnan* that tan dead plant matter the exact same way leather tanners preserve cowhide.

With no oxygen, high acid, and natural tanning chemicals, bacteria and fungi simply cannot work. When the bottom of the {{ target_moss_species }} stem dies, **it never rots**.

> Instead of returning to the atmosphere, every gram of carbon that {{ target_moss_species }} pulls out of the air stays locked at the bottom of the bog for **ten thousand years**. It is so preservative that when peat cutters in {{ archaeological_bog_region }} accidentally uncover Iron Age "bog bodies" buried {{ bog_body_age_years }} years ago, the people still have their eyelashes, fingerprints, and last meal preserved inside their stomachs!

Because a peat bog is so wet, so acidic, and so low in nutrients, ordinary plants and animals can't live there. Instead, peatlands are sanctuaries for some of the coolest, rarest wildlife on Earth.

# Draining the Sponge, Lighting the Bomb

For centuries, humans looked at peat bogs and saw only soggy, useless swamps that needed to be "improved."

Across {{ drained_regions_list }}, we dug millions of kilometers of straight **drainage ditches** to dry out the peat so we could plant commercial timber plantations, graze sheep, strip-mine peat bricks for power stations, or -- most absurd of all -- bag up ten-thousand-year-old peat moss to sell at garden centers as **cheap potting compost** for petunias!

And in {{ tropical_peatland_region }}, industrial conglomerates bulldozed the peat swamp forests and dug deep drainage canals to plant monocultures of **{{ industrial_plantation_crop }}**.

Watch what happens the moment you dig a ditch and drain the water out of a peat bog. Oxygen rushes down into the dry peat for the first time in ten millennia. Immediately, dormant bacteria wake up and start feasting on ten thousand years of stored carbon -- turning a **carbon sink** into a roaring **carbon smokestack**.

Even worse, dry peat is literally **young coal**. When a stray spark hits a drained peatland during an El Niño drought like the one in {{ peat_fire_crisis_year }}, the fire doesn't just burn the trees on top; **the ground itself catches fire**, smoldering meters underground for months where even monsoon rains struggle to put it out.

> Today, damaged and drained peatlands -- covering just **0.4 percent** of the Earth's land -- emit **{{ drained_peat_global_emissions_percentage }} of all global human-caused greenhouse gas emissions** every single year: more than the entire global aviation and shipping industries combined!

# Plugging the Ditches and Farming Wet

Here is the thrilling news: **fixing a broken peatland is one of the fastest, cheapest climate wins on the planet**.

To stop a drained bog from leaking millions of tons of carbon and catching fire, you don't have to wait fifty years for slow trees to grow. You just have to **plug the ditches** so the rainwater stays on the land!

Over the past {{ restoration_years_count }} years, across {{ rewetting_project_site }} and in partnership with village Fire-Free Communities in {{ tropical_peatland_region }}, our teams have been building simple peat dams, timber sluices, and contour bunds across thousands of kilometers of old drainage canals.

On bare scars where peat mining stripped the surface down to black dirt, we use helicopters and low-ground-pressure tractors to spread **"moss smoothies"** -- tiny fragments of living {{ target_moss_species }} mixed with protective straw mulch that knit back into a living, self-watering moss carpet in just **{{ moss_carpet_recovery_years }} years**.

And for farmers living on drained peatlands in {{ agricultural_peat_region }}, we are pioneering **paludiculture** -- or **wet farming** -- growing profitable wet-loving crops like {{ paludiculture_crops_list }} right on rewetted bogs so farmers keep making a great living while keeping the carbon locked safely underwater.

> Within weeks of raising the water table back to the surface at {{ rewetting_project_site }}, carbon emissions dropped by **{{ emissions_drop_percentage }}**, wildfire risk plummeted to near zero, and the bog filtered rainwater so cleanly that local water treatment costs fell by {{ water_treatment_savings_percentage }}.
