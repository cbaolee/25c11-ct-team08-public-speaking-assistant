Picture a tiny, shy porpoise no longer than a ten-year-old child, swimming in the warm, shallow waters at the very top of {{ argument_gulf_location_name }}.

Around its eyes are dark black rings like a panda's, and around its lips is a curved black line that makes it look like it is smiling at you.

Local fishermen call her the {{ argument_target_porpoise_species }} -- Spanish for "the little cow." She lives in only one tiny patch of water on planet Earth, smaller than {{ argument_range_size_comparison_city }}.

And as I stand on this stage tonight, there are around {{ argument_surviving_individuals_count }} of them left alive in the entire world. Not {{ argument_surviving_individuals_count }} thousand. Just {{ argument_surviving_individuals_count }}.

The {{ argument_target_porpoise_species }} is the most endangered marine mammal on Earth -- sitting on the absolute knife-edge of the Red List.

Here is the heartbreaking twist: nobody has ever hunted a {{ argument_target_porpoise_species }} on purpose. Nobody eats their meat, and nobody buys their fins.

Every single {{ argument_target_porpoise_species }} that dies is an accidental victim of a black-market crime aimed at a completely different animal: a giant endangered fish living in the exact same water called the {{ argument_target_fish_species }}.

Why are criminal cartels setting miles of illegal gillnets at night to catch the {{ argument_target_fish_species }}? Not even for the fish itself, but solely for its swim bladder -- the gas bag inside the fish -- which smugglers dry and sell on the black market in {{ argument_destination_market_region }} for up to {{ argument_swim_bladder_price_per_kilo }} a kilogram, earning it the nickname "aquatic cocaine."

Because a {{ argument_target_porpoise_species }} is almost the exact same size as a {{ argument_target_fish_species }}, when it swims into one of those invisible nylon walls in the dark, it gets tangled, cannot swim up to the surface to breathe air, and drowns in minutes. Between {{ argument_decline_start_year }} and {{ argument_decline_end_year }}, illegal nets wiped out {{ argument_population_loss_percentage }} of the entire species.

When our acoustic and visual survey team went out into the Zero Tolerance Area of {{ argument_gulf_location_name }} in {{ argument_recent_survey_year }}, critics told us to give up. They said, "With only {{ argument_surviving_individuals_count }} animals left, aren't they doomed by inbreeding anyway?"

Then two things happened that proved the critics wrong.

First, genomic scientists sequenced the DNA of the {{ argument_target_porpoise_species }} and discovered that because this species has always lived in a tiny, naturally small population for {{ argument_evolutionary_history_years }} years, it purged its harmful genetic mutations thousands of years ago!

Second, when our observers looked through high-powered binoculars from our research ship, the {{ argument_surviving_individuals_count }} {{ argument_target_porpoise_species }} we spotted were plump, healthy, actively feeding -- and swimming right beside a mother was a brand-new, healthy baby calf!

The {{ argument_target_porpoise_species }} hasn't given up on living; she just needs us to get the nets out of her home.

In {{ argument_enforcement_year }}, working alongside the Navy and conservation ships in {{ argument_gulf_location_name }}, authorities dropped {{ argument_concrete_blocks_count }} massive concrete blocks fitted with steel hooks onto the seafloor of the Zero Tolerance Area to snag and rip apart any illegal gillnet dragged into the sanctuary, cutting illegal netting inside the core zone by {{ argument_net_reduction_percentage }}.

Today, by supporting local fishing families in {{ argument_local_fishing_towns }} to transition to vaquita-safe handline gear and sustainable aquaculture, we are fighting for every single breath of those last {{ argument_surviving_individuals_count }} survivors.

Ten is a terrifyingly small number -- but ten is not zero. As long as mothers are still having calves in {{ argument_gulf_location_name }}, the fight to keep the little smiling porpoise off the extinct list is still on. Thank you.
