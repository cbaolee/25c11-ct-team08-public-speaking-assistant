# Introduction: Forty-Two Days Searching a Silent River

In the winter of **{{ expedition_year }}**, an international team of thirty top marine biologists from {{ participating_countries_count }} countries boarded two research vessels at the mouth of the **{{ river_name }}** and began sailing slowly upstream for **{{ survey_distance_km }} kilometers**.

On the roof of the boats stood observers with high-powered military binoculars scanning every ripple on the water. Trailing behind the hulls were underwater **hydrophone arrays** listening for high-pitched ultrasonic clicks and whistles.

We were looking for a living legend: the **{{ extinct_river_dolphin_species }}**.

For **{{ evolutionary_age_million_years }} million years** -- since long before the first human ever walked along the banks of the {{ river_name }} -- this pale blue-gray freshwater dolphin with a long, upturned beak had swum through these muddy waters. Local fishermen revered her as the **"Goddess of the {{ river_name }},"** believing that spotting her graceful white fin brought protection from storms.

For **forty-two straight days**, our ships sailed up and down the river. We looked through the binoculars until our eyes burned. We listened to the hydrophones for {{ listening_hours_total }} hours.

When we tied up at the dock on the final evening of December {{ expedition_year }}, the tally sheet was completely blank.

We hadn't seen a single fin. We hadn't heard a single click.

> For the first time in modern history, humanity had driven an **entire evolutionary family of cetaceans** -- a whale or dolphin species -- off the face of the planet, not by hunting it, but simply by turning its river into an **industrial highway**.

# Why Rivers Are the Hardest Place to Be a Giant

When we talk about endangered wildlife, almost all our attention goes to the **green forests** on land and the **blue oceans** at sea.

We forget the thin silver threads connecting them: **Earth's freshwater rivers and lakes**.

Think about this astonishing statistic: freshwater rivers and lakes cover **less than {{ freshwater_surface_percentage }} of the Earth's surface** -- yet they are home to **{{ freshwater_fish_percentage }} of all fish species** and nearly **one-third of all vertebrate species on planet Earth**!

And since {{ monitoring_baseline_year }}, according to the global Living Planet Index, while land wildlife populations have declined by {{ terrestrial_decline_pct }} and ocean populations by {{ marine_decline_pct }}, **freshwater wildlife populations have crashed by a staggering {{ freshwater_decline_percentage }}** -- faster than any other biome on the globe!

Why are river giants -- what biologists call **freshwater megafauna** -- in such terrible danger?

Because in the open ocean, if an oil spill or a noisy fleet of ships ruins one bay, a whale can swim five hundred kilometers west into open water.

> A river animal lives inside a **one-dimensional ribbon of water**. It cannot climb up onto the riverbank to walk around a concrete dam, and it cannot swim away when pollution or ship propellers fill its narrow channel from bank to bank.

# The Death of Two River Titans

The **{{ extinct_river_dolphin_species }}** wasn't the only giant we lost in the {{ river_name }} during our lifetime.

Just sixteen years after our empty dolphin expedition, in **{{ paddlefish_extinction_year }}**, the IUCN Red List officially declared the **{{ extinct_giant_fish_species }}** -- the **"King of Freshwater Fish,"** a silver giant that could grow up to **{{ max_fish_length_meters }} meters long**, weigh {{ max_fish_weight_kg }} kilograms, and used an electrosensory sword-like snout to hunt fish in murky water since the days of the **Late Cretaceous dinosaurs** -- **100% Extinct**.

How did a dolphin that survived twenty million years and a fish that survived the asteroid that killed *T. rex* both vanish in the same generation?

Look at what happened to the {{ river_name }} between the 1950s and the 2000s:

1. **Deadly fishing gear:** Fishermen used **"rolling hooks"** -- long bottom lines studded with thousands of unbaited razor-sharp hooks that snagged dolphins to death -- alongside illegal **electro-fishing**, where battery cables dropped into the water electrocuted every living creature in the channel.
2. **Acoustic blindness:** Because river water is dark and muddy, the {{ extinct_river_dolphin_species }} had tiny, nearly blind eyes and relied entirely on **echolocation sonar** to find fish and navigate. As thousands of roaring diesel cargo barges filled the river, the underwater engine noise blinded their sonar so they swam straight into spinning **ship propellers**.
3. **Concrete walls:** When massive dams like the **{{ major_dam_name }}** were built across the main river in {{ dam_construction_year }}, they blocked the {{ extinct_giant_fish_species }} from swimming upstream to the only gravelly rapids in the upper basin where the fish could lay its eggs!

# Turning Tragedy Into a Ten-Year Rescue for the Survivors

When the {{ extinct_river_dolphin_species }} was declared functionally extinct in {{ expedition_year }}, one species of cetacean still remained alive in that exact same river: the **{{ surviving_porpoise_species }}** -- a round-headed, chubby freshwater porpoise with a permanent mischievous grin that had dropped down to **just {{ lowest_porpoise_count }} individuals** and was shrinking by {{ annual_porpoise_decline_rate }} a year.

Everyone said, *"In fifteen years, the {{ surviving_porpoise_species }} will follow the {{ extinct_river_dolphin_species }} into the history books."*

Instead, that painful wakeup call triggered the most sweeping **river conservation turnaround** in history!

First, scientists realized we couldn't wait while the main river was still dangerous. So we established **{{ oxbow_reserve_count }} natural "oxbow lake" reserves** -- old curved loops of the river cut off from shipping traffic where water quality is clean and zero cargo ships or fishing nets ever enter -- moving founder groups of {{ surviving_porpoise_species }} into safe waters where they immediately started having **{{ annual_oxbow_calves_count }} healthy baby calves a year**!

Second, in **{{ fishing_ban_year }}**, the national government did something many thought was politically impossible: it enacted a **complete, ten-year commercial fishing ban** across the entire main stem of the {{ river_name }} and {{ protected_tributaries_count }} major lakes and tributaries -- retiring **{{ retired_fishing_boats_count }} fishing vessels**, helping {{ transitioned_fishermen_count }} former fishermen transition to onshore jobs and river patrol rangers, shutting down polluting riverside chemical plants, and banning rolling hooks and electro-fishing forever!

> Look at what happened just **three years** after the nets came out of the water: in the {{ recent_census_year }} river-wide survey, for the **first time in forty years**, the population of **{{ surviving_porpoise_species }}** stopped falling and **jumped upward by {{ population_rebound_percentage }}** to {{ current_porpoise_count }} animals -- with mothers and calves now leaping right beneath the city bridges of {{ riverside_city_name }}!

# A Blueprint for the World's Last River Dolphins and Giants

Today, we are taking the hard-won lessons of the {{ river_name }} to save the **five remaining species of river dolphins** and the great freshwater giants still swimming in the **Amazon, the Ganges, the Indus, the Mekong, and the Irrawaddy**.

In {{ south_asian_or_amazon_river }}, working alongside local fishing communities through the **{{ global_river_dolphin_declaration }}**:

* We are installing cheap, battery-powered **acoustic pingers** on legal fishing nets that emit a gentle high-pitched beep, warning **{{ surviving_river_dolphin_example }}** away from nets and cutting entanglement deaths to near zero.
* On gated barrages and irrigation canals along the {{ indus_or_ganges_river }} where dolphins get trapped when water levels drop in the dry season, our **Canal Rescue Brigades** have safely rescued and returned **over {{ canal_rescued_dolphins_count }} stranded river dolphins** back into the main river!
* And in the **Mekong River**, home to the **{{ mekong_giant_fish_examples }}** -- including a freshwater stingray weighing {{ giant_stingray_weight_kg }} kilograms that our team recently tagged and released -- fishermen who accidentally catch a rare giant now call our **24-hour Fish Rescue Hotline** to have the fish weighed, tagged, and released alive in exchange for a fair conservation reward!

> We cannot bring back the Goddess of the {{ river_name }}. Her silence is the price of waiting too long.

Yet every time a smiling {{ surviving_porpoise_species }} calf breaks the surface of a healing river today, it proves that when we pull the nets out, quiet the engines, and let our rivers breathe, freshwater life comes roaring back. Thank you.
