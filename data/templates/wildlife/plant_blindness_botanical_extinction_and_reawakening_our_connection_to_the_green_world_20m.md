# Why Our Brains Ignore Ninety-Nine Percent of Life

Why do human beings suffer from plant blindness? First, blame our evolutionary wiring.

Your visual cortex processes roughly **ten million bits of data** every second from your eyes, yet your conscious mind can only pay attention to about **forty bits** at a time. To keep our ancestors alive on the savanna, our brains evolved a strict filter: **only pay attention to things that move, have eyes facing you, or change color quickly** -- because a plant has never jumped out of a bush and chased a human down for lunch!

Because plants move on a timescale of hours and seasons rather than seconds, grow without faces, and blend together in shades of green, our brain's spam filter automatically deletes them from our conscious awareness.

Second, blame our modern education and urban lives. An average ten-year-old child in {{ modern_country_name }} today can easily name **over {{ corporate_logos_known_count }} corporate brand logos** and pokemon characters at a glance -- yet cannot name **five wild native plants** growing within fifty meters of their own front door.

In universities across {{ academic_decline_region }}, standalone **Botany departments** have been shut down or merged out of existence over the past thirty years, leaving us with a critical shortage of field botanists who can actually tell an endangered wild species from a common weed at the exact moment the planet needs them most.

# Putting on Botanical Glasses: What Plants Are Actually Doing

How do we cure plant blindness? We don't cure it by forcing people to memorize dusty Latin names on flashcards. We cure it by changing the speed of the camera and showing people **what wild plants are actually doing** when we think they are standing still.

When you put a **time-lapse camera** on a forest floor in {{ time_lapse_forest_site }} and speed up a week into ten seconds, the "passive green wallpaper" turns into a ferocious gladiatorial arena!

You see wild **{{ climbing_vine_species }}** whipping its tip around in circles like a radar dish -- sniffing the air for the chemical scent of its favorite host plant and steering straight toward it while actively avoiding toxic neighbors.

You discover that plants have **more senses than humans do**. While they don't have eyes or ears made of flesh, a wild plant can sense **{{ plant_senses_count }} distinct environmental signals**: it sees the exact color ratio of red to far-red light bouncing off a neighbor's leaf before the neighbor even casts a shadow on it; it feels the exact frequency of a **{{ chewing_caterpillar_species }}**'s jaws vibrating on a leaf -- distinguishing the vibration of a chewing caterpillar from the vibration of the wind or a harmless raindrop -- and immediately floods its veins with mustard-oil toxins!

> Once you realize that every tree, vine, and wildflower you walk past is tasting the soil, smelling the air, listening to vibrations, and trading with fungi underground, **you can never see "green wallpaper" again**.

# Learning Our Neighbors' Names

I want to leave you with one simple challenge for tomorrow morning when you walk out your front door on the way to work or school.

Before you look at your phone, stop at the very first wild tree or weed growing by the side of the path. Touch its bark or its leaf. Notice how its veins are built to catch the sun and channel the rain. Learn its name, and learn the name of just **one insect or bird** that calls that plant home.

> The day you know the name and the story of a plant, it stops being scenery -- and becomes a **neighbor**.

Without animals, the world of plants would look a little different, a little quieter, and still go right on blooming. Without plants, there is no oxygen in our lungs, no soil on our rocks, no rain in our clouds, and no wildlife anywhere on Earth. Let's open our eyes, cure our plant blindness, and give the green makers of our world the love and protection they have earned for half a billion years. Thank you.
