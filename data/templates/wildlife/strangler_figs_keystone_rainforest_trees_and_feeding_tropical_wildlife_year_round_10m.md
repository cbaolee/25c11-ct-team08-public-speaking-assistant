If you are trying to win a popularity contest in the plant world, being named the "Strangler Fig" does not do you any favors. It sounds like the villain in a horror movie.

And if you hike into the rainforest of {{ argument_rainforest_reserve_name }} and look at how a {{ argument_target_fig_species }} grows, you can see why early explorers gave it that name.

Unlike almost every other tree in the woods that sprouts on the dark forest floor and struggles upward toward the light, a {{ argument_target_fig_species }} starts its life at the top. A bird or a monkey drops a tiny fig seed into a mossy crack high on the branch of a giant host tree -- say, a {{ argument_host_tree_species }}.

From that high perch in the sun, the baby fig sends long, muscular roots snaking all the way down the trunk of the host tree into the ground, wrapping the host in a tight lattice of living wooden cables until, decades later, the host tree dies and rots away -- leaving the {{ argument_target_fig_species }} standing as a hollow, cathedral-like tower of roots.

It looks like cold-blooded botanical murder. Yet if you talk to any tropical wildlife biologist on Earth, they will tell you that this "strangler" is actually the number-one life-saving hero of the entire rainforest.

Why is the {{ argument_target_fig_species }} -- along with its {{ argument_global_fig_species_count }} cousins around the tropics -- considered the ultimate keystone plant of the jungle?

Think about how fruit trees work in most forests. Most tropical trees fruit all at once during the rainy season in {{ argument_peak_fruiting_months }}. For two months, there is a massive feast of mangoes, nuts, and berries everywhere. Then, for {{ argument_lean_season_months }} of the dry season, almost every tree in the forest stops fruiting at the same time.

Without fruit during the lean months, fruit-eating animals -- {{ argument_frugivore_wildlife_list }} -- would starve to death.

Enter the {{ argument_target_fig_species }}. Unlike every other tree in {{ argument_rainforest_reserve_name }} that follows the calendar, fig trees fruit completely out of sync with each other all year round. In January, Tree A is dripping with {{ argument_figs_per_crop_statistic }} sweet figs; in February, Tree B ripens half a mile away; in March, Tree C explodes with fruit right in the middle of a drought.

Because of that staggered schedule, figs feed over {{ argument_vertebrate_species_fed_count }} species of birds and mammals -- more wildlife than any other plant genus on planet Earth. In the dry season in {{ argument_rainforest_reserve_name }}, figs make up {{ argument_lean_season_diet_percentage }} of the entire diet of fruit-eating primates, hornbills, and fruit bats.

How did figs pull off this year-round fruiting miracle? Through the oldest, tightest partnership in the history of evolution: a deal struck {{ argument_coevolution_age_million_years }} million years ago with a wasp no bigger than the period at the end of a sentence.

Here is a fun botanical fact that surprises people at dinner parties: a fig is not actually a fruit. It is an inside-out bouquet of hundreds of tiny flowers locked inside a hollow green ball, with only a microscopic keyhole at the bottom called an ostiole.

No normal bee or butterfly can get inside that ball to pollinate the flowers. Only one creature on Earth has the key to that specific fig species: its partner {{ argument_fig_wasp_species }}.

A pregnant female wasp -- carrying pollen from the fig tree she was born in -- squeezes so tightly through that keyhole that her wings and antennae literally rip off on the way in. Once inside the dark hollow fig, she pollinates the flowers, lays her eggs in a few of them, and dies. Weeks later, her sons and daughters hatch, mate, load up on fresh pollen, chew an exit tunnel, and fly off to find the next {{ argument_target_fig_species }} that is ready to bloom.

Because the wasp only lives for {{ argument_wasp_lifespan_hours }} hours as an adult, there always has to be another {{ argument_target_fig_species }} blooming somewhere in the forest that week -- otherwise the wasps would die out, and the fig trees would go extinct right along with them!

Beyond feeding the jungle, that hollow lattice trunk of the "strangler" is the best apartment building in the tropics. Inside its dark, cool central chimney roost {{ argument_hollow_roost_species_list }}, while its wide, gnarled roots survive hurricanes that blow solid-trunked trees right over because the wind passes straight through the gaps in its lattice.

When our restoration team in {{ argument_degraded_pasture_site }} tried to regrow a cleared cattle pasture back into rainforest ten years ago, planting fifty different species of rainforest trees by hand was slow and expensive.

So we tried a shortcut inspired by the framework species method: we planted just a handful of fast-growing {{ argument_target_fig_species }} seedlings spaced across the empty grass field.

Within {{ argument_fruiting_maturity_years }} years, those young fig trees started fruiting. Immediately, flocks of {{ argument_seed_dispersing_birds_and_bats }} flew out of the distant forest to eat the figs -- and while sitting on the branches, they pooped out thousands of seeds from {{ argument_recruited_tree_species_count }} other rainforest tree species right onto the pasture below!

By planting just one keystone tree, the animals of the forest came back and planted the rest of the jungle for us for free. Never judge a tree by a scary name -- sometimes the "strangler" is the very heart keeping the rainforest beating. Thank you.
