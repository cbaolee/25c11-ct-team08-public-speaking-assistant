That statistic is not a geographical coincidence. And yet, for more than a century, mainstream Western conservation operated on the exact opposite premise -- the catastrophic idea that to save nature, you had to empty it of the very people who had kept it alive for millennia.

To understand why modern conservation is at a crossroads, we have to confront the dark history of what historians call fortress conservation.

Beginning in the late nineteenth century, when colonial administrations and early conservation organizations established the first national parks across {{ argument_historical_example_regions_list }}, they imported a romanticized myth of "untouched, virgin wilderness."

They looked at teeming savannas and rich forests and assumed that because there were no factories or paved roads, no human hands had shaped them. In reality, those landscapes were biocultural mosaics carefully tended through rotational agroforestry, selective harvesting, sacred taboos, and seasonal fire management by {{ argument_indigenous_group_examples }}.

Under the fortress model, millions of Indigenous and local stewards were forcibly evicted from their ancestral homelands at gunpoint, turned overnight into "trespassers" and "poachers" when they gathered medicinal plants or hunted for subsistence.

And what happened when you kicked the ancestral guardians out of the forest and replaced them with underfunded government fences? Often, the ecosystem actually degraded.

Without constant community presence on the land, corrupt logging concessions, illegal gold miners, and commercial poaching syndicates moved into the vacuum, while displaced communities sitting in poverty outside the park boundaries grew understandably hostile toward the wildlife inside.
