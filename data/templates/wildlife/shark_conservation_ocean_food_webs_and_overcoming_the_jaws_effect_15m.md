All I have to do is hum two alternating bass notes -- duh-dun... duh-dun... -- and every single person in this room immediately pictures a dorsal fin slicing through the water.

When the movie Jaws hit theaters in the summer of {{ argument_movie_release_year }}, it didn't just invent the modern summer blockbuster; it triggered a global wave of panic and trophy hunting that marine biologists call the Jaws Effect. Overnight, an ancient ocean predator that had kept the seas healthy for {{ argument_shark_evolutionary_age_statistic }} -- surviving four mass extinctions and swimming the oceans before there were even trees on land -- was recast in the public mind as a vindictive, man-eating monster that deserved to be wiped out. Even the book's author, {{ argument_author_name }}, spent the last years of his life apologizing and campaigning to save the animals his story had demonized.

Let's look at the real numbers for a second. In an average year worldwide, sharks are responsible for roughly {{ argument_annual_human_fatalities_by_sharks }} human fatalities -- fewer than falling coconuts, lightning strikes, or backyard lawnmowers.

Now flip that coin over: how many sharks do humans kill every single year?

Roughly {{ argument_annual_sharks_killed_by_humans }} sharks are pulled out of the ocean every year -- that works out to about {{ argument_sharks_killed_per_minute }} sharks killed every single minute as I stand on this stage.

We are terrified of them, yet we have wiped out over {{ argument_global_pelagic_shark_decline_percentage }} of the world's open-ocean sharks and rays in just fifty years.

Why should you be far more afraid of an ocean without sharks than an ocean with them? Because after {{ argument_diving_experience_years }} of diving face-to-face with {{ argument_target_shark_species_list }} without a cage in {{ argument_primary_dive_region }}, I can tell you that sharks are not mindless killers; they are the doctors and traffic cops of the marine food web.

Think about what a {{ argument_reef_apex_shark_species }} does on a coral reef. First, it preys on the sick, the slow, and the weak fish, stopping diseases from spreading through fish schools and keeping the gene pool strong.

Second, and even more important, just by cruising along the reef drop-off, sharks keep mid-sized predatory fish like {{ argument_mesopredator_fish_species }} on the move and stop them from eating all the herbivorous {{ argument_reef_grazer_fish_species }}.

Why do we need {{ argument_reef_grazer_fish_species }} on a reef? Because they are the full-time scrubbing crew that eat fast-growing seaweed off the coral so baby corals have clean rock to settle on.

In our comparative dives between remote, shark-filled atolls in {{ argument_pristine_atoll_name }} and heavily fished reefs in {{ argument_overfished_reef_name }}, the difference hits you the moment you put your face in the water. Where the sharks are gone, {{ argument_mesopredator_fish_species }} take over, wipe out the {{ argument_reef_grazer_fish_species }}, and within a few years, thick mats of slimy algae smother the coral reef to death.

Remove the shark from the top of the reef, and the entire rainbow cathedral beneath it turns into a mossy ruin.

Where are {{ argument_annual_sharks_killed_by_humans }} sharks going every year? Many people know about the cruel practice of shark finning -- slicing off a shark's fins for luxury soup and throwing the living body back into the sea to drown. Thanks to public campaigns and import bans in {{ argument_fin_ban_countries_list }}, the high-end fin trade has finally started to shrink.

Yet an even bigger, quieter killer remains out on the high seas: industrial bycatch and the growing market for cheap shark meat and squalene liver oil used in {{ argument_consumer_products_using_shark }}.

Every night across {{ argument_high_seas_ocean_name }}, industrial longline vessels set fishing lines stretching {{ argument_longline_length_statistic }} across the water, dangling up to {{ argument_hooks_per_line_statistic }} baited hooks aimed at tuna and swordfish. Sharks are drawn to the bait, get hooked on the lines, and suffocate before the boat even hauls the gear back in.

And unlike tuna or cod that lay millions of tiny eggs at once, sharks reproduce just like mammals. A female {{ argument_slow_breeding_shark_species }} doesn't even have her first litter until she is {{ argument_shark_maturity_age }} years old, carries her pups inside her body for up to {{ argument_gestation_months }}, and gives birth to only {{ argument_litter_size_statistic }} pups every two or three years. You simply cannot harvest an animal like that as if it were a fast-growing anchovy.

The most exciting part of my work today isn't just counting how many sharks are left; it is working directly on the decks of commercial fishing boats in {{ argument_fishery_test_location }} to invent cheap, clever ways to keep sharks off the hooks in the first place.

Sharks have a sixth sense that humans don't have: their snouts are covered in tiny jelly-filled pores called the ampullae of Lorenzini that can detect the faint electrical heartbeat of a fish buried under the sand.

By clipping a small {{ argument_deterrent_device_type }} right above the fishhook -- or weaving flashing LED lights and electromagnetic pulses into fishing nets -- we create a harmless sensory buzz that feels to an approaching shark like standing next to a screeching megaphone, while tuna and swordfish don't notice it at all.

In our sea trials across {{ argument_trial_hooks_count }} hooks in {{ argument_fishery_test_location }}, these smart deterrents slashed accidental shark catches by {{ argument_bycatch_reduction_percentage }} without costing fishermen a single pound of their target catch.

At the same time, by attaching satellite tags to migrating {{ argument_pelagic_shark_species }} and overlaying their tracks with global vessel data from {{ argument_satellite_monitoring_platform }}, we discovered that open-ocean sharks gather at predictable underwater mountain peaks -- seamounts -- and thermal fronts. Protecting just those seasonal hotspots gives migrating sharks a safe highway across the high seas.

Every summer, I take students and local lawmakers from {{ argument_home_country_or_city }} out on our research boat to snorkel in clear, shallow water with {{ argument_gentle_shark_species }}. Almost every single person climbs down the boat ladder with their heart pounding in their throat.

And every single time, when they climb back onto the deck thirty minutes later, they pull off their mask with a huge grin on their face and say the exact same thing: "They weren't scary at all -- they were gorgeous."

We protect what we love, and we cannot love what we have been taught to fear.

Sharks have kept our oceans balanced, resilient, and alive for four hundred million years. If we trade our movie-screen nightmares for science, smart fishing gear, and safe ocean sanctuaries, these ancient guardians will keep patrolling the blue planet for millions of years to come. Thank you.
