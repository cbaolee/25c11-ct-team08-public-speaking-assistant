Welcome to the heartbeat of the Arctic tundra: a wild three-to-four-year rollercoaster of boom and bust driven entirely by a rodent that weighs less than a lemon.

Before we go any further, we have to clear up the biggest lie in wildlife history.

Almost everyone has heard the phrase that "lemmings commit mass suicide by jumping off cliffs into the sea." Where did that myth come from? From a staged scene in a {{ argument_movie_year }} nature documentary where filmmakers literally bought a few dozen lemmings, put them on a spinning snow turntable in {{ argument_filming_province_name }}, and herding them off a rock ledge into a river!

Real wild {{ argument_target_lemming_species }} do not jump off cliffs on purpose. What they do have is the most explosive reproductive superpower in the mammal world!

Unlike almost any other rodent that sleeps or slows down during the freezing dark winter, {{ argument_target_lemming_species }} do their best breeding right in the middle of winter underneath three feet of snow.

Down in the cozy subnivean zone -- the tiny pocket of air between the dirt and the bottom of the snowpack where the snow acts like an igloo blanket keeping the temperature right around freezing even when it is minus forty outside -- female {{ argument_target_lemming_species }} start having babies when they are only {{ argument_maturity_age_days }} days old, giving birth to a new litter of {{ argument_litter_size_statistic }} pups every three weeks all winter long!

By the time the snow melts in June, a few dozen lemmings have exploded into {{ argument_peak_density_per_hectare }} animals per hectare -- triggering a feast for every predator on the tundra: {{ argument_tundra_predator_guild_list }}.

For millennia, that four-year boom-and-bust heartbeat kept the entire Arctic ecosystem ticking. Even in a crash year, the predators knew that in three years, the snow blanket would build up another huge lemming boom.

Over the past {{ argument_decline_timespan_years }} years across {{ argument_affected_tundra_regions }}, however, field biologists have watched something eerie happen: the booms have stopped booming. The four-year heartbeat has flatlined into a permanent bust.

Why are the lemmings disappearing even though nobody hunts them and the tundra hasn't been paved over?

Because of changing autumn snow.

For {{ argument_target_lemming_species }} to build their winter tunnels and reach the mosses they eat, the first October snow needs to fall cold, dry, and fluffy. Today, warmer autumns in {{ argument_affected_tundra_regions }} bring freezing rain and wet slush that freezes against the ground like a layer of solid concrete.

Trapped above or below a sheet of solid ice, {{ argument_target_lemming_species }} cannot dig tunnels to reach their food, and the icy crust conducts bitter winter cold straight down to the ground -- wiping out the winter baby boom before spring even begins!

When the lemming boom fails, the dominoes fall across the entire hemisphere.

First, nomadic specialists like the {{ argument_target_arctic_owl_species }} and {{ argument_jaeger_or_skua_species }} simply cannot lay eggs without lemmings; across {{ argument_study_breeding_range }}, {{ argument_target_arctic_owl_species }} populations have dropped by {{ argument_owl_decline_percentage }} in just three generations.

Second, look at what happens to the millions of migratory shorebirds and geese -- {{ argument_shorebird_and_goose_species_list }} -- that fly all the way from {{ argument_wintering_continents_list }} to nest on the tundra.

In a good lemming year, {{ argument_arctic_fox_or_weasel_predator }} are so stuffed full of lemmings that they leave bird nests alone. In a year when the lemming cycle fails, hungry {{ argument_arctic_fox_or_weasel_predator }} switch 100% of their hunting over to bird eggs and chicks -- wiping out up to {{ argument_nest_predation_percentage }} of shorebird nests in a single summer and causing bird populations to drop on beaches ten thousand kilometers away!
