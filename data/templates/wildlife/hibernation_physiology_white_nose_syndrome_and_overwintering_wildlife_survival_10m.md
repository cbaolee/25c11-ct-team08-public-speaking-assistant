Imagine walking deep into an abandoned limestone mine in {{ argument_temperate_region_name }} in the dead of February. Your breath freezes in the beam of your headlamp. Clinging to the rock ceiling above you is a {{ argument_hibernating_mammal_species }}.

If you gently place a thermal camera or a stethoscope against its chest, you might think the animal is dead. Its body temperature has dropped from a warm {{ argument_summer_body_temperature }} down to {{ argument_torpor_body_temperature }} -- just one degree above the freezing air of the cave. Its heart, which beats {{ argument_active_heart_rate }} times a minute in July, is now beating only {{ argument_torpor_heart_rate }} times a minute. It takes a single breath once every {{ argument_apnea_duration_minutes }}.

This animal is not dead; it is practicing deep torpor, one of the most extreme physiological feats in the terrestrial animal kingdom.

For insect-eating and plant-eating mammals in {{ argument_temperate_and_arctic_zones }} that cannot migrate thousands of kilometers south when snow covers the land, winter is an existential energy crisis. There is no food for {{ argument_winter_fasting_months }}, yet staying warm requires burning massive amounts of calories.

Hibernation solves this equation by turning the body's thermostat down by {{ argument_metabolic_reduction_percentage }}. Before entering the cave or burrow in autumn, a {{ argument_hibernating_mammal_species }} or {{ argument_hibernating_ground_squirrel_species }} packs on {{ argument_fat_gain_percentage }} of its body weight in specialized brown adipose tissue.

Every gram of that fat is budgeted down to the milligram. Periodically during the winter -- roughly once every {{ argument_normal_arousal_interval_days }} -- the animal must warm its body back up to {{ argument_summer_body_temperature }} for a few hours to clear metabolic waste, rehydrate, and Reactivate its immune system. Those brief arousals account for {{ argument_arousal_energy_cost_percentage }} of its entire winter fat budget.

If anything forces a hibernating animal to wake up just three or four extra times during the winter, it runs out of fat in March and starves to death before spring insects emerge.

In the winter of {{ argument_disease_discovery_year }}, cavers in {{ argument_index_cave_location }} photographed something terrifying: thousands of dead {{ argument_hibernating_mammal_species }} littering the snow outside a cave entrance in mid-winter, with a fuzzy white growth around their muzzles and wings.

The culprit was {{ argument_cold_loving_fungus_name }}, an invasive cold-loving fungus accidentally introduced on dirty caving gear from {{ argument_pathogen_origin_region }}. While mammals in {{ argument_pathogen_origin_region }} had co-evolved with this fungus for millennia, the species in {{ argument_affected_continent_name }} had zero immunity.

The fungus only grows between {{ argument_fungus_temp_min }} and {{ argument_fungus_temp_max }} -- the exact temperature of a hibernating {{ argument_hibernating_mammal_species }}. While the animal's immune system is switched off in torpor, the fungus eats into the delicate skin of its wings, causing severe dehydration and irritation.

The infection tricks the {{ argument_hibernating_mammal_species }} into waking up every {{ argument_infected_arousal_interval_days }} instead of every {{ argument_normal_arousal_interval_days }} -- burning through its entire winter fuel tank two months before spring arrives.

Within {{ argument_epidemic_spread_years }}, White-Nose Syndrome swept across {{ argument_affected_states_and_provinces_count }} states and provinces, killing over {{ argument_mortality_count_statistic }} animals and wiping out {{ argument_colony_collapse_percentage }} of populations in affected caves.

How do you treat millions of wild animals sleeping inside dark underground labyrinths? You cannot catch every individual to give it an injection, and spraying broad-spectrum fungicides inside caves would destroy unique subterranean cave ecosystems.

Over the past {{ argument_research_intervention_years }}, our team has attacked this crisis from two angles. First, we discovered that the few {{ argument_hibernating_mammal_species }} surviving in affected mines were clustering in the coldest microclimates near the entrance -- around {{ argument_optimal_cold_refuge_temp }} -- where the fungus grows much more slowly and the animals burn even less fat.

By modifying airflow in abandoned mines and subterranean culverts across {{ argument_conservation_region_name }}, we engineered thermal refugia that lowered winter cave temperatures by {{ argument_temperature_modification_degrees }}, boosting overwinter survival from {{ argument_baseline_survival_rate }} to over {{ argument_improved_survival_rate }}.

Second, we isolated a naturally occurring bacterium from the skin of resistant animals -- {{ argument_probiotic_bacteria_strain }} -- and developed a non-toxic aerosol mist applied to cave walls in autumn that slows fungal growth just enough for the animals to make it to April alive.

Today, the surviving populations are passing down genes for more efficient fat storage and smarter torpor patterns to their offspring.

Hibernation teaches us that wildlife is often most vulnerable when it is completely out of sight. Whether it is a {{ argument_hibernating_mammal_species }} in a cave, a {{ argument_hibernating_bear_or_marmot }} in an alpine den, or a {{ argument_overwintering_reptile_or_insect }} buried beneath frost-covered leaf litter, disturbing an overwintering animal in January can be a death sentence.

By enforcing winter cave closures, disinfecting field gear, and protecting subterranean hibernacula from development, we guard the quiet winter sleep that brings the spring forest back to life. Thank you.
