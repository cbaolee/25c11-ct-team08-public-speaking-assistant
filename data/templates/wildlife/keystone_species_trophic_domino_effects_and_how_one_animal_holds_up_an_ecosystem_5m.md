In the summer of {{ argument_experiment_year_1963 }}, a young marine biologist named {{ argument_ecologist_name }} walked out onto the rocky tide pools of {{ argument_coastal_site_name }} carrying a simple iron crowbar.

Every week for a year, on one single strip of rock, he pried off every purple {{ argument_target_starfish_species }} he could find and tossed them back into the deep water. On the rock right next door, he left the starfish alone.

His colleagues thought it was a silly little experiment. After all, there were fifteen different species of barnacles, mussels, limpets, sponges, and red seaweeds living happily together on that rock. Removing just one predator shouldn't change much, right?

Within twelve months of removing the {{ argument_target_starfish_species }}, the fifteen species on that rock had crashed down to one.

Without the starfish eating them, {{ argument_dominant_mussel_species }} multiplied so fast they crowded every other creature off the stone -- turning a vibrant 15-species neighborhood into a single-species monoculture!

Out of that wet rock experiment, {{ argument_ecologist_name }} gave science one of the most important words in conservation history: the Keystone Species.

Why did he call it a "keystone"?

Picture an old Roman stone archway built without cement. You can chip away at the stones on the sides and the arch stays standing.

Pull out that single wedge-shaped stone at the very top center of the arch -- the keystone -- and even though it makes up less than five percent of the stone in the arch, the entire bridge collapses into rubble in two seconds.

Across planet Earth, certain wild animals play that exact role in their ecosystems -- punching ten or a hundred times above their weight!

On the American prairies, {{ argument_prairie_keystone_mammal }} dig underground towns that aerate the hard dirt for rain and create homes for over {{ argument_dependent_prairie_species_count }} other species -- from burrowing owls to black-footed ferrets!
In the African savanna, {{ argument_savanna_keystone_megafauna }} push down thorny bush thickets to keep the grasslands open for zebras and gazelles and dig waterholes in dry riverbeds during droughts that save every bird, bee, and monkey in the valley from dying of thirst.
In the northern rivers, migrating {{ argument_keystone_fish_species }} swim upstream from the ocean and bring {{ argument_marine_nitrogen_tonnage }} tons of ocean fertilizer deep into the mountain forests, feeding bears, eagles, and ancient cedar trees!

Why does understanding keystone species change how we protect nature?

Because when conservation budgets are tight, you can't manage ten thousand species of insects, plants, and birds one by one.

When you protect and restore the keystone species of a landscape -- as we did in {{ argument_restoration_valley_name }} by bringing back {{ argument_restored_keystone_species }} in {{ argument_restoration_year }} -- the keystone animal goes right back to work doing the heavy lifting for free, and {{ argument_rebounding_species_count }} other threatened plants and animals bounce back automatically!

In nature, some species aren't just passengers on the bus; they are driving the bus.

Protect the keystone at the top of the arch, and the whole living bridge stays standing for everyone. Thank you.
