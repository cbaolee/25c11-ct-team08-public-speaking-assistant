In the spring of {{ argument_peak_abundance_year }}, if you hiked up into the misty cloud forest of {{ argument_reserve_location_name }}, the muddy pools along the mountain ridge looked like someone had spilled a bucket of glowing neon orange paint across the forest floor.

Gathered around the puddles were over {{ argument_historical_toad_count }} {{ argument_extinct_toad_species }} -- tiny, shiny, fluorescent-orange toads that lived on only {{ argument_range_area_sq_km }} square kilometers of high mountain ridge and nowhere else in the universe.

Biologist {{ argument_biologist_witness_name }} photographed them, counted them, and hiked back down the mountain.

The following spring, in {{ argument_crash_year }}, researchers hiked back up to the exact same puddles during the April rains. Out of {{ argument_historical_toad_count }} neon orange toads, they found just one single lonely male.

In {{ argument_final_sighting_year }}, they found that same single male sitting by the puddle waiting for a mate that never came. And in {{ argument_extinction_confirmation_year }}, there were zero.

In just twenty-four months -- inside a strictly protected reserve where not a single tree had been cut down -- the {{ argument_extinct_toad_species }} vanished from the face of the Earth forever.

The disappearance of the {{ argument_extinct_toad_species }} was the first alarm bell of the largest, quietest extinction crisis of our lifetime.

Today, when you open the IUCN Red List, {{ argument_threatened_amphibian_percentage }} of all known amphibian species -- more than {{ argument_threatened_amphibian_count }} species of frogs, toads, salamanders, and caecilians -- are threatened with extinction! That makes amphibians the most endangered class of vertebrates on planet Earth.

Why are frogs and salamanders dying twice as fast as birds or mammals?

Because of their super-absorbent skin.

A frog doesn't drink water with its mouth like you and I do; it drinks and breathes right through its thin, moist skin. That makes an amphibian's body an open window to everything happening in its environment.

If the mountain clouds dry out by two degrees from climate change, their skin dries out. If pesticide drift blows in from a farm ten kilometers away, it soaks straight into their bloodstream. And when the microscopic waterborne chytrid fungus ({{ argument_chytrid_scientific_name }}) arrived on the boots of global trade, it swept through cloud-forest streams like wildfire -- wiping out {{ argument_extinct_amphibian_species_count }} species completely and pushing hundreds more down to their last few dozen survivors.

When chytrid hit the cloud forests of {{ argument_crisis_country_name }} in {{ argument_rescue_launch_year }}, field herpetologists realized that leaving the last wild {{ argument_rescued_frog_species_a }} and {{ argument_rescued_frog_species_b }} in their infected mountain streams was a death sentence.

We didn't have five years to apply for government building permits to construct a fancy brick research institute. The fungal wave was moving up the mountain valley at {{ argument_fungal_spread_speed_km_per_year }} kilometers a year.

So through the global {{ argument_conservation_coalition_name }}, our emergency teams bought insulated forty-foot refrigerated shipping containers, trucked them to the edge of the rainforest, and retrofitted them inside with rows of glass terrariums, UV sterilizers, automated misting nozzles that mimic tropical rainstorms, and solar-powered air conditioners!

Inside a single retrofitted shipping container -- costing less than a luxury SUV -- a team of two local biologists can keep an entire species of critically endangered frog safe from extinction!

Racing just weeks ahead of the fungal front in {{ argument_rescue_site_name }}, our field teams waded up mountain streams at midnight with headlamps and sterile plastic bags, catching the last {{ argument_founder_pair_count }} healthy pairs of {{ argument_rescued_frog_species_a }}, swabbing their skin, treating any trace of fungus with a simple ten-day bath of antifungal eye drops, and moving them into the Shipping Container Ark.

Keeping wild rainforest frogs alive and breeding inside a glass tank, however, turned out to be way harder than keeping goldfish!

Because amphibians have the most bizarre, specialized parenting habits on Earth.

Some species in our Ark only lay eggs inside rushing whitewater waterfalls; if the water in the tank is calm, they refuse to mate -- so our keepers had to build miniature indoor waterfalls with aquarium pumps and play recordings of thunderstorms and chorus calls over Bluetooth speakers at two in the morning!

Even wilder, look at the {{ argument_extinct_gastric_brooding_frog }} of Australia, which went extinct in the 1980s before the Ark existed: the mother frog literally swallowed her fertilized eggs, switched off her stomach acid so she didn't digest them, turned her own stomach into a womb for six weeks, and then burped live baby frogs right out of her mouth!

Or look at {{ argument_darwin_frog_species }} in our South American Ark in {{ argument_south_american_ark_country }}: after the female lays eggs on the damp moss, the father frog swallows the hatching tadpoles into his vocal sac, where they grow for {{ argument_vocal_sac_brooding_weeks }} weeks until he opens his mouth and hops out a litter of fully formed froglets!

Today, over {{ argument_ark_species_saved_count }} species of Red-Listed amphibians are living safely inside {{ argument_global_ark_facilities_count }} biosecure Ark facilities around the world -- many of which, like the {{ argument_extinct_in_wild_toad_species }}, are currently Extinct in the Wild and exist only inside those tanks.

And we aren't keeping them in glass boxes forever. Over the past {{ argument_reintroduction_research_years }} years, we discovered something thrilling:

First, in sunny stream clearings where overhanging branches allow warm patches of sunlight to hit the rocks, wild frogs can give themselves a "natural behavioral fever" -- sitting on a warm rock at {{ argument_sun_basking_temp_celsius }} degrees for two hours a day to literally cook the heat-sensitive chytrid fungus right off their skin!

Second, byinoculating captive-bred tadpoles with beneficial skin bacteria ({{ argument_protective_skin_bacteria_name }}) harvested from naturally immune survivor frogs before releasing them back into mountain streams with sunny basking clearings, our released {{ argument_rescued_frog_species_a }} are now surviving and breeding in the wild right alongside the fungus!

We arrived one year too late to save the neon orange {{ argument_extinct_toad_species }} of {{ argument_reserve_location_name }} -- and that loss broke our hearts into action.

Because of the Shipping Container Arks we built in its memory, hundreds of other jewel-colored frogs will still be singing in the rain when our grandchildren walk into the woods. Thank you.
