# The Amnesia That Makes Us Accept a Broken World

The term **Shifting Baseline Syndrome** was coined in **{{ pauly_paper_year }}** by fisheries scientist **{{ daniel_pauly_name }}**.

He noticed something strange about how successive generations of marine biologists and fishermen measured whether an ocean fishery was "healthy."

* Generation One -- in the **1920s** -- saw an ocean teeming with **100%** of its historic giant cod, sharks, and whales. By the time Generation One retired, overfishing had knocked the ocean down to **60%**.
* Generation Two grew up in the **1960s** seeing an ocean at **60%** -- and assumed *that* was the **100% natural baseline**! When the ocean dropped to **30%** on their watch, they thought it had only fallen by half.
* Generation Three starts work **today** looking at an ecosystem sitting at **10%** of its original wildlife abundance. If they manage to bump it up from 10% to **12%**, they throw a party and declare a total conservation victory!

Because each new generation of human beings is born with **ecological amnesia** -- taking the degraded, impoverished world of their own childhood as "normal" -- we slowly ratify the destruction of the planet without ever feeling the full weight of what we have lost!

# The Question No Economist Can Answer: Do They Have a Right to Exist?

Over the course of this conference, we have talked about all the practical, selfish reasons why humanity *needs* wildlife to survive.

We have talked about how wild pollinators put food on our plates, how wetlands filter our drinking water, how intact forests lock away carbon to stop climate change, and how wild plants hold the cures for human diseases. Every single one of those utilitarian arguments is 100% true and scientifically urgent.

Yet tonight, in the final minutes of our time together, I want to ask a deeper question -- a question that doesn't fit onto a spreadsheet or a cost-benefit analysis:

**What if a wild species doesn't make us a single dollar?**

What if a rare deep-sea octopus, a tiny alpine flower on a peak in {{ mountain_example }}, or a nocturnal salamander in {{ forest_example }} never cures a human disease, never filters a city's water, and never gets visited by a single paying tourist?

**Does it still have a right to exist?**

Imagine if an alien civilization landed on Earth tomorrow, looked at the Louvre Museum in Paris or the ancient temples of Kyoto, and said, *"Well, if we grind these paintings and temples up into gravel, we can pave a parking lot worth fifty thousand dollars!"*

We would be horrified! We would say that a masterpiece of art and history has **intrinsic value** -- a value in and of itself, beyond the price of its raw materials.

Now look at a living **{{ flagship_animal_or_plant_example }}**.

That creature is not a painting made of canvas and oil in a few months; it is an **irreplaceable masterpiece of {{ evolutionary_lineage_million_years }} million years of unbroken evolution**. Its eyes see colors we cannot imagine; its ears hear symphonies we cannot detect; it loves its offspring, fears pain, and fights to stay alive with every breath in its body.

> Humanity did not weave the web of life; **we are just one younger brother among ten million older siblings**. Having the power to bulldoze the rest of the family does not give us the moral right to do so.

# Stealing Inheritance From People Who Cannot Vote Yet

And this brings us to the second moral pillar of why we must protect wildlife: **Intergenerational Justice** -- justice between the people who are alive today, and the billions of children who have not been born yet.

Why is it so easy for short-sighted politicians and corporations today to delay wildlife and climate action?

Because the people who will pay the highest price for our delay -- the children of **2050, 2080, and 2100** -- **are not in the room yet**. They cannot vote in next Tuesday's election, they cannot buy stocks, and they cannot hire lobbyists!

Think about how every great moral awakening in human history has worked.

Over centuries, humanity slowly expanded our **circle of moral and legal care** -- recognizing that rights belong not just to kings and property owners, but to every human being regardless of race, gender, or age.

Today, in the twenty-first century, we are witnessing the next great expansion of that circle: the **Rights of Nature movement**.

# How to Break Shifting Baseline Syndrome in Your Own Life

So how do we make sure that my seven-year-old niece -- and your children and grandchildren -- don't grow up accepting a concrete lawn with one crow as "normal"?

We do three things starting this week:

**First, we tell the stories of what used to be here -- and what *can* be here again.**

Don't let the baseline shift silently downward! Take a young person to the river or the hillside in your town and tell them: *"Fifty years ago, {{ local_historic_species_list }} lived right here -- and here is how we are bringing them back!"* When we remember our ecological history, **restoration becomes an adventure instead of a chore**.

**Second, we shift from passive consumers to active citizens.**

Every single thing we buy at the grocery store, every pension fund or bank we put our savings in, and every local town council vote on zoning, pesticides, and green spaces is a vote for **what kind of planet we are leaving behind**. Support indigenous-led conservation, demand deforestation-free supply chains, and turn your own balcony, schoolyard, or neighborhood park into a living refuge for native plants and wildlife.

**Third, never underestimate the infectious power of stubborn hope.**

Cynicism and despair are the best friends of the status quo; if you convince people that the living world is already doomed, nobody lifts a finger to save it, and the destroyers win without a fight.

As the great conservationist **{{ quoted_conservation_hero }}** reminded us: **hope is not passive wishful thinking** -- where you sit on the couch and hope someone else fixes the problem.

**Real hope is an action verb.** You roll up your sleeves, you take one step forward to protect a wetland, plant a native oak, or pull a snare out of the woods, and when you see the birds and the frogs come back to that one patch of earth, **action creates hope**!
