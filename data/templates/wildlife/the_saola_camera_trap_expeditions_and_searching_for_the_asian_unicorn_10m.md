By the late twentieth century, biologists were pretty sure we had already discovered every large mammal on planet Earth. Sure, you might find a new beetle in the canopy or a tiny frog in a bog -- but you don't just stumble across a brand-new, {{ argument_animal_weight_kg }}-kilogram horned forest giant in {{ argument_discovery_year }}!

Then a joint team of Vietnamese forestry officials and wildlife surveyors hiked into the steep, mist-shrouded rainforests of the {{ argument_mountain_range_name }} along the border of Vietnam and Laos.

Inside a hunter's house in a remote mountain village, they saw a pair of long, straight,polished black horns reaching {{ argument_horn_length_cm }} centimeters long, attached to a skull unlike anything in any museum on Earth.

It wasn't a goat, it wasn't an antelope, and DNA showed it had split off from wild cattle {{ argument_evolutionary_split_million_years }} million years ago.

It was the {{ argument_target_species_name }} -- dubbed the "Asian Unicorn" -- the most spectacular large mammal discovery of the modern era. And the day the world learned it existed, it was already Critically Endangered on the Red List.

To give you an idea of how elusive the {{ argument_target_species_name }} is: twelve human beings have walked on the surface of the Moon.

How many wildlife biologists have ever seen a live {{ argument_target_species_name }} in the wild with their own two eyes?

Zero. Not a single scientist has ever spotted one while walking in the forest!

Everything we know about how it lives comes from Indigenous {{ argument_local_indigenous_groups }} villagers who have shared the {{ argument_mountain_range_name }} with it for centuries, a handful of camera-trap photos, and one captive female named {{ argument_captive_individual_name }} who was briefly held in a town in {{ argument_town_observation_year }} for {{ argument_captivity_days_count }} days.

From those eighteen days with {{ argument_captive_individual_name }}, researchers saw an animal that seemed to step straight out of a fairy tale: completely calm around humans, with a chocolate-brown coat, striking white stripes around its eyes, and huge maxillary scent glands on its snout covered in little flaps that it opens like wings to mark mossy rocks with musky paste.

If the {{ argument_target_species_name }} lives in steep, wet evergreen mountain ravines where almost no roads go and where its horns aren't even prized by international ivory or rhino-horn cartels, why are there estimated to be fewer than {{ argument_max_estimated_population }} individuals -- and possibly only a few dozen -- left alive today?

Because of the most indiscriminate, silent killer in the forests of Southeast Asia: the wire snare.

Driven by urban demand for illegal "wild bushmeat" in city restaurants across the region, commercial poaching gangs hike into the {{ argument_mountain_range_name }} and set thousands of cheap bicycle-brake cable snares tied to bent saplings along every animal trail and stream crossing.

A wire snare costs less than {{ argument_snare_cost_cents }} cents, takes two minutes to set, and doesn't care who steps into it.

The poacher might be hoping to catch a common wild pig or a civet for a restaurant table -- yet when one of the last {{ argument_target_species_name }} on Earth walks down that trail to drink at a mountain stream, that fifty-cent wire loop closes around its leg and wipes out millions of years of unique evolution in an afternoon.

Across the protected reserves of the {{ argument_mountain_range_name }}, community forest guards have pulled out over {{ argument_removed_snares_count }} wire snares in the last decade alone!

The last confirmed camera-trap photograph of a wild {{ argument_target_species_name }} was snapped on a rainy night in {{ argument_last_photo_year }} in {{ argument_last_photo_province }}. Since then, over ten years have passed without a single verified photo.

Does that mean the Asian Unicorn is gone? Ask the elders in the mountain villages of {{ argument_search_region_name }} and the field trackers of the {{ argument_conservation_working_group_name }}, and they will tell you: the {{ argument_mountain_range_name }} has thousands of square kilometers of vertical karst cliffs and roadless valleys where no camera has ever been placed.

Today, we are launching the most intensive scientific search mission in conservation history to find the last survivors before it is too late.

Instead of just hoping a {{ argument_target_species_name }} walks right in front of a camera lens, our teams are using two cutting-edge detective tools:

First, leech blood eDNA: terrestrial forest leeches drop off animals after taking a blood meal, preserving the mammal's DNA inside their stomachs for months! By collecting thousands of leeches and stream-water samples across {{ argument_sampled_watershed_area }} and sequencing the DNA inside them, we can detect which valley holds a living {{ argument_target_species_name }}.

Second, we have trained conservation detection dogs to recognize the scent of {{ argument_target_species_name }} dung preserved in museum freezers so they can sniff out a fresh trail from {{ argument_scent_detection_distance }} meters away.

And when we find the last survivors, we are ready. Inside {{ argument_national_park_breeding_site }}, right in the heart of its native mountain climate, our coalition has already built the world's first {{ argument_target_species_name }} Conservation Breeding Center staffed by the top ungulate veterinarians on Earth, while local Forest Guard teams funded by {{ argument_community_guard_program }} patrol the ridges year-round to keep the forests snare-free.

We didn't discover the Asian Unicorn at the end of the twentieth century just to watch it go extinct at the start of the twenty-first.

By clearing the wire snares from the mountains and finding the last hidden survivors of the {{ argument_mountain_range_name }}, we can make sure this gentle legend of the mist steps out of the shadows and back from the brink. Thank you.
