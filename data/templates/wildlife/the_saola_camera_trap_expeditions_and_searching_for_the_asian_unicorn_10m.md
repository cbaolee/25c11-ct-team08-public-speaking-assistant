Sure, you might find a new beetle in the canopy or a tiny frog in a bog -- but you don't just stumble across a brand-new, **{{ animal_weight_kg }}-kilogram horned forest giant** in **{{ discovery_year }}**!

Then a joint team of Vietnamese forestry officials and wildlife surveyors hiked into the steep, mist-shrouded rainforests of the **{{ mountain_range_name }}** along the border of Vietnam and Laos.

Inside a hunter's house in a remote mountain village, they saw a pair of long, straight,polished black horns reaching **{{ horn_length_cm }} centimeters** long, attached to a skull unlike anything in any museum on Earth.

It wasn't a goat, it wasn't an antelope, and DNA showed it had split off from wild cattle **{{ evolutionary_split_million_years }} million years ago**.

> It was the **{{ target_species_name }}** -- dubbed the **"Asian Unicorn"** -- the most spectacular large mammal discovery of the modern era. And the day the world learned it existed, it was already **Critically Endangered** on the Red List.

# More Astronauts on the Moon Than People Who Have Seen One

To give you an idea of how elusive the {{ target_species_name }} is: twelve human beings have walked on the surface of the Moon.

How many wildlife biologists have **ever seen a live {{ target_species_name }} in the wild** with their own two eyes?

**Zero.** Not a single scientist has ever spotted one while walking in the forest!

Everything we know about how it lives comes from Indigenous **{{ local_indigenous_groups }}** villagers who have shared the {{ mountain_range_name }} with it for centuries, a handful of camera-trap photos, and one captive female named **{{ captive_individual_name }}** who was briefly held in a town in {{ town_observation_year }} for {{ captivity_days_count }} days.

From those eighteen days with {{ captive_individual_name }}, researchers saw an animal that seemed to step straight out of a fairy tale: completely calm around humans, with a chocolate-brown coat, striking white stripes around its eyes, and huge **maxillary scent glands** on its snout covered in little flaps that it opens like wings to mark mossy rocks with musky paste.

# Death by a Million Wire Loops

If the {{ target_species_name }} lives in steep, wet evergreen mountain ravines where almost no roads go and where its horns aren't even prized by international ivory or rhino-horn cartels, why are there estimated to be **fewer than {{ max_estimated_population }} individuals** -- and possibly only a few dozen -- left alive today?

Because of the most indiscriminate, silent killer in the forests of Southeast Asia: the **wire snare**.

Driven by urban demand for illegal **"wild bushmeat"** in city restaurants across the region, commercial poaching gangs hike into the {{ mountain_range_name }} and set thousands of cheap **bicycle-brake cable snares** tied to bent saplings along every animal trail and stream crossing.

A wire snare costs **less than {{ snare_cost_cents }} cents**, takes two minutes to set, and doesn't care who steps into it.

> The poacher might be hoping to catch a common wild pig or a civet for a restaurant table -- yet when one of the last {{ target_species_name }} on Earth walks down that trail to drink at a mountain stream, that fifty-cent wire loop closes around its leg and wipes out millions of years of unique evolution in an afternoon.

Across the protected reserves of the {{ mountain_range_name }}, community forest guards have pulled out **over {{ removed_snares_count }} wire snares** in the last decade alone!

The last confirmed camera-trap photograph of a wild {{ target_species_name }} was snapped on a rainy night in **{{ last_photo_year }}** in {{ last_photo_province }}. Since then, over ten years have passed without a single verified photo.

Today, we are launching the most intensive **scientific search mission** in conservation history to find the last survivors before it is too late.
