# Introduction: A Four-Kilogram Emerald Owl-Parrot That Forgot How to Fly

Imagine if you combined a bright green parrot, a fluffy nocturnal owl, a teddy bear, and a brass tuba -- and made it smell like **old-fashioned honey and wildflowers**.

You would get the **{{ target_parrot_species }}** of {{ country_name }}.

Weighing up to **{{ weight_kg_statistic }} kilograms**, the {{ target_parrot_species }} is the heaviest, longest-living parrot on planet Earth -- capable of living for **over {{ lifespan_years_statistic }} years**!

Because it evolved for millions of years on islands where there were zero land mammals to chase it, the {{ target_parrot_species }} completely gave up the ability to fly. Instead, it uses its strong scaly legs to hike all the way up into the tops of {{ native_conifer_tree_species }} trees at night, chews on leaves and berries, and when it wants to get back down, spreads its short little wings like a faulty parachute and **plummets to the forest floor like a feathered bowling ball**.

> It is easily the most endearing, ridiculous bird on the planet -- and by **{{ population_low_year }}**, invasive predators had wiped out **every single {{ target_parrot_species }} on Earth except for {{ lowest_population_count }} birds**.

# Why Making Babies Once Every Four Years Is Tough

When conservationists rescued those last **{{ lowest_population_count }}** surviving {{ target_parrot_species }} and moved them onto predator-free offshore islands like **{{ sanctuary_island_name }}**, they thought, *"Great! Now that the stoats, cats, and rats are gone, the parrots will start having babies like crazy!"*

They didn't. Because the {{ target_parrot_species }} has the **weirdest, slowest love life** in the entire bird kingdom.

First, look at how a male tries to attract a girlfriend. During mating season, males hike up to the very top of a mountain ridge, dig a shallow bowl in the dirt next to a rock that acts as a sound reflector, inflate their chest like a bagpipe until they look like a green beach ball, and let out a deep, sub-audible **"BOOM... BOOM... BOOM"** for eight hours a night!

That low-frequency boom travels **{{ boom_distance_km }} kilometers** through the night forest. There is just one problem: the booming sound is so low-pitched that when a female finally hikes up the mountain, **she can barely tell which bush the sound is coming from** -- and if a male gets startled by another bird, he freezes like a statue while the female walks right past him!

Second, and even more frustrating for biologists, {{ target_parrot_species }} refuse to breed unless the native **{{ mast_fruiting_tree_species }}** trees have a massive **"mast fruiting" year** -- which only happens once every **{{ fruiting_interval_years }} to four years**!

Even worse, our early rangers noticed that when they gave hungry mother birds extra supplemental food to help them lay more eggs, the well-fed mothers produced **{{ male_chick_bias_percentage }} male chicks** -- which was the last thing a species with only twenty females needed!

# Every Single Bird Wears a Smart Backpack

How did New Zealand's **{{ recovery_program_name }}** team turn {{ lowest_population_count }} aging birds into a thriving population without losing a single individual?

By giving **every single living {{ target_parrot_species }} on Earth** its own personal name, health chart, and **smart activity tracker backpack**!

Today, out on {{ sanctuary_island_name }} and {{ second_sanctuary_island }}, every bird wears a tiny transmitter harnessed to its back that acts like a wildlife Fitbit. It records the bird's daily steps, foraging hours, and health -- and during breeding season, it actually **detects the exact movements of mating** and beams a text message to the rangers' hut telling them: *"{{ male_bird_name }} just mated with {{ female_bird_name }} for {{ mating_duration_minutes }} minutes on Ridge Three!"*

Once a mother lays her eggs inside a hollow tree trunk on the ground, the nest is wired up like a smart home:

* **Infrared cameras** watch the eggs twenty-four hours a day.
* When the mother leaves the nest at midnight to go eat berries, a **proximity sensor** reads her backpack ID and automatically turns on a tiny **thermostat-controlled heat blanket** over the eggs so they don't get cold while she is out!
* Rangers even replace the real fragile eggs with **3D-printed smart dummy eggs** while the real chicks hatch safely inside a predator-proof incubator room down the trail, slipping the newly hatched babies right back under mom just as she expects them to hatch!

# Crowd-Sourced Genomes and Flying Drone Couriers

And to beat inbreeding among descendants of just {{ lowest_population_count }} founders, the {{ recovery_program_name }} became the **first project in history to sequence the entire genome of every single living individual in a species** -- funded by everyday citizens around the world!

With that genetic map on a tablet, during the brief {{ breeding_window_days }}-day breeding window on {{ sanctuary_island_name }}, vets collect sperm from males with rare genes on one side of the island and fly the samples across the rugged mountains in **{{ drone_flight_minutes }} minutes using custom UAV drones** to artificially inseminate females on the other side of the island before the sample cools down!

And by carefully adjusting the mothers' diet right before egg-laying, scientists cracked the sex-ratio puzzle -- bringing healthy **baby girl chicks** back to 50/50!

# Returning to the Mainland After Forty Years

Thanks to that blend of high-tech gadgets, Indigenous **{{ maori_iwi_partner }}** guardianship, and volunteers who spend their winters camping in the rain to guard nests, the {{ target_parrot_species }} population has **quadrupled** from {{ lowest_population_count }} to **over {{ current_kakapo_population }} birds** -- so many that the offshore islands are actually getting full!

In **{{ mainland_return_year }}**, for the first time in forty years, a group of {{ translocated_count }} {{ target_parrot_species }} was released back onto the New Zealand mainland inside the giant predator-fenced sanctuary of **{{ mainland_sanctuary_name }}**.

> The {{ target_parrot_species }} proves that when we refuse to give up on a species -- even when there are only fifty-one left and they only breed once every four years -- human ingenuity can rewrite fate. Thank you.
