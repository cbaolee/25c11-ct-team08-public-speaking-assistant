Imagine if you combined a bright green parrot, a fluffy nocturnal owl, a teddy bear, and a brass tuba -- and made it smell like old-fashioned honey and wildflowers.

You would get the {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} of {{ argument_country_name }}.

Weighing up to {{ argument_weight_kg_statistic }} kilograms, the {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} is the heaviest, longest-living parrot on planet Earth -- capable of living for over {{ argument_lifespan_years_statistic }} years!

Because it evolved for millions of years on islands where there were zero land mammals to chase it, the {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} completely gave up the ability to fly. Instead, it uses its strong scaly legs to hike all the way up into the tops of {{ argument_native_conifer_tree_species }} trees at night, chews on leaves and berries, and when it wants to get back down, spreads its short little wings like a faulty parachute and plummets to the forest floor like a feathered bowling ball.

It is easily the most endearing, ridiculous bird on the planet -- and by {{ argument_population_low_year }}, invasive predators had wiped out every single {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} on Earth except for {{ argument_lowest_population_count }} birds.

When conservationists rescued those last {{ argument_lowest_population_count }} surviving {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} and moved them onto predator-free offshore islands like {{ argument_sanctuary_island_name }}, they thought, "Great! Now that the stoats, cats, and rats are gone, the parrots will start having babies like crazy!"

They didn't. Because the {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} has the weirdest, slowest love life in the entire bird kingdom.

First, look at how a male tries to attract a girlfriend. During mating season, males hike up to the very top of a mountain ridge, dig a shallow bowl in the dirt next to a rock that acts as a sound reflector, inflate their chest like a bagpipe until they look like a green beach ball, and let out a deep, sub-audible "BOOM... BOOM... BOOM" for eight hours a night!

That low-frequency boom travels {{ argument_boom_distance_km }} kilometers through the night forest. There is just one problem: the booming sound is so low-pitched that when a female finally hikes up the mountain, she can barely tell which bush the sound is coming from -- and if a male gets startled by another bird, he freezes like a statue while the female walks right past him!

Second, and even more frustrating for biologists, {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} refuse to breed unless the native {{ argument_mast_fruiting_tree_species }} trees have a massive "mast fruiting" year -- which only happens once every {{ argument_fruiting_interval_years }} to four years!

Even worse, our early rangers noticed that when they gave hungry mother birds extra supplemental food to help them lay more eggs, the well-fed mothers produced {{ argument_male_chick_bias_percentage }} male chicks -- which was the last thing a species with only twenty females needed!

How did New Zealand's {{ argument_recovery_program_name }} team turn {{ argument_lowest_population_count }} aging birds into a thriving population without losing a single individual?

By giving every single living {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} on Earth its own personal name, health chart, and smart activity tracker backpack!

Today, out on {{ argument_sanctuary_island_name }} and {{ argument_second_sanctuary_island }}, every bird wears a tiny transmitter harnessed to its back that acts like a wildlife Fitbit. It records the bird's daily steps, foraging hours, and health -- and during breeding season, it actually detects the exact movements of mating and beams a text message to the rangers' hut telling them: "{{ argument_male_bird_name }} just mated with {{ argument_female_bird_name }} for {{ argument_mating_duration_minutes }} minutes on Ridge Three!"

Once a mother lays her eggs inside a hollow tree trunk on the ground, the nest is wired up like a smart home:

Infrared cameras watch the eggs twenty-four hours a day.
When the mother leaves the nest at midnight to go eat berries, a proximity sensor reads her backpack ID and automatically turns on a tiny thermostat-controlled heat blanket over the eggs so they don't get cold while she is out!
Rangers even replace the real fragile eggs with 3D-printed smart dummy eggs while the real chicks hatch safely inside a predator-proof incubator room down the trail, slipping the newly hatched babies right back under mom just as she expects them to hatch!

And to beat inbreeding among descendants of just {{ argument_lowest_population_count }} founders, the {{ argument_recovery_program_name }} became the first project in history to sequence the entire genome of every single living individual in a species -- funded by everyday citizens around the world!

With that genetic map on a tablet, during the brief {{ argument_breeding_window_days }}-day breeding window on {{ argument_sanctuary_island_name }}, vets collect sperm from males with rare genes on one side of the island and fly the samples across the rugged mountains in {{ argument_drone_flight_minutes }} minutes using custom UAV drones to artificially inseminate females on the other side of the island before the sample cools down!

And by carefully adjusting the mothers' diet right before egg-laying, scientists cracked the sex-ratio puzzle -- bringing healthy baby girl chicks back to 50/50!

Thanks to that blend of high-tech gadgets, Indigenous {{ argument_maori_iwi_partner }} guardianship, and volunteers who spend their winters camping in the rain to guard nests, the {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} population has quadrupled from {{ argument_lowest_population_count }} to over {{ argument_current_kakapo_population }} birds -- so many that the offshore islands are actually getting full!

In {{ argument_mainland_return_year }}, for the first time in forty years, a group of {{ argument_translocated_count }} {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} was released back onto the New Zealand mainland inside the giant predator-fenced sanctuary of {{ argument_mainland_sanctuary_name }}.

The {{ argument_target_parrot_species }} proves that when we refuse to give up on a species -- even when there are only fifty-one left and they only breed once every four years -- human ingenuity can rewrite fate. Thank you.
