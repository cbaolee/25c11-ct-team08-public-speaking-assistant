# Introduction: Looking Into the Eyes of a Two-Hundred-Kilogram Cousin

Hike for three hours through stinging nettles, bamboo thickets, and cold volcanic mud up the slopes of the **{{ volcano_range_name }}** at the border of {{ range_countries_list }}, and suddenly your tracker holds up a hand and whispers, *"Look down."*

Sitting three meters away in a clearing of wild celery is a **{{ silverback_weight_kg }}-kilogram silverback {{ target_gorilla_subspecies }}**.

His chest is as wide as a barrel, his canine teeth are the size of your thumb, and he has the strength of **ten adult men**.

Yet as you crouch down slowly and bow your head to show respect, he doesn't roar or charge. He leans back against a mossy tree trunk, picks a delicate purple flower off a vine to munch on, and looks straight into your eyes with warm, deep brown eyes that hold **98.3% of your own human DNA** -- while his two-month-old baby tumbles over his silver back like a furry acrobat!

> When primatologist {{ pioneer_primatologist_name }} counted the **{{ target_gorilla_subspecies }}** of the {{ volcano_range_name }} back in **{{ low_point_year }}**, there were **only {{ lowest_population_count }} individuals left alive** -- and she predicted that without urgent action, the subspecies would be **100% extinct before the year 2000**.

Today, the {{ target_gorilla_subspecies }} is not extinct. In fact, it is the **only great ape on planet Earth whose numbers are actually rising**!

# The Three Threats in the Mist

How did the {{ target_gorilla_subspecies }} go from a death sentence in {{ low_point_year }} to moving *down* a threat category on the IUCN Red List from **Critically Endangered** to **Endangered** in {{ downlisting_year }}?

Because the people living around the volcanoes decided to practice what we call **"Extreme Conservation."**

Think about what the {{ target_gorilla_subspecies }} was up against in the 1980s and 1990s:

1. **Habitat clearing and snares:** Surrounded by some of the highest rural human population densities in Africa -- up to **{{ human_density_per_sq_km }} people per square kilometer** right up against the park boundary -- farmers were clearing forest up the volcano slopes, while wire snares set for bushmeat antelope were maiming baby gorillas' hands and feet.
2. **Armed conflict:** Civil wars and armed militias swept directly through the forests of {{ park_names_list }}, turning the gorillas' only home into a battleground where **over {{ fallen_rangers_count }} courageous park rangers** lost their lives protecting the wildlife and their communities.
3. **Human germs:** Because {{ target_gorilla_subspecies }} are so genetically close to us, a common **human cold, flu, or measles virus** carried into the forest by a tourist, a ranger, or a villager can give a gorilla fatal pneumonia!

# The World's Only Wild Animal With Its Own Family Doctor

To beat those three threats, field rangers and wildlife veterinarians built a safety net unlike anything else in the wild animal kingdom.

Today, every single habituated {{ target_gorilla_subspecies }} in {{ volcano_range_name }} has a **name, a family tree, and a team of field doctors** -- the **{{ gorilla_vet_project_name }}** -- who hike into the mountains every month to give them visual check-ups!

Wait -- you don't medicate a wild gorilla for a natural scratch or an old-age ache; nature still takes its course.

When a baby gorilla gets a **man-made wire snare** wrapped around its wrist, however, or catches a human-linked respiratory infection that threatens the whole family group, the **{{ gorilla_vet_project_name }}** team springs into action.

Picture how brave you have to be to do a field intervention inside a wild gorilla family:

> While the {{ silverback_weight_kg }}-kilogram silverback dad stands five meters away screaming and beating his chest to protect his baby, rangers form a calm human shield -- speaking to the silverback in low, reassuring **"belch vocalizations"** that mean *"We are peaceful, we aren't hurting your child"* -- while the vets quickly anesthetize the mother and baby, cut the deadly wire snare off the infant's wrist, give it antibiotics, and wake them both up in the forest in **under {{ intervention_minutes }} minutes**!

Our long-term population models showed that **veterinary snare removals and health care alone accounted for up to {{ vet_contribution_percentage }} of the population's growth** over the last thirty years!

Even before the rest of the world learned about surgical masks and social distancing in 2020, anyone visiting the {{ target_gorilla_subspecies }} in {{ volcano_range_name }} had to wear a **surgical mask**, disinfect their boots, and stay **{{ safe_viewing_distance_meters }} meters back** so human respiratory viruses never jump to our closest cousins.

And what changed the minds of the human communities living right outside the park boundary?

In {{ revenue_sharing_country_list }}, governments enacted a historic **Tourism Revenue Sharing** policy: **{{ community_revenue_share_percentage }} of every single gorilla trekking permit fee** paid by international visitors goes directly into a community fund for the villages bordering the park -- building **{{ community_projects_built_list }}**, while former poachers are employed as respected **Gorilla Guardians** and porters who chase wandering gorillas safely out of farm fields and cut thousands of snares out of the bamboo!

Today, census teams have counted **over {{ current_population_count }} {{ target_gorilla_subspecies }}** living across their two mountain homes -- **more than quadruple** the number {{ pioneer_primatologist_name }} feared would be the last.
