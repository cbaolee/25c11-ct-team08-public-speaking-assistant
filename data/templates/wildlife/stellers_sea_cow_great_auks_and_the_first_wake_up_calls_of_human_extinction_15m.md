For almost all of human history, even the greatest scientists in the world believed that extinction was impossible.

If you walked into a university in Europe in the year {{ argument_pre_extinction_belief_year }} and showed a professor a fossilized mammoth bone or a giant ammonite shell, they wouldn't say, "Oh, that animal went extinct." They would say, "Nature is perfect and complete; no species ever disappears completely -- that giant animal is probably just still living somewhere in the unexplored interior of America or at the bottom of the sea!"

Then, between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, European ships sailed out to the remotest corners of the oceans -- and encountered two gentle, defenseless marine giants that shattered that comforting illusion forever: the {{ argument_extinct_marine_mammal_name }} of the North Pacific, and the {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} of the North Atlantic.

What happened to those two animals in the blink of an eye forced humanity to confront a terrifying new idea for the very first time: our own hands could erase a creation of nature from the universe forever.

Let's travel back to November of {{ argument_shipwreck_year }}, to a freezing, treeless island in the Bering Sea called {{ argument_island_name }}.

A Russian exploratory ship commanded by {{ argument_explorer_captain_name }} had just wrecked on the rocks in a winter storm. Stranded on the beach in freezing sleet, dying of scurvy and starvation, was the expedition's German naturalist, {{ argument_naturalist_name }}.

When {{ argument_naturalist_name }} looked out into the shallow kelp beds right off the beach, he saw herds of the largest, strangest marine mammals any European had ever laid eyes on: the {{ argument_extinct_marine_mammal_name }}.

Picture a dugong or a manatee -- and now blow it up to the size of a city bus!

A full-grown {{ argument_extinct_marine_mammal_name }} reached {{ argument_sea_cow_length_meters }} meters long and weighed up to {{ argument_sea_cow_weight_tons }} metric tons -- bigger than many whales! Because it lived in icy sub-Arctic water and ate nothing except tough giant kelp growing near the surface, it had a {{ argument_blubber_thickness_cm }}-centimeter-thick coat of blubber and bark-like skin so buoyant that the animal couldn't even dive underwater; its huge back floated above the waves like an overturned boat all day long!

Instead of teeth, it had two wide, ridged keratin plates in its mouth to mash kelp, and it used its stubby front flippers to "walk" along the shallow rocks and hold onto its mate.

Most heartbreaking of all was what {{ argument_naturalist_name }} wrote in his journal about their family loyalty:

They mated for life, kept their young calves safely in the middle of the herd, and when sailors rowed out and harpooned one animal, the rest of the herd didn't swim away. Instead, its mate and family swam right up to the boat -- trying to push the harpoon rope off their injured friend or press down on the rope to break it, staying by the body on the beach for days in grief!

When {{ argument_naturalist_name }} and the surviving sailors finally built a small boat out of their ship's wreckage and sailed back to {{ argument_mainland_port_name }} in {{ argument_return_year }}, they told fur traders pursuing {{ argument_hunted_otter_species }} pelts that {{ argument_island_name }} had "living mountains of delicious meat and lamp oil" floating right near the shore that didn't even swim away when you harpooned them.

Within a year, waves of fur-hunting expeditions descended on {{ argument_island_name }}.

Hunters didn't even bother bringing animals ashore; they simply speared passing {{ argument_extinct_marine_mammal_name }} from small boats and waited for five out of every six wounded animals to wash up dead on the rocks days later. And at the same time, by slaughtering thousands of {{ argument_hunted_otter_species }} for their fur, the hunters unleashed a plague of sea urchins that ate down the very kelp forests the {{ argument_extinct_marine_mammal_name }} needed to survive!

Do the math on the calendar:

{{ argument_shipwreck_year }}: {{ argument_naturalist_name }} becomes the first scientist in history to see and describe a living {{ argument_extinct_marine_mammal_name }}.
{{ argument_sea_cow_extinction_year }}: Just twenty-seven years later, a hunter named {{ argument_last_hunter_name }} kills the very last {{ argument_extinct_marine_mammal_name }} on Earth.

Twenty-seven years from "Hello, what a magnificent new creature" to total oblivion. Not a single living scientist ever saw one again.

Now spin the globe to the freezing waters of the North Atlantic -- from Newfoundland and Greenland across to Iceland and Scotland -- to meet the {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }}.

Today, when you hear the word "penguin," you think of birds in the Southern Hemisphere. Yet the word {{ argument_original_penguin_latin_name }} originally belonged to the {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} of the north! Standing {{ argument_auk_height_cm }} centimeters tall, dressed in a sharp black-and-white tuxedo with a massive grooved bill, it was a flightless seabird that "flew" underwater like a torpedo to catch fish and gathered by the millions on rocky offshore islands like {{ argument_main_colony_island_name }} every summer to lay a single, pear-shaped egg on bare stone.

For thousands of years, Indigenous coastal communities and early Norse fishermen harvested a sustainable number of {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} each spring without denting the millions-strong flocks.

Then, in the {{ argument_industrial_feather_century }}, European ships ran out of nearshore bait and discovered a massive commercial market for down feathers to stuff luxury pillows and mattresses!

Crews landed on {{ argument_main_colony_island_name }}, built stone corrals on the flat rock, and because the flightless {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} walked awkwardly on land and had zero fear of humans on their isolated breeding rocks, sailors simply herded thousands of birds up wooden planks straight into the holds of their ships -- or worse, boiled them alive in giant cauldrons right on the island using the oily bodies of other {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} as firewood to pluck their feathers!

By the early 1800s, the millions of {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} were gone, except for one last secret colony clinging to a sheer volcanic rock stack off Iceland called {{ argument_volcanic_stack_name }}, surrounded by treacherous whirlpools that no boat dared approach.

Then, in {{ argument_volcanic_eruption_year }}, an underwater volcanic eruption blew {{ argument_volcanic_stack_name }} into the sea!

The few surviving pairs swam to a nearby low-lying rocky islet called {{ argument_final_islet_name }}.

Now think about what you would hope humans would do when a majestic bird is down to its last fifty survivors on one tiny rock. You would hope people would leave them alone!

Instead, the exact opposite happened -- a deadly economic trap that conservationists today call the Anthropogenic Allee Effect: the rarer the {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} became, the more money museum directors and wealthy private collectors in Europe offered to pay for one last stuffed skin and egg!

On the morning of {{ argument_final_kill_date }}, three fishermen hired by a merchant collector rowed out to the rocks of {{ argument_final_islet_name }}.

High on a basalt ledge, they spotted a single male and female {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} incubating one single egg.

As two of the men cornered and strangled the last two adults for the collector's taxidermist, the third fisherman, {{ argument_third_fisherman_name }}, accidentally stepped on their single egg on the rock -- crushing the last {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} embryo under his boot.

Fourteen years after that egg was crushed on {{ argument_final_islet_name }}, prominent British ornithologist {{ argument_ornithologist_founder_name }} traveled to Iceland looking for the {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }}.

When he interviewed those exact fishermen and realized that he had arrived fourteen years too late -- that a bird that once numbered in the millions was gone forever because nobody had passed a law to stop people from killing them on their breeding rocks -- he was shaken to his core.

He sailed back to Britain and spent the next decade campaigning before Parliament until, in {{ argument_seabird_protection_act_year }}, lawmakers passed the Sea Birds Preservation Act -- one of the very first modern wildlife conservation laws in world history, banning the killing of seabirds during their summer breeding season!

And today, every time our marine biologists design a Marine Protected Area around a kelp forest in the North Pacific or guard a puffin and razorbill colony in the North Atlantic, we are applying the two lessons bought with the lives of the {{ argument_extinct_marine_mammal_name }} and the {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }}:

Trophic cascades are real: When hunters killed the sea otters around {{ argument_island_name }}, the urchins ate the kelp and starved the giant sea cows before the harpoons even finished the job. You cannot save a single species without saving its food web!
Rarity must trigger protection, never a bidding war: Under the international CITES treaty today, the moment a species drops into the Red List's danger zone, commercial international trade is banned so collectors can never again bid for the "last specimen on Earth."

We cannot hear the gentle sigh of a ten-ton {{ argument_extinct_marine_mammal_name }} floating in the Bering kelp anymore, or watch {{ argument_extinct_flightless_seabird_name }} dive off the rocks of Iceland.

Yet out of their tragedy, humanity finally opened its eyes, threw away the myth that nature is inexhaustible, and wrote the laws that keep millions of whales, manatees, and seabirds alive today. Let's honor their memory by making sure no other ocean giant ever follows them into the dark. Thank you.
