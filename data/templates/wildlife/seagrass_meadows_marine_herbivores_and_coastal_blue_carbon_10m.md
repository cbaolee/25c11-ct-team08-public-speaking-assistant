# Introduction: The Ocean's Forgotten Lawn

When you go on a beach vacation and wade out into waist-deep water, what do you do when your toes brush against a patch of slimy green grass on the bottom? Be honest: most of us squeal, jump back, and swim over to the bare white sand because we think there might be crabs hiding in the weeds.

For decades, coastal hotels in {{ tourist_coast_region }} actually paid crews to rip out underwater **seagrass meadows** so tourists could have "clean" swimming beaches.

As a marine ecologist, watching someone rip up seagrass makes my heart sink. Because that humble underwater lawn isn't seaweed; it is the **only true flowering plant** that returned to the ocean {{ evolutionary_return_age }} ago -- producing flowers, pollen, and seeds completely underwater -- and it is one of the most powerful life-support systems on our planet.

> Acre for acre, a healthy seagrass meadow locks away carbon **up to {{ carbon_capture_multiplier }} times faster** than a tropical rainforest on land.

# Scars in the Meadow

Despite doing all this heavy lifting for our climate and our dinner plates, we have lost **{{ global_seagrass_loss_percentage }} of the world's seagrass meadows** since {{ baseline_loss_year }} -- losing an area the size of two soccer fields every single hour.

In {{ study_lagoon_name }}, the damage comes from two directions. First, fertilizer runoff and untreated sewage from onshore farms and cities wash into the bay, feeding thick blooms of slimy plankton that block the sunlight seagrass needs to breathe.

Second, careless boaters take shortcuts across shallow flats at low tide. Their spinning propellers slice deep trenches right through the underground root mats -- leaving barren **propeller scars** that can take {{ scar_recovery_years }} to heal and leak ancient stored carbon right back into the water.

# Planting Seeds Beneath the Waves

Five years ago, people told us restoring seagrass at scale was impossible. Today, in {{ restoration_site_name }}, our community team of scientists, local fishers, and school kids is proving them wrong.

Every summer during flowering season, volunteers snorkel over healthy meadows to hand-pick millions of ripe seagrass seeds -- or even better, we suspend mesh bags filled with seed pods from floating buoys so the seeds drop naturally onto bare mudflats as they ripen.

Even more clever, in {{ shellfish_partnership_site }}, we plant **{{ native_oyster_or_mussel_species }} reefs** right alongside our baby seagrass plots. A single adult oyster filters up to {{ water_filtration_gallons_per_day }} of dirty bay water every day, clearing away the murky sediment so sunlight can reach the young seagrass blades on the bottom.

# Looking Down with Appreciation

Next time you are standing on a boat or wading at the beach and you see a dark green patch of grass swaying beneath the ripples, don't steer your boat through it and don't wish for bare sand.

Put on a pair of goggles, float over the blades, and thank the humble underwater meadow that is feeding our oceans, sheltering baby sea life, and quietly cooling our planet. Thank you.
