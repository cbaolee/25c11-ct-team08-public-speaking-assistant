Take a globe and spin it to the bottom half of the world, down between forty and sixty degrees south latitude.

Notice something strange: unlike the northern hemisphere, which is packed with continents, down here there is almost no land at all. For twenty thousand kilometers around the globe, the wind and waves of the Southern Ocean race in an unbroken circle without hitting a single mountain range -- creating what sailors for centuries have called the Roaring Forties, the Furious Fifties, and the Screaming Sixties.

And poking up out of that stormy, freezing ocean like tiny green emeralds are a handful of remote volcanic specks: the sub-Antarctic islands of {{ argument_subantarctic_island_examples }}.

Step off a Zodiac boat onto the beach of {{ argument_target_subantarctic_island }} in November, and the noise hits you like a rock concert.

Crowded onto every tussock hillside and pebble beach are {{ argument_total_breeding_seabirds_millions }} million marine animals -- towering {{ argument_penguin_species_on_island }}, roaring four-ton {{ argument_elephant_or_fur_seal_species }}, and high up on the windy ridges, the undisputed monarch of the southern skies: the {{ argument_target_albatross_species }}, with a wingspan of {{ argument_albatross_wingspan_meters }} meters -- the widest wingspan of any living bird on Earth!

Because ninety-nine percent of the Southern Ocean is open water, 100% of the air-breathing wildlife in that ocean has to squeeze onto less than 0.01% of the area -- these tiny sub-Antarctic islands -- just to lay an egg or give birth to a pup!

Climb up into the mossy tussock grass of {{ argument_target_subantarctic_island }} with me, sit down quietly five meters away from a nesting mound, and watch a pair of {{ argument_target_albatross_species }}.

With a {{ argument_albatross_wingspan_meters }}-meter wingspan and specialized shoulder-lock tendons that lock their wings out flat like airplane wings without using any muscle energy, a {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} can glide across the stormy Southern Ocean for {{ argument_flight_days_without_flapping }} straight days without flapping its wings once -- riding the wind shear just above the waves at {{ argument_flight_speed_kmh }} kilometers an hour. Some juveniles fly all the way around the globe in {{ argument_circumnavigation_days }} days before they ever touch land again!

When a young {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} finally returns to {{ argument_target_subantarctic_island }} to look for a mate, it doesn't rush into things. It spends several years learning a complex courtship dance of bill-clacking, sky-pointing, wing-spreading, and trumpeting with a single partner until their two routines match in perfect synchrony.

Once they bond, they mate for life -- which for an albatross means {{ argument_albatross_lifespan_years }} to seventy years!

Because it takes an entire year to raise a single giant, fluffy white chick on a mud pedestal -- with mom and dad taking turns flying {{ argument_foraging_trip_distance_km }} kilometers out to sea to bring back squid and fish oil -- a pair of {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} can only lay one single egg every two years.

Think about how precious every single parent and every single chick is when a bird doesn't even start breeding until age ten and only raises one baby every twenty-four months.

For millions of years, {{ argument_target_subantarctic_island }} had zero land mammals. A ten-kilogram {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} chick sitting in an open nest on the ground had nothing to fear except the wind and the occasional skua.

Then, in the {{ argument_sealing_ship_century }}, wooden sealing and whaling ships anchored off {{ argument_target_subantarctic_island }} -- and swimming ashore on mooring ropes and inside food barrels came house mice, black rats, cats, and rabbits.

Wait a second: you might understand how a feral cat could kill a seabird, or how rabbits could eat all the native {{ argument_megaherb_plant_species }} until the hillsides collapsed in mudslides.

What could a tiny twenty-gram house mouse possibly do to a ten-kilogram {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} chick that is three hundred times bigger than the mouse?

When our winter field team set up infrared night-vision cameras aimed at {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} nests on {{ argument_mouse_invaded_island_name }} in {{ argument_camera_discovery_year }}, the footage horrified the entire world of conservation.

As temperatures warmed in the sub-Antarctic and winter snow cover shrank, the invasive mouse population exploded while their summer insect food ran out by autumn. Starving in the winter dark, packs of mice climbed right up onto the backs of giant {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} chicks and even sitting adult birds at night -- chewing into them while they were still alive.

Because {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} evolved for millions of years on mammal-free islands, they have zero instinct to recognize a tiny crawling rodent as a threat. The giant chick simply sits on its nest, unable to fight back against a creature the size of its toe, until up to {{ argument_chick_mortality_by_mice_percentage }} of chicks on the island die before spring.

And underground, where {{ argument_burrowing_petrel_and_prion_count }} million nocturnal burrowing petrels and prions nest inside earthen tunnels, invasive rats and mice wiped out {{ argument_underground_seabird_loss_percentage }} of the population -- turning once-noisy islands into silent graveyards.

And for the adult {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} that survived on land and flew out to sea to find food for their chick, a second deadly trap was waiting along the edge of the Southern Ocean.

Every day, commercial longline and trawl fishing boats chasing {{ argument_commercial_southern_fish_species }} trail tens of thousands of baited hooks behind their sterns.

To a hungry {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} following a ship with its incredible sense of smell, a baited fishhook floating on the surface for ten seconds before it sinks looks like an easy squid lunch. The bird dives, swallows the hook, gets dragged underwater by the heavy line, and drowns.

By the late {{ argument_bycatch_crisis_decade }}, an estimated {{ argument_annual_albatross_bycatch_deaths }} albatrosses and petrels were drowning on commercial fishing hooks every single year -- pushing {{ argument_threatened_albatross_species_count }} out of the world's 22 albatross species toward extinction!

Here is how science, engineering, and human determination turned both of those nightmares around.

First, to stop the slaughter behind fishing boats, our {{ argument_albatross_task_force_name }} instructors climbed directly aboard commercial fishing vessels in {{ argument_partner_fishing_countries }} to test three simple, dirt-cheap fixes:

Bird-scaring streamer lines (tori lines): hanging bright orange plastic streamers from a pole on the back of the boat that flap in the wind right over the sinking hooks like a scarecrow, keeping birds back for the fifteen seconds it takes the bait to sink out of reach.
Line weighting: adding small lead weights to the lines so baited hooks sink twice as fast.
Night setting: setting hooks only after dark when visual hunters like {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} aren't feeding.

When fishing fleets in {{ argument_success_story_fishery_region }} adopted those three simple rules, accidental albatross deaths dropped by an astonishing {{ argument_bycatch_reduction_percentage }} -- going from thousands of dead birds a year down to near zero without costing fishermen a single fish!

Second, we went back to the sub-Antarctic islands to lift the siege of invasive mammals once and for all.

On {{ argument_restored_subantarctic_island_name }} -- a rugged, mountainous island spanning {{ argument_island_area_hectares }} hectares in the middle of the stormy Southern Ocean -- our coalition launched the largest island restoration expedition in history.

Using ship-based helicopters flying with centimeter-accurate differential GPS through freezing gales, alongside field teams hiking {{ argument_dog_handler_kilometers }} kilometers with conservation detection dogs over {{ argument_campaign_duration_years }} years, we removed every single invasive rat, mouse, and rabbit from the entire island!

If you step off a boat onto {{ argument_restored_subantarctic_island_name }} today, the recovery will bring tears to your eyes.

The hillsides that were chewed down to bare brown dirt by rabbits are once again waist-deep in giant flowering {{ argument_megaherb_plant_species }}. Insects and spiders that hadn't been seen in fifty years are crawling through the tussock grass.

Most magical of all, at night, the sky is filled once again with the whirring wings and eerie calls of millions of returning burrowing petrels, while species like the {{ argument_newly_recolonizing_bird_species }} have nested successfully on the main island for the first time in human memory -- and up on the windy ridges, {{ argument_target_albatross_species }} chicks sit safely in their nests all winter long without a single bite mark on their feathers.

Today, we are taking the blueprint from {{ argument_restored_subantarctic_island_name }} to the remaining mouse-invaded strongholds like {{ argument_next_target_island_name }}, where saving one island will protect two million seabirds in a single stroke.

The sub-Antarctic islands are the beating nurseries of the entire Southern Ocean.

When we keep baited hooks out of their flight paths at sea and give them back safe sanctuaries on land, we ensure that the greatest wings on Earth keep riding the winds at the bottom of the world. Thank you.
