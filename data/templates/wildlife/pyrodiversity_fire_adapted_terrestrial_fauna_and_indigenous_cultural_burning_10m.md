When we see footage of a wall of flame roaring through the woodlands of {{ argument_fire_prone_region_name }}, our immediate human instinct is horror. We view wildfire as an unnatural disaster -- an enemy that must be stamped out at all costs to protect wildlife and timber.

For over a century, forestry agencies across {{ argument_country_or_state_name }} operated under a strict policy of total fire suppression. Every spark was extinguished within hours. We thought we were saving the forest. Instead, we were building a ticking ecological time bomb.

By banishing small, frequent fires from the landscape, we starved fire-dependent wildlife of their habitat and set the stage for the catastrophic megafires we see today.

To understand terrestrial ecosystems in {{ argument_fire_prone_region_name }}, we have to realize that fire is not an external disturbance; it is an evolutionary architect. Over millions of years, hundreds of plant and animal species evolved not just to survive fire, but to require it.

Or look at the {{ argument_terrestrial_marsupial_or_mammal_species }} on the forest floor. It relies on a mosaic of pyrodiversity: unburned patches of old hollow logs for shelter right next to recently burned clearings where nutrient-rich {{ argument_fire_stimulated_fungi_or_shoots }} sprout after the smoke clears.

When we suppressed natural and cultural fires for {{ argument_suppression_duration_statistic }}, two devastating things happened. When a drought and lightning strike finally hit {{ argument_megafire_location_name }} in {{ argument_megafire_year }}, the resulting inferno burned at {{ argument_extreme_fire_temperature_statistic }}. How do we break this cycle? The answer isn't a new piece of firefighting technology; it is listening to the people who successfully managed these landscapes for {{ argument_indigenous_stewardship_millennia }}.

Long before colonial fire suppression, the {{ argument_indigenous_nation_name }} practiced cultural burning -- lighting cool, slow-moving, low-intensity fires during {{ argument_cultural_burning_season }} when humidity and soil moisture were just right.

Cool fire walks gently through the understory like water, clearing dry fuel without scorching the canopy and giving even the slowest {{ argument_slow_moving_terrestrial_species }} time to move to safety.
