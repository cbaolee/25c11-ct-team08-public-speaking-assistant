# Introduction: The Tiny Pink Bug Holding Up the Giants

When people dream of going on an expedition to **Antarctica**, they want to see the giants: hundred-ton blue whales, leopard seals with toothy grins, and millions of penguins diving off icebergs.

Nobody pays ten thousand dollars to take a cruise to Antarctica to see a **six-centimeter pink shrimp**.

Yet if you scoop a bucket of water out of the Southern Ocean off {{ antarctic_peninsula_location }}, that tiny translucent creature with big black eyes and glowing light organs on its belly -- **{{ target_krill_species }}** -- is the single most important animal in the entire southern hemisphere.

> Every whale, every penguin, every seal, every albatross, and every squid in Antarctica is either **eating {{ target_krill_species }} directly**, or eating someone who just ate {{ target_krill_species }} vijf minutes ago!

# Swarms You Can See from Outer Space

It is almost impossible for the human brain to wrap itself around how many {{ target_krill_species }} live in the Southern Ocean.

Together, all the {{ target_krill_species }} around Antarctica weigh roughly **{{ krill_biomass_million_tons }} million metric tons** -- more than the weight of **every human being on Earth**.

When they gather into a "superswarm" off {{ swarm_location_name }}, a single swarm can stretch across **{{ swarm_area_sq_km }} square kilometers**, pack **{{ krill_per_cubic_meter }} individuals into every cubic meter of water**, and turn the blue ocean so pink that satellites can photograph the swarm from **outer space**!

And they aren't just floating bird food; they are **tiny climate superheroes**.

Every single day, billions of {{ target_krill_species }} eat carbon-rich microscopic algae at the surface and then swim **{{ daily_vertical_migration_meters }} meters straight down** into the deep ocean, where they shed their heavy outer shells and drop fast-sinking carbon pellets to the seafloor.

> That daily elevator ride by a six-centimeter shrimp locks away **{{ annual_krill_carbon_million_tons }} million tons of carbon** in the deep ocean every single year -- equal to taking **{{ car_emissions_equivalent_millions }} million cars** off the road!

# The Upside-Down Lawn Under the Winter Ice

Here is the secret of how {{ target_krill_species }} survive the pitch-black Antarctic winter when there is no open-water phytoplankton to eat: **they need sea ice just as much as a polar bear does**.

When winter sea ice freezes over the ocean around {{ winter_nursery_region }}, microscopic green algae grow inside the tiny salty channels on the bottom of the ice -- turning the underside of the ice sheet into an **upside-down green pasture**.

Flip an underwater camera upward under the winter ice, and you see millions of baby larval {{ target_krill_species }} walking **upside down** on the ceiling of the ice, using the bristly hairs on their legs like tiny rakes to scrape algae off the ice while hiding in ice cracks from hungry fish!

# A Double Squeeze on the Pantry

Over the past {{ warming_period_years }} years along the rapidly warming **{{ warming_antarctic_sector }}**, however, winter sea ice is forming later and covering {{ ice_decline_percentage }} less area. No winter ice ceiling means no upside-down lawn for baby krill to eat -- pushing the center of the {{ target_krill_species }} population **{{ southward_shift_km }} kilometers further south** toward the pole.

And right as climate change is squeezing {{ target_krill_species }} into smaller pockets of icy water, a second pressure has arrived: **giant industrial vacuum ships**.

Modern super-trawlers from {{ fishing_nations_list }} use continuous underwater hoses to pump up **{{ annual_krill_catch_tons }} tons of {{ target_krill_species }}** a year -- not to feed starving people, but to grind them up into **{{ commercial_krill_uses_list }}**!

Even though {{ annual_krill_catch_tons }} tons sounds like a small fraction of the whole ocean, the ships don't spread out across the whole ocean; they park right in the exact narrow bays of {{ concentrated_fishing_strait }} where **breeding chinstrap and gentoo penguins** have to swim out every afternoon to find dinner for their chicks!

> When a super-trawler vacuums up the krill swarm five kilometers outside a penguin colony, mother penguins have to swim twice as far and come back with empty stomachs.

And third, consumers have power right at the pharmacy shelf: we don't need to take omega-3 pills squeezed out of the mouths of Antarctic penguins and whales when we can get the exact same healthy oils directly from **farmed marine microalgae** grown in tanks on land!

> In nature, the biggest giants on Earth always stand on the shoulders of the smallest creatures.

Protect the six-centimeter pink shrimp under the ice, and the whales, the penguins, and the Southern Ocean will thrive for centuries to come. Thank you.
