When people dream of going on an expedition to Antarctica, they want to see the giants: hundred-ton blue whales, leopard seals with toothy grins, and millions of penguins diving off icebergs.

Nobody pays ten thousand dollars to take a cruise to Antarctica to see a six-centimeter pink shrimp.

Yet if you scoop a bucket of water out of the Southern Ocean off {{ argument_antarctic_peninsula_location }}, that tiny translucent creature with big black eyes and glowing light organs on its belly -- {{ argument_target_krill_species }} -- is the single most important animal in the entire southern hemisphere.

Every whale, every penguin, every seal, every albatross, and every squid in Antarctica is either eating {{ argument_target_krill_species }} directly, or eating someone who just ate {{ argument_target_krill_species }} vijf minutes ago!

It is almost impossible for the human brain to wrap itself around how many {{ argument_target_krill_species }} live in the Southern Ocean.

Together, all the {{ argument_target_krill_species }} around Antarctica weigh roughly {{ argument_krill_biomass_million_tons }} million metric tons -- more than the weight of every human being on Earth.

When they gather into a "superswarm" off {{ argument_swarm_location_name }}, a single swarm can stretch across {{ argument_swarm_area_sq_km }} square kilometers, pack {{ argument_krill_per_cubic_meter }} individuals into every cubic meter of water, and turn the blue ocean so pink that satellites can photograph the swarm from outer space!

And they aren't just floating bird food; they are tiny climate superheroes.

Every single day, billions of {{ argument_target_krill_species }} eat carbon-rich microscopic algae at the surface and then swim {{ argument_daily_vertical_migration_meters }} meters straight down into the deep ocean, where they shed their heavy outer shells and drop fast-sinking carbon pellets to the seafloor.

That daily elevator ride by a six-centimeter shrimp locks away {{ argument_annual_krill_carbon_million_tons }} million tons of carbon in the deep ocean every single year -- equal to taking {{ argument_car_emissions_equivalent_millions }} million cars off the road!

Here is the secret of how {{ argument_target_krill_species }} survive the pitch-black Antarctic winter when there is no open-water phytoplankton to eat: they need sea ice just as much as a polar bear does.

When winter sea ice freezes over the ocean around {{ argument_winter_nursery_region }}, microscopic green algae grow inside the tiny salty channels on the bottom of the ice -- turning the underside of the ice sheet into an upside-down green pasture.

Flip an underwater camera upward under the winter ice, and you see millions of baby larval {{ argument_target_krill_species }} walking upside down on the ceiling of the ice, using the bristly hairs on their legs like tiny rakes to scrape algae off the ice while hiding in ice cracks from hungry fish!

Over the past {{ argument_warming_period_years }} years along the rapidly warming {{ argument_warming_antarctic_sector }}, however, winter sea ice is forming later and covering {{ argument_ice_decline_percentage }} less area. No winter ice ceiling means no upside-down lawn for baby krill to eat -- pushing the center of the {{ argument_target_krill_species }} population {{ argument_southward_shift_km }} kilometers further south toward the pole.

And right as climate change is squeezing {{ argument_target_krill_species }} into smaller pockets of icy water, a second pressure has arrived: giant industrial vacuum ships.

Modern super-trawlers from {{ argument_fishing_nations_list }} use continuous underwater hoses to pump up {{ argument_annual_krill_catch_tons }} tons of {{ argument_target_krill_species }} a year -- not to feed starving people, but to grind them up into {{ argument_commercial_krill_uses_list }}!

Even though {{ argument_annual_krill_catch_tons }} tons sounds like a small fraction of the whole ocean, the ships don't spread out across the whole ocean; they park right in the exact narrow bays of {{ argument_concentrated_fishing_strait }} where breeding chinstrap and gentoo penguins have to swim out every afternoon to find dinner for their chicks!

When a super-trawler vacuums up the krill swarm five kilometers outside a penguin colony, mother penguins have to swim twice as far and come back with empty stomachs.

In {{ argument_policy_campaign_year }}, our coalition of polar scientists, fishing fleet managers, and conservation groups took this data to {{ argument_international_body_name }}.

First, we convinced {{ argument_voluntary_buffer_companies_count }} major krill-fishing companies to establish voluntary seasonal no-fishing buffer zones extending {{ argument_buffer_width_km }} kilometers around every breeding penguin colony during the summer chick-raising months.

Second, we are campaigning to designate the {{ argument_proposed_mpa_name }} as a permanent Marine Protected Area -- protecting the exact sea-ice nurseries where baby {{ argument_target_krill_species }} hatch and feed the returning great whales.

And third, consumers have power right at the pharmacy shelf: we don't need to take omega-3 pills squeezed out of the mouths of Antarctic penguins and whales when we can get the exact same healthy oils directly from farmed marine microalgae grown in tanks on land!

In nature, the biggest giants on Earth always stand on the shoulders of the smallest creatures.

Protect the six-centimeter pink shrimp under the ice, and the whales, the penguins, and the Southern Ocean will thrive for centuries to come. Thank you.
