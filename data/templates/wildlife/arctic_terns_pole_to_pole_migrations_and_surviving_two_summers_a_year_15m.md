# Introduction: The Bird That Sees More Daylight Than Any Creature on Earth

Hold out two AA batteries in your hand.

That -- roughly **{{ bird_weight_grams }} grams**, about the weight of a small apple -- is the entire body weight of an **{{ target_tern_species }}**.

Now imagine that tiny white seabird hatching out of a speckled egg on a gravelly beach in **{{ arctic_breeding_location }}**, right near the top of the world, in July.

For the first two months of its life, the sun never sets. Then, in August, as the first chill of Arctic autumn touches the air, this three-month-old bird lifts its narrow wings into the wind and starts flying south.

It flies down the entire length of the Atlantic Ocean, past Europe, past Africa, across the Roaring Forties, all the way to the pack ice of **Antarctica** at the bottom of the world -- arriving just in time for the **Antarctic summer** where the sun *also* never sets! And in March, it turns around and flies all the way back to the Arctic!

> Because it chases the summer from the North Pole to the South Pole and back every single year, an **{{ target_tern_species }}** experiences **two summers a year** and sees more daylight than any other living creature in the history of planet Earth.

# The Day We Opened the Backpack Data

For over a century, ornithologists knew from metal leg bands that {{ target_tern_species }} flew from the Arctic to the Antarctic. Looking at a globe, we calculated that a straight line from {{ arctic_breeding_location }} to Antarctica and back was about forty thousand kilometers a year -- which already sounded almost impossible for a {{ bird_weight_grams }}-gram bird.

Then, in {{ geolocator_study_year }}, electronics finally got small enough for our research team in {{ arctic_breeding_location }} to clip a tiny **light-level geolocator** weighing just **{{ tag_weight_grams }} grams** -- lighter than a paperclip -- onto the legs of {{ tagged_birds_count }} breeding {{ target_tern_species }}.

When we recaptured those exact same birds on their gravel nests the following summer and plugged their tiny recorders into a laptop, our jaws hit the table.

They hadn't flown forty thousand kilometers. Because they ride giant figure-eight **planetary wind loops** across the oceans instead of fighting headwinds in a straight line, our birds had flown an average of **{{ average_annual_distance_km }} kilometers** in a single year -- with one champion bird logging **{{ record_annual_distance_km }} kilometers**!

Now multiply that by how long an {{ target_tern_species }} lives. These birds can live for **{{ maximum_lifespan_years }} years**.

> Over its thirty-year lifetime, a single {{ bird_weight_grams }}-gram {{ target_tern_species }} flies roughly **{{ lifetime_distance_million_km }} million kilometers** -- equivalent to flying **all the way from the Earth to the Moon and back three times** on its own two wings!

# How Do You Sleep and Eat Over Five Oceans?

How on earth does a bird the size of a robin cross the entire planet twice a year without dropping into the sea from exhaustion?

First, it is built like a high-performance **sailplane**. With long, narrow, swept-back wings, an {{ target_tern_species }} barely has to flap when the wind is blowing; it glides effortlessly on the updrafts bouncing off ocean waves, burning almost no more energy in flight than it does sitting on a rock. It has even mastered the art of **unihemispheric slow-wave sleep** -- literally putting half of its brain to sleep for a few seconds at a time while the other half keeps one eye open and steers!

Second, unlike ducks or gulls that have oily waterproof feathers and can float on the waves like corks all day, an {{ target_tern_species }} has light, non-oily plumage. If it sits in the water too long, it gets waterlogged. So it feeds on the wing -- hovering in mid-air and plunge-diving into the top {{ plunge_dive_depth_cm }} centimeters of the ocean to snatch **{{ surface_prey_species_list }}**.

Third, our geolocator tracks revealed the secret that keeps them alive between the poles: **five mid-ocean refueling stations**.

Right in the middle of the North Atlantic at {{ north_atlantic_hotspot_name }}, and down off {{ southern_hemisphere_hotspot_name }}, cold deep-water currents crash into underwater mountain chains and push nutrient-rich water up to the surface. Our terns paused at those exact mid-ocean oases for **{{ stopover_duration_days }} days**, gorging on plankton and fish to pack on fuel before making the final sprint across the equator!

# When the Polar Calendars Drift Out of Sync

Because an {{ target_tern_species }} stitches the North Pole and the South Pole together in a single year, it is exposed to **double the climate risk** of any bird that stays in one hemisphere.

Up at its northern breeding colonies in {{ arctic_breeding_location }}, the crisis is all about **timing and food depth**.

When two parent terns hatch their chicks on the gravel beach in {{ chick_hatching_month }}, they have to fly out to the cold water edge every twenty minutes and bring back a tiny, high-fat fish -- like **{{ arctic_forage_fish_species }}** -- holding it crosswise in their bill to feed the growing chick.

In recent years, as sea surface temperatures around {{ arctic_breeding_location }} spiked by **{{ water_warming_degrees }} degrees Celsius**, those cold-water {{ arctic_forage_fish_species }} dove deeper into cold layers twenty meters down -- **completely out of reach** of a plunge-diving tern that can only reach the top {{ plunge_dive_depth_cm }} centimeters of water!

Desperate parents tried feeding their chicks flat, spiny **{{ low_quality_replacement_fish }}** or marine insects instead -- which either got stuck sideways in the tiny chicks' throats or had {{ calorie_deficit_percentage }} fewer calories, causing whole colonies of {{ colony_size_pairs }} pairs to abandon their nests in warm-water summers.

At the same time, melting Arctic sea ice has allowed **{{ terrestrial_nest_predator }}** and invading **{{ southern_gull_competitor }}** to swim out to low-lying offshore nesting islands that used to be protected by moats of shifting pack ice, wiping out thousands of eggs in a weekend.

And six months later, when the surviving terns arrive at the **Antarctic pack ice** in December to molt all their flight feathers during their winter vacation, they find the southern ice edge retreating and industrial krill trawlers competing for their winter food!

# Protecting the Stepping Stones from Pole to Pole

How do you protect a bird that belongs to **no single country** -- a bird that spends eighty percent of its life out over the international **High Seas** beyond national borders?

For a long time, the High Seas -- which cover nearly half of planet Earth -- were the Wild West of the ocean, with zero legal mechanism to create marine national parks in international waters.

Then, using tracking data from our {{ target_tern_species }}, albatrosses, whales, and sea turtles, scientists pinpointed the exact **mid-ocean biological oases** where the whole ocean comes to eat.

In {{ treaty_landmark_year }}, backed by this migration map, the {{ international_marine_commission }} established the **{{ north_atlantic_mpa_name }}** -- protecting nearly **{{ protected_high_seas_area_sq_km }} square kilometers** of international ocean right over the exact mid-Atlantic refueling station our {{ target_tern_species }} had revealed to us! And with the historic **UN High Seas Biodiversity Treaty**, we finally have the global legal tool to protect every stepping stone along their pole-to-pole highway.

Meanwhile, right on the breeding islands in {{ arctic_breeding_location }}, local community wardens and student volunteers spend every June and July guarding nesting beaches from disturbances, putting out simple wooden **A-frame chick shelters** on bare gravel so chicks can hide from predatory gulls and cold rain, and managing invasive predators before the terns touch down in May.

> Next time you are standing on a windy northern beach in June and a small white bird with a bright red bill dives furiously at your hat to protect two speckled eggs in the gravel, tip your cap in respect.

That {{ bird_weight_grams }}-gram parent just flew from the icebergs of Antarctica to your beach -- and if we keep its ocean stepping stones teeming with fish and its polar shores safe, it will keep chasing the endless summer from the top of the world to the bottom forever. Thank you.
