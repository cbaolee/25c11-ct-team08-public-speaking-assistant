Think about the worst winter snowstorm you have ever walked through in your life -- the kind where your fingers go numb in three minutes and you run inside for a hot cup of cocoa.

Now imagine standing on a flat sheet of frozen ocean in Antarctica in the middle of June. The sun went down in April and won't come back up until August. It is pitch dark, twenty-four hours a day. The temperature is minus {{ argument_winter_air_temperature_celsius }} degrees Celsius, and katabatic winds are screaming off the polar plateau at {{ argument_hurricane_wind_speed_kmh }} kilometers an hour.

Every other animal on the continent -- every seal, every whale, every seabird -- has packed up and swum north toward warmer water.

Only one creature walks inland into the dark to have its baby right in the middle of the Antarctic winter: the {{ argument_target_penguin_species }}.

While the mothers trek {{ argument_mother_foraging_trek_km }} kilometers back to the open sea to find fish, the fathers stay behind on the ice for {{ argument_father_fasting_days }} straight days without eating a single bite of food -- balancing a single precious egg on the tops of their feet under a warm fold of belly skin!

How on earth do {{ argument_colony_male_count }} fathers standing on bare sea ice survive four months of minus-sixty-degree blizzards without freezing solid?

Not by toughing it out alone. They cuddle.

When a gale hits the colony at {{ argument_antarctic_colony_location }}, thousands of male {{ argument_target_penguin_species }} pack together so tightly -- up to {{ argument_birds_per_square_meter }} birds per square meter -- that in the very center of the huddle, the temperature rises to a cozy plus {{ argument_huddle_center_temperature }} degrees Celsius!

And nobody gets left out on the freezing edge. Every {{ argument_shuffle_interval_seconds }} seconds, the whole huddle takes one tiny baby step forward in a slow-motion wave -- rotating the cold birds from the outside into the warm middle, and once they warm up, cycling them back out to take their turn blocking the wind for their neighbors.

In Antarctica, survival isn't about "survival of the fittest individual"; if you try to be a lone wolf on the ice, you die in an hour. You only survive if the whole group takes care of each other.

Because {{ argument_target_penguin_species }} breed on remote sea ice thousands of kilometers from human cities in the middle of winter darkness, for a century scientists had no idea how many colonies actually existed around Antarctica. You simply couldn't fly a small plane or drive a ship into half of those bays.

Then, in {{ argument_satellite_discovery_year }}, my research team at {{ argument_polar_research_institute }} had a funny idea: what if we looked for penguins using space satellites orbiting {{ argument_satellite_altitude_km }} kilometers above the Earth?

Even from space, you can't see an individual penguin that is one meter tall. What can you see on bright white snow from space? Giant brown stains of penguin poop -- what scientists politely call guano!

By training computer algorithms to scan high-resolution satellite images of the entire Antarctic coastline for reddish-brown smudges on the sea ice, we discovered {{ argument_newly_discovered_colonies_count }} brand-new {{ argument_target_penguin_species }} colonies that no human being had ever laid eyes on -- boosting our map of known colonies by {{ argument_colony_map_increase_percentage }}!

Those same satellites, however, also revealed a heartbreaking emergency unfolding in the {{ argument_vulnerable_antarctic_region }}.

Remember that {{ argument_target_penguin_species }} don't nest on solid rock land like smaller penguins do; they nest on land-fast sea ice -- frozen ocean water locked against the coast.

For a chick to survive, that ice platform has to stay frozen solid from April all the way until December or January, when the chick finally sheds its fluffy gray baby down and grows waterproof adult feathers.

In the spring of {{ argument_catastrophic_melt_year }}, record-low sea ice across the {{ argument_vulnerable_antarctic_region }} caused the ice sheet beneath {{ argument_failed_colonies_count }} colonies to crack apart and melt into the sea in November -- a full month before the chicks had grown their waterproof feathers.

When the ice floor gives way beneath a colony in November, thousands of downy chicks that cannot swim yet are dropped into freezing water -- resulting in what we call total breeding failure across an entire region.

If global greenhouse gas emissions stay on their current track, climate models warn that over {{ argument_projected_decline_percentage }} of {{ argument_target_penguin_species }} colonies could be quasi-extinct by the year {{ argument_projection_year }}.

Does that mean the {{ argument_target_penguin_species }} is doomed? Absolutely not -- because they have a secret weapon that land animals don't have: they can move.

Unlike other penguins that stubbornly return to the exact same pebble nest even when it is ruined, our satellite tracking proved that when sea ice goes bad in one bay, {{ argument_target_penguin_species }} colonies will actually pack up and march {{ argument_relocation_distance_km }} kilometers to find thicker, more stable ice shelves or even climb up onto flat icebergs and glacier tongues!

And right now, our job is to make sure that when they move to those southern climate refuges, their food is waiting for them.

By combining our satellite colony maps with tracking tags on swimming penguins, we are working with the {{ argument_antarctic_treaty_commission_name }} to establish a ring of Marine Protected Areas across the Ross Sea, the Weddell Sea, and the East Antarctic -- banning industrial krill and silverfish trawlers from fishing inside the critical foraging zones where penguin parents hunt to feed their chicks.

The {{ argument_target_penguin_species }} has mastered the art of standing together in the darkest storm on Earth.

If humanity learns just a fraction of that cooperation -- cutting our carbon emissions and guarding their icy ocean pantries -- the tallest, toughest birds on the planet will keep marching out of the Antarctic dawn. Thank you.
