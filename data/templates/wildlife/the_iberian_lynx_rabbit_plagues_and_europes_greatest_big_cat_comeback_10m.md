# Introduction: Ninety-Four Cats Away from the First Cat Extinction in Two Thousand Years

Ask someone where the world's most endangered wild cats live, and they will guess the jungles of Asia or the savannas of Africa.

They almost never guess sunny **{{ european_peninsula_name }}** -- just an hour's drive from modern European highways, high-speed trains, and olive groves!

Yet back in the year **{{ crisis_year_2002 }}**, if you walked through the Mediterranean scrublands of southern **{{ country_a_name }}** and **{{ country_b_name }}**, you were standing in the home of a spotted wild cat with long black ear tufts, a handsome white beard, and golden eyes that was about to make tragic history: the **{{ target_lynx_species }}**.

By {{ crisis_year_2002 }}, field censuses found that the {{ target_lynx_species }} had crashed from over {{ historical_population_count }} cats down to **just {{ lowest_population_count }} individuals** trapped in two tiny pockets of {{ southern_region_name }} -- with only **{{ breeding_females_low_count }} breeding females** left in the entire world!

> Had those last {{ lowest_population_count }} cats died out, the {{ target_lynx_species }} would have become the **very first wild cat species on Earth to go extinct since the prehistoric saber-toothed tiger** ten thousand years ago.

Instead, what happened over the next twenty-two years became the **greatest cat comeback story in the history of the IUCN Red List**.

# The Pickiest Eater in the Cat Family

Why did the {{ target_lynx_species }} come within a whisker of extinction in {{ crisis_year_2002 }}?

Because unlike a leopard or a mountain lion that will happily switch to eating deer, raccoons, or birds when its favorite prey runs low, the {{ target_lynx_species }} is an **extreme food specialist**.

Fully **{{ rabbit_diet_percentage }} of its entire diet** is one single animal: the wild **{{ prey_rabbit_species }}**. A mother {{ target_lynx_species }} raising three kittens needs to catch **{{ rabbits_per_day_for_mother }} rabbits every single day** just to produce milk! If a valley doesn't have enough rabbits, female lynxes literally stop ovulating and have zero kittens.

In the second half of the twentieth century, **two devastating viral plagues** -- first *myxomatosis* in the 1950s, and then *Rabbit Hemorrhagic Disease* in the 1980s -- swept through the wild rabbit populations of {{ european_peninsula_name }}, wiping out **{{ rabbit_crash_percentage }} of the rabbits**!

At the exact same time, Mediterranean scrubland was being bulldozed for industrial eucalyptus and olive plantations, new high-speed highways were slicing through lynx territories, and illegal snares and box traps set for foxes and rabbits were killing the few starving lynxes left.

# To Save a Predator, First Build Apartment Buildings for Its Prey

When {{ conservation_coalition_name }} launched an all-out rescue plan in {{ program_start_year }} backed by the European Union's **LIFE program**, the biologists knew two things: first, we needed a captive breeding safety net; and second, **releasing captive-born cats into a forest with no rabbits was pointless**.

So before releasing a single lynx, field teams spent years becoming **wild rabbit real-estate developers**!

Across thousands of hectares of private hunting estates and olive farms in {{ restoration_regions_list }}, we built hundreds of artificial **underground rabbit warrens** out of buried concrete tubes, wooden pallets, and brush piles, vaccinated and released **{{ released_rabbits_count }} disease-resistant wild rabbits** to rebuild the food base, and signed stewardship agreements with **{{ private_landowners_count }} private landowners, hunters, and olive farmers** who agreed to remove illegal traps and leave wild scrub corridors between their trees!

Meanwhile, inside {{ breeding_centers_count }} specialized captive breeding centers, vets cracked a tricky behavioral puzzle: in the wild, two-month-old {{ target_lynx_species }} kittens go through a brief phase where **siblings fight so fiercely** that one kitten can kill the other if food is tight! By monitoring litters 24/7 on closed-circuit cameras and temporarily separating feisty siblings for just a few weeks while teaching them to hunt live rabbits in huge natural enclosures without ever seeing a human face, survival rates of captive-born kittens soared!

# Fixing the Highways and Connecting the Clans

As we began releasing young {{ target_lynx_species }} back into restored rabbit-rich valleys in {{ reintroduction_sites_list }} starting in **{{ first_release_year }}**, a new challenge emerged: as the population grew and young cats set out to find new territories, **high-speed roads** became their number-one killer.

Using GPS collar maps showing the exact hundred-meter stretches of highway where lynxes tried to cross at night, transportation engineers built **{{ wildlife_underpasses_and_ecoducts_count }} green wildlife overpasses, enlarged drainage underpasses, and {{ guide_fencing_km }} kilometers of funnel fencing** -- cutting roadkill deaths in treated hotspots by **{{ roadkill_reduction_percentage }}**!

And when genetic tests showed that one isolated clan had developed high rates of genetic kidney disease and seizures from decades of inbreeding, field vets carefully **swapped wild and captive-bred males** between the regions -- erasing the inbreeding bottleneck in two generations!

# ATwenty-Fold Surge Off the Critical List

Now look at the numbers announced by the IUCN Red List in **{{ milestone_year_2024 }}**.

From just **{{ lowest_population_count }} {{ target_lynx_species }}** in {{ crisis_year_2002 }}, the wild population across {{ country_a_name }} and {{ country_b_name }} has **skyrocketed twenty-fold to over {{ current_lynx_population }} cats** -- including **{{ current_breeding_females_count }} breeding females** roaming across {{ current_occupied_sites_count }} different regions where the lynx had been extinct for fifty years!

Because of that twenty-year triumph, the {{ target_lynx_species }} officially graduated off the *Endangered* list down to *Vulnerable* -- the biggest single-species recovery of any cat in conservation history.

> The {{ target_lynx_species }} taught us a simple recipe for saving a predator: **bring back its food, connect its roads, partner with the people who own the land, and never accept extinction as inevitable**. Thank you.
