Ask someone where the world's most endangered wild cats live, and they will guess the jungles of Asia or the savannas of Africa.

They almost never guess sunny {{ argument_european_peninsula_name }} -- just an hour's drive from modern European highways, high-speed trains, and olive groves!

Yet back in the year {{ argument_crisis_year_2002 }}, if you walked through the Mediterranean scrublands of southern {{ argument_country_a_name }} and {{ argument_country_b_name }}, you were standing in the home of a spotted wild cat with long black ear tufts, a handsome white beard, and golden eyes that was about to make tragic history: the {{ argument_target_lynx_species }}.

By {{ argument_crisis_year_2002 }}, field censuses found that the {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} had crashed from over {{ argument_historical_population_count }} cats down to just {{ argument_lowest_population_count }} individuals trapped in two tiny pockets of {{ argument_southern_region_name }} -- with only {{ argument_breeding_females_low_count }} breeding females left in the entire world!

Had those last {{ argument_lowest_population_count }} cats died out, the {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} would have become the very first wild cat species on Earth to go extinct since the prehistoric saber-toothed tiger ten thousand years ago.

Instead, what happened over the next twenty-two years became the greatest cat comeback story in the history of the IUCN Red List.

Why did the {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} come within a whisker of extinction in {{ argument_crisis_year_2002 }}?

Because unlike a leopard or a mountain lion that will happily switch to eating deer, raccoons, or birds when its favorite prey runs low, the {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} is an extreme food specialist.

Fully {{ argument_rabbit_diet_percentage }} of its entire diet is one single animal: the wild {{ argument_prey_rabbit_species }}. A mother {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} raising three kittens needs to catch {{ argument_rabbits_per_day_for_mother }} rabbits every single day just to produce milk! If a valley doesn't have enough rabbits, female lynxes literally stop ovulating and have zero kittens.

In the second half of the twentieth century, two devastating viral plagues -- first myxomatosis in the 1950s, and then Rabbit Hemorrhagic Disease in the 1980s -- swept through the wild rabbit populations of {{ argument_european_peninsula_name }}, wiping out {{ argument_rabbit_crash_percentage }} of the rabbits!

At the exact same time, Mediterranean scrubland was being bulldozed for industrial eucalyptus and olive plantations, new high-speed highways were slicing through lynx territories, and illegal snares and box traps set for foxes and rabbits were killing the few starving lynxes left.

When {{ argument_conservation_coalition_name }} launched an all-out rescue plan in {{ argument_program_start_year }} backed by the European Union's LIFE program, the biologists knew two things: first, we needed a captive breeding safety net; and second, releasing captive-born cats into a forest with no rabbits was pointless.

So before releasing a single lynx, field teams spent years becoming wild rabbit real-estate developers!

Across thousands of hectares of private hunting estates and olive farms in {{ argument_restoration_regions_list }}, we built hundreds of artificial underground rabbit warrens out of buried concrete tubes, wooden pallets, and brush piles, vaccinated and released {{ argument_released_rabbits_count }} disease-resistant wild rabbits to rebuild the food base, and signed stewardship agreements with {{ argument_private_landowners_count }} private landowners, hunters, and olive farmers who agreed to remove illegal traps and leave wild scrub corridors between their trees!

Meanwhile, inside {{ argument_breeding_centers_count }} specialized captive breeding centers, vets cracked a tricky behavioral puzzle: in the wild, two-month-old {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} kittens go through a brief phase where siblings fight so fiercely that one kitten can kill the other if food is tight! By monitoring litters 24/7 on closed-circuit cameras and temporarily separating feisty siblings for just a few weeks while teaching them to hunt live rabbits in huge natural enclosures without ever seeing a human face, survival rates of captive-born kittens soared!

As we began releasing young {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} back into restored rabbit-rich valleys in {{ argument_reintroduction_sites_list }} starting in {{ argument_first_release_year }}, a new challenge emerged: as the population grew and young cats set out to find new territories, high-speed roads became their number-one killer.

Using GPS collar maps showing the exact hundred-meter stretches of highway where lynxes tried to cross at night, transportation engineers built {{ argument_wildlife_underpasses_and_ecoducts_count }} green wildlife overpasses, enlarged drainage underpasses, and {{ argument_guide_fencing_km }} kilometers of funnel fencing -- cutting roadkill deaths in treated hotspots by {{ argument_roadkill_reduction_percentage }}!

And when genetic tests showed that one isolated clan had developed high rates of genetic kidney disease and seizures from decades of inbreeding, field vets carefully swapped wild and captive-bred males between the regions -- erasing the inbreeding bottleneck in two generations!

Now look at the numbers announced by the IUCN Red List in {{ argument_milestone_year_2024 }}.

From just {{ argument_lowest_population_count }} {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} in {{ argument_crisis_year_2002 }}, the wild population across {{ argument_country_a_name }} and {{ argument_country_b_name }} has skyrocketed twenty-fold to over {{ argument_current_lynx_population }} cats -- including {{ argument_current_breeding_females_count }} breeding females roaming across {{ argument_current_occupied_sites_count }} different regions where the lynx had been extinct for fifty years!

Because of that twenty-year triumph, the {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} officially graduated off the Endangered list down to Vulnerable -- the biggest single-species recovery of any cat in conservation history.

The {{ argument_target_lynx_species }} taught us a simple recipe for saving a predator: bring back its food, connect its roads, partner with the people who own the land, and never accept extinction as inevitable. Thank you.
