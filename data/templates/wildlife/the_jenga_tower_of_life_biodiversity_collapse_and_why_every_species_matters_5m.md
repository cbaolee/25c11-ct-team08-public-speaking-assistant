# Introduction: Playing Jenga With the Floor Beneath Our Feet

Everyone in this room has played a game of **Jenga** at least once. You stack fifty-four little wooden blocks into a tall tower, and then you take turns pulling blocks out of the bottom and middle, one by one.

Pull out the first block -- the tower doesn't even wobble. Pull out the fifth block, the tenth block, the twentieth block -- the tower is still standing, and you start thinking, *"Hey, maybe we don't even need the blocks at the bottom!"*

Until someone slides out **one single block** that was quietly holding the weight of everything above it -- and in half a second, the entire tower crashes to the table.

> For the last hundred years, humanity has been playing a giant game of Jenga with the **living web of wildlife on planet Earth** -- pulling species out one by one and assuming the tower will never fall.

# We Are Sitting on the Top Floor

The biggest mistake we make when we hear the word **"wildlife conservation"** is thinking that we are doing animals a charitable favor -- like donating spare change to a distant cause that has nothing to do with our own lives.

Look at where human beings sit on that Jenga tower.

We don't sit at the bottom. **We sit on the very top floor.**

Every calorie of food on your dinner plate, every drop of clean water in your tap, the cotton in your shirt, the timber in your roof, and {{ breathable_oxygen_percentage }} of the oxygen in your lungs is manufactured and delivered by the wild plants, insects, fungi, fish, and animals working on the floors below us.

> When we protect a wetland, a coral reef, or an endangered pollinator, we aren't just saving a cute animal for a nature documentary; **we are saving the structural beams holding up our own house**.

# Putting the Blocks Back In

The good news about nature's tower is that unlike wooden Jenga blocks, **living blocks can multiply and heal themselves** when we give them back their space.

In {{ restored_watershed_location }}, simply fencing out development along a {{ buffer_width_meters }}-meter riverside wildlife corridor and bringing back native {{ keystone_restored_species_list }} brought the whole food web roaring back in {{ recovery_years_statistic }} years -- cutting flood damage in town by {{ flood_reduction_percentage }} and delivering crystal-clear water for free.

Next time someone asks you why we should care about a tiny frog, a wild bee, or a forest tree you have never met, remind them of the Jenga tower: **every block we save keeps the whole living world standing strong**. Thank you.
