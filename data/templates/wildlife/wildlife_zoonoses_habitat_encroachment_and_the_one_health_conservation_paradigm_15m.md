In {{ argument_outbreak_year }}, in a small farming settlement carved out of the freshly logged rainforest of {{ argument_frontier_region_name }}, a mysterious neurological fever began striking down workers at a commercial {{ argument_livestock_facility_type }} built right beneath the fruiting trees of the forest edge.

At first, public health officials assumed it was a known mosquito-borne virus. Only when veterinary pathologists and wildlife ecologists arrived on the ground did the full picture emerge.

Vast forest fires and timber clearing across {{ argument_deforested_region_name }} had destroyed {{ argument_habitat_loss_statistic }} of the fruiting canopy. Starving and displaced, thousands of {{ argument_reservoir_wildlife_species }} -- which had peacefully carried {{ argument_emerging_pathogen_name }} for millennia without getting sick -- were forced to roost in the orchard trees planted directly over the crowded pig pens below. Half-eaten fruit dropped into the pens, amplifying the virus in livestock before jumping to human handlers.

That outbreak didn't happen because wild animals attacked human civilization; it happened because we shredded the ecological buffer that had kept the virus safely contained in the forest for a million years.

For over a century, our universities and governments divided the living world into three separate silos. Medical doctors treated human patients in hospitals; veterinarians looked after farm cows and pet dogs; and wildlife biologists studied wild animals in distant parks. Rarely did the three ever sit in the same room.

Yet nature recognizes no boundary between human bodies, domestic animals, and wild ecosystems.Roughly {{ argument_zoonotic_infectious_disease_percentage }} of all emerging infectious diseases in humans are zoonotic -- meaning they originate in animals -- and over {{ argument_wildlife_origin_percentage }} of those come from terrestrial wildlife.

Even less discussed is the reverse direction, which we call reverse zoonosis or zooanthroponosis. When ecotourists, researchers, or stray village dogs enter {{ argument_great_ape_or_carnivore_sanctuary }} carrying a common human cold virus like {{ argument_human_respiratory_virus_name }} or canine distemper, it can wipe out {{ argument_mortality_rate_in_wildlife }} of an endangered {{ argument_susceptible_wildlife_species }} group that has zero immunity to human illnesses.

Human health, livestock health, and wildlife health are not three separate problems; they are three faces of a single planetary immune system. Scientists call this One Health.

Why does destroying terrestrial biodiversity actually increase the risk of disease outbreaks? In our multi-year field study across {{ argument_landscape_gradient_site }} -- comparing intact diverse forests against fragmented, species-poor agricultural patches -- we documented a powerful ecological mechanism known as the dilution effect.

In an intact, healthy forest with {{ argument_intact_species_richness_statistic }} mammal and bird species, a disease vector like a tick or mosquito takes blood meals from dozens of different hosts -- {{ argument_dilution_host_species_list }} -- most of which are dead-end hosts that cannot efficiently replicate or transmit the pathogen {{ argument_vector_borne_pathogen_name }}. And apex predators like {{ argument_native_predator_species_list }} keep the populations of small rodents tightly in check.

What happens when you fragment that forest into small patches? The sensitive specialists and large predators vanish first. Who thrives in degraded, trash-strewn forest edges? Fast-breeding, generalist species like {{ argument_reservoir_rodent_species }} -- the exact hyper-reservoirs that amplify pathogens at ten times the rate of other species.

In fragmented plots where predator diversity had been wiped out, the infection prevalence of {{ argument_vector_borne_pathogen_name }} in vectors jumped by {{ argument_infection_increase_percentage }}.

Intact biodiversity acts as a natural vaccine firewall; when we simplify ecosystems, we strip away the shield and leave only the super-spreaders standing.

Until recently, global health spending operated almost entirely like a fire department that only shows up after the whole city is already burning -- spending trillions of dollars after a pandemic hits while investing pennies in preventing spillover at the forest edge.

Through the {{ argument_one_health_consortium_name }}, our field teams of wildlife vets, indigenous forest rangers, and community health workers in {{ argument_hotspot_regions_list }} are building an early-warning immune system directly at high-risk interfaces.

When rangers on patrol in {{ argument_protected_park_name }} encounter a wild animal carcass, they don't just walk past it. Wearing field PPE, they take rapid swab samples and test them using portable PCR units right at the ranger station.
