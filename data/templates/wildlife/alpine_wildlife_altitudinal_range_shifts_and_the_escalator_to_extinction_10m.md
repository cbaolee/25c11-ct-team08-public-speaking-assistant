At {{ argument_elevation_statistic }} above sea level in the {{ argument_mountain_range_name }}, the air holds half the oxygen of the coast, winter temperatures plunge to {{ argument_winter_temperature_statistic }}, and howling winds strip snow from the jagged granite talus. To us, this alpine zone feels like one of the most hostile environments on Earth.

Yet to the {{ argument_target_alpine_species }}, this freezing rock pile is the only home it can survive in. Equipped with {{ argument_cold_adaptation_traits_list }}, this creature is so finely tuned to extreme cold that if ambient temperatures rise above {{ argument_lethal_heat_threshold_statistic }} for just a few hours, it can die of acute overheating.

For thousands of years, the cold mountain peaks were their fortress; today, that fortress is turning into a trap.

When lowland terrestrial species face a warming climate, they have a straightforward option: they can migrate poleward across hundreds of kilometers of flat land. Mountain species do not have that luxury. They live on sky islands -- isolated peaks surrounded by a sea of warm valleys they cannot cross.

As mountain regions warm at {{ argument_mountain_warming_rate_statistic }} the global average, the only way for {{ argument_target_alpine_species }} to stay within their thermal comfort zone is to move uphill. Over the past {{ argument_survey_timespan_years }}, my team has resurveyed historical transects across {{ argument_mountain_range_name }} originally mapped by {{ argument_historical_naturalist_name }} in {{ argument_historical_survey_year }}.

We found that the lower elevation boundary of {{ argument_target_alpine_species }} has crept upward by {{ argument_upward_shift_rate_statistic }} per decade. Ecologists call this the escalator to extinction.

Because mountains are shaped like cones, every hundred meters a species climbs uphill, the available surface area of its habitat shrinks dramatically -- until eventually, you reach the summit and there is nowhere left to go but the sky.

The crisis on the peaks isn't just about summer heat; paradoxically, a warming climate is also causing {{ argument_target_alpine_species }} to freeze to death in winter.

In a healthy alpine winter, a deep snowpack of {{ argument_normal_snowpack_depth }} acts like an insulating igloo blanket over the boulder fields, keeping the subnivean spaces beneath the rocks at a steady {{ argument_subnivean_temperature_statistic }}. In recent years, however, winter precipitation in {{ argument_study_watershed_name }} has fallen as rain instead of snow, or melted early in {{ argument_early_melt_month }}. Without that protective snow blanket, bitter late-season cold snaps penetrate deep into the rocks, wiping out entire overwintering colonies of {{ argument_target_alpine_species }}.

Meanwhile, lowland competitors like {{ argument_encroaching_lowland_species }} are marching up the mountain slopes, bringing new parasites and outcompeting alpine specialists for {{ argument_alpine_food_resource }}.

Is all hope lost for wildlife living on the roof of the world? During our field expeditions in {{ argument_refugia_study_site }}, we discovered something surprising: even at relatively low elevations where {{ argument_target_alpine_species }} should have vanished decades ago, pockets of them were still thriving.

The secret lay beneath the boulders. Certain rock glaciers and deep talus slopes contain subterranean ice reservoirs that trap cold winter air and vent it slowly throughout the summer, keeping the rocks {{ argument_microclimate_cooling_difference }} cooler than the surrounding mountainside.

Using thermal infrared drones and temperature loggers, our team is now mapping these climate microrefugia across {{ argument_protected_mountain_network }}.

Instead of treating all mountain acreage equally, we are redirecting conservation resources to protect these cold-air oases from {{ argument_human_alpine_disturbances_list }}, while restoring high-altitude wetlands that buffer thermal extremes.

We cannot air-condition a mountain range, but by protecting nature's own thermal shelters while we fight to cut global emissions, we can give alpine wildlife a fighting chance to hold the high ground. Thank you.
