# Climbing the Escalator to Extinction

When lowland terrestrial species face a warming climate, they have a straightforward option: they can migrate poleward across hundreds of kilometers of flat land. Mountain species do not have that luxury. They live on **sky islands** -- isolated peaks surrounded by a sea of warm valleys they cannot cross.

As mountain regions warm at {{ mountain_warming_rate_statistic }} the global average, the only way for {{ target_alpine_species }} to stay within their thermal comfort zone is to move uphill. Over the past {{ survey_timespan_years }}, my team has resurveyed historical transects across {{ mountain_range_name }} originally mapped by {{ historical_naturalist_name }} in {{ historical_survey_year }}.

We found that the lower elevation boundary of {{ target_alpine_species }} has crept upward by {{ upward_shift_rate_statistic }} per decade. Ecologists call this the **escalator to extinction**.

> Because mountains are shaped like cones, every hundred meters a species climbs uphill, the available surface area of its habitat shrinks dramatically -- until eventually, you reach the summit and there is nowhere left to go but the sky.

# When the Snow Blanket Vanishes

The crisis on the peaks isn't just about summer heat; paradoxically, a warming climate is also causing {{ target_alpine_species }} to **freeze to death** in winter.

In a healthy alpine winter, a deep snowpack of {{ normal_snowpack_depth }} acts like an insulating igloo blanket over the boulder fields, keeping the subnivean spaces beneath the rocks at a steady {{ subnivean_temperature_statistic }}. In recent years, however, winter precipitation in {{ study_watershed_name }} has fallen as rain instead of snow, or melted early in {{ early_melt_month }}. Without that protective snow blanket, bitter late-season cold snaps penetrate deep into the rocks, wiping out entire overwintering colonies of {{ target_alpine_species }}.

Meanwhile, lowland competitors like {{ encroaching_lowland_species }} are marching up the mountain slopes, bringing new parasites and outcompeting alpine specialists for {{ alpine_food_resource }}.

# Mapping Microrefugia on the Peaks

Is all hope lost for wildlife living on the roof of the world? During our field expeditions in {{ refugia_study_site }}, we discovered something surprising: even at relatively low elevations where {{ target_alpine_species }} should have vanished decades ago, pockets of them were still thriving.

The secret lay beneath the boulders. Certain rock glaciers and deep talus slopes contain **subterranean ice reservoirs** that trap cold winter air and vent it slowly throughout the summer, keeping the rocks {{ microclimate_cooling_difference }} cooler than the surrounding mountainside.

# Protecting the Cold Oases

Using thermal infrared drones and temperature loggers, our team is now mapping these **climate microrefugia** across {{ protected_mountain_network }}.

Instead of treating all mountain acreage equally, we are redirecting conservation resources to protect these cold-air oases from {{ human_alpine_disturbances_list }}, while restoring high-altitude wetlands that buffer thermal extremes.

> We cannot air-condition a mountain range, but by protecting nature's own thermal shelters while we fight to cut global emissions, we can give alpine wildlife a fighting chance to hold the high ground. Thank you.
