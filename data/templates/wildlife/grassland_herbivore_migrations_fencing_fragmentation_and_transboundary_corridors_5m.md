We often think of great terrestrial migrations as relics of the Pleistocene, except in a few famous African parks. In reality, long-distance overland migrations of {{ argument_regional_ungulate_list }} used to occur across almost every continent on Earth.

Migration is not just how terrestrial herbivores find food; it is the circulatory system that pumps nutrients across entire continents.

Over the last {{ argument_fragmentation_timeframe }}, however, the great overland migrations of {{ argument_study_region_name }} have been quietly strangled. The weapon wasn't the rifle; it was barbed wire and linear infrastructure.

When we constructed {{ argument_railway_or_highway_name }} and erected {{ argument_fence_length_statistic }} of veterinary and agricultural fencing across {{ argument_border_zone_name }}, we severed an ancient ecological artery. How do animals know where to go across featureless plains? Using solar-powered GPS collars on {{ argument_collared_individual_count }} lead females, our research revealed that migration routes are cultural memories.

Young calves learn the exact mountain passes, river crossings, and mineral licks from their mothers. Once a herd is blocked for a single generation and the elders die off, that collective geographical memory is erased forever, even if you tear the fence down later.

To save the {{ argument_migrating_herbivore_species }} of {{ argument_study_region_name }}, we didn't need to buy millions of hectares of land. Because the herds only use a specific corridor for {{ argument_passage_duration_timeframe }} each year, we needed dynamic coexistence.

We replaced lethal wire with wildlife-friendly fencing, built highway underpasses, and created a compensation fund that pays pastoralists to lower their boundary gates during the {{ argument_seasonal_migration_month }} migration window.

Saving terrestrial migrations requires thinking at the scale of landscapes, not property lines. When we give hoofed animals the freedom to move, the entire grassland breathes again. Thank you.
