# Introduction: A Hundred-Year-Old Map

Picture a dark tropical beach in {{ nesting_beach_location }} at two in the morning. Under the moonlight, fifty tiny **{{ target_sea_turtle_species }}** hatchlings -- each no bigger than the palm of your hand -- burst out of the warm sand at the exact same moment and scramble toward the crashing waves.

The odds against them are heartbreaking. Out of every {{ hatchling_survival_denominator }} hatchlings that touch the water tonight, only **one** will survive the gulls, the crabs, and the big fish to reach adulthood.

Yet if that single lucky survivor makes it, it will swim {{ migration_distance_statistic }} across entire oceans for {{ maturity_wait_years }}, and then -- using a built-in magnetic compass we still barely understand -- swim right back to this **exact same stretch of sand** to lay her own eggs.

> {{ target_sea_turtle_species }} have been making this journey since the days of the dinosaurs; today, however, the beach they are returning to has changed.

# Hot Sand, Only Sisters

Here is a weird quirk of biology that most people don't know about {{ target_sea_turtle_species }}: whether an egg becomes a boy or a girl isn't decided by chromosomes when the egg is laid. It is decided by the **temperature of the sand** around the nest.

Cooler sand below {{ pivotal_temperature_statistic }} produces males. Hotter sand above that mark produces females. We have an easy rhyme for it on the beach: **"Hot chicks, cool dudes."**

Over the last {{ warming_trend_years }}, as summers in {{ nesting_beach_location }} have gotten hotter and hotter, our field surveys uncovered a shocking number: **{{ female_hatchling_percentage }} of all the baby turtles** hatching on our beaches are now female. If there are no males left in twenty years, the species simply cannot survive.

# Confusion on the Shore and Traps at Sea

And for the hatchlings that do make it out of the nest, a second trap awaits. Naturally, baby turtles find the ocean by crawling toward the brightest horizon -- the moon and stars reflecting off the water.

When resorts and streetlamps light up the beachfront behind the dunes in {{ coastal_town_name }}, the hatchlings get confused. They crawl inland toward the traffic and streetlights instead of the sea.

# Simple Fixes That Save Generations

The good news is that we don't need rocket science to fix this. Starting in {{ community_project_year }}, we teamed up with local villagers and hotel owners in {{ coastal_town_name }} to launch {{ turtle_program_name }}.

First, hotels switched to **turtle-friendly red lights** and drew their curtains after {{ night_curfew_hour }}. Second, our beach rangers started planting native shade trees like {{ coastal_shade_plant }} behind the high-tide line and gently watering the hottest nests, cooling the sand by just {{ sand_cooling_degrees }} -- enough to bring baby boy turtles back into the mix. And out on the water, local fishing boats installed simple escape hatches called **Turtle Excluder Devices** in their nets.

> Last season alone, over {{ saved_hatchlings_statistic }} healthy hatchlings made it safely into the surf of {{ nesting_beach_location }} -- both sisters and brothers.

When we shade the sand and dim the beach lights for a few months a year, we keep one of Earth's oldest marathon runners swimming strong. Thank you.
