Looking back, a few things made the biggest difference in getting the internship, and I think they apply to most of you.

First, a CV with real projects. Interviewers for internships know you don't have professional experience. What they look for is evidence that you can build things and learn. One or two projects that you can explain in depth are worth much more than a long list of technologies you've only touched briefly.

Second, strong fundamentals. Most internship interviews include questions on data structures, algorithms, object-oriented programming, databases, and sometimes basic networking. These are exactly the courses some students try to just pass. Don't. They come back in interviews.

And third, applying widely and early. Many companies open internship programs months before they start. Rejections are completely normal. Most people collect several before an offer arrives.

Now, what was the internship actually like?

The first week was mostly about setup and learning. I installed tools, got access to systems, read documentation, and tried to understand the codebase, which felt enormous. If you have the same feeling, it's completely normal. Nobody expects an intern to understand everything in the first week, or even the first month.

After that, I was given small, real tasks. Fixing a minor bug. Adding a field to a form. Writing a few tests. They seemed small, but each one taught me something important about how a real team works: how to create a branch, how to write a good commit message, how a pull request gets reviewed, and how code finally reaches production.

The lessons are what I'd most like you to keep. There are five.

Lesson one: real-world code is very different from school assignments. At school, you usually write small programs from scratch, alone, and you never touch them again after the deadline. In a company, you mostly read and change existing code that many people wrote over several years. So the skill of reading code, searching through it, and understanding it before changing it is far more important than I expected.

Lesson two: asking questions is a skill. At first, I was afraid of asking too many questions and looking stupid. So I sometimes stayed stuck for a whole day on something my mentor could have explained in five minutes. Later, I learned a better approach. Try on your own first, for a reasonable amount of time, maybe thirty minutes to an hour. Write down what you've tried. Then ask a specific question, like: "I'm trying to do this. I tried A and B, and I got this error. Could you point me in the right direction?" Mentors love questions like that, because they show effort and are easy to answer.

Lesson three: code review is a gift, not a judgment. My first pull request received a lot of comments. I was a bit shocked. But every comment taught me something: naming, error handling, edge cases, or team conventions. By the end of the internship, my pull requests received far fewer comments, and that was one of the clearest signs of my progress.

Lesson four: communication matters as much as code. My mentor didn't only care whether I finished tasks. They cared whether I gave updates, told them early when I was stuck, and explained what I'd done clearly in stand-up meetings. A short daily update, like "Yesterday I finished this, today I'm working on that, and I'm blocked on this," made a big difference to how much the team trusted me.

And lesson five: take notes about everything. Commands, system names, who to ask about what, and things you learned. In the first weeks, there's so much new information that you'll forget most of it otherwise. My notes became my personal guide, and I still use some of them.

Finally, some practical advice, depending on where you are.

If you haven't applied yet, start building one or two meaningful projects now, put them on GitHub with a clear README, and review your fundamentals before interview season.

If you already have an internship coming up, spend the time before it learning Git properly, practicing reading other people's code, for example an open-source project, and getting comfortable with the basics of the technology the company uses.

And during the internship, ask good questions, welcome feedback, communicate often, take notes, and treat every small task as a chance to learn how professional software is built.

In a few words: getting an internship is about real projects, strong fundamentals, and persistence. Doing well in one is less about being the best programmer, and more about being curious, communicative, reliable, and open to feedback. And the mistakes you'll make are part of the process.
