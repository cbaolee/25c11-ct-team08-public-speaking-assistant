I'm {{ argument_speaker_name }}, and {{ argument_speaker_background }} Today, I'll try to answer a question that students and junior professionals ask constantly: "Are IT certifications worth it? Should I spend my time and money on them?"

You've probably seen people with long lists of certificates on their professional profiles: cloud certifications, networking certifications, Scrum certifications, security certifications. And you might wonder: do employers really care? Or is it just decoration?

The answer is: it depends. Certifications can be very valuable in some situations, and almost useless in others. So today, I'd like to help you understand what certifications are, which types exist, when they truly help, when they don't, how to choose one, and how to prepare properly.

Let's start with the basics. What is an IT certification?

An IT certification is a credential, usually earned by passing an exam, that shows you've reached a certain level of knowledge in a specific technology or practice. Unlike a university degree, which covers a broad field over several years, a certification is narrow and focused. It says: "This person understands this specific platform, tool, or method, at this specific level."

There are three main types.

The first type is vendor-specific certifications. These are offered by technology companies for their own products. Examples include the cloud certifications from Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud, and networking certifications from Cisco, such as the well-known CCNA, the Cisco Certified Network Associate. These are very practical, because they're tied to tools that companies actually use.

The second type is vendor-neutral certifications. These cover concepts that apply across many products. For example, CompTIA offers certifications in IT support, networking, and security, and ISTQB offers certifications in software testing. They're useful for building broad foundations.

And the third type is process and methodology certifications, such as Scrum certifications for agile teams, or project management certifications like the PMP. These focus on how work is organized and managed, rather than on a specific technology.

Certifications also have levels. Many vendors follow a similar structure: a foundational or entry level, an associate level for practitioners, and a professional or expert level for experienced specialists. Some advanced certifications even require years of documented work experience before you can earn them. For example, the well-known CISSP security certification requires several years of relevant professional experience.

Now, the most important question: when are certifications really valuable?

Situation one: in cloud, networking, and security roles. In these fields, certifications are widely recognized and often appear in job postings. A cloud certification shows that you understand a platform's services, security model, and best practices. For networking roles, the CCNA has long been a respected entry point. And in security, certifications are often expected, especially as you become more senior.

Situation two: in IT service and outsourcing companies. Many technology vendors run partner programs, and a company's partner level often depends on how many certified professionals it employs. So for these companies, your certification has direct business value, and some will even pay for your exam.

Situation three: when you're switching careers or don't have a degree in IT. A certification can provide structured learning and a recognizable signal on your CV, which helps you get past the first screening.

Situation four: in organizations with formal requirements. Government agencies, banks, and large enterprises sometimes require specific certifications for certain roles or projects.

And situation five: as a structured way to learn. Even if no employer asks for it, preparing for a good certification can force you to learn a topic systematically, rather than in random pieces.

Now, when are certifications less valuable?

The clearest case is software development. For most developer roles, employers care much more about what you can build than about certificates. A strong portfolio of projects, contributions on GitHub, and good performance in technical interviews usually matter far more. A developer with ten certificates and no projects is much less convincing than one with no certificates and three excellent projects.

Certifications are also less valuable when they're collected without real experience. Interviewers can usually tell within a few questions whether someone really understands a topic or just memorized exam answers. And a certificate you can't back up with real skills can actually hurt your credibility.

A few words on the three examples in today's title.

Cloud certifications, such as those from AWS. Cloud skills are in high demand across many types of companies. An associate-level cloud certification, combined with hands-on projects where you actually deploy and manage something, can be a strong signal for cloud, DevOps, and back-end roles.

The CCNA. For anyone interested in networking or infrastructure, it provides a solid, practical foundation in how networks work: routing, switching, IP addressing, and security basics. Even outside pure networking roles, that knowledge is valuable for DevOps and security work.

And Scrum certifications. These show that you understand the Scrum framework: its roles, events, and principles. They're particularly relevant for people aiming to become Scrum Masters, product owners, or project managers, and some employers like seeing them. But a point worth making: knowing the rules of Scrum and leading a team well are two different things. Experience and soft skills matter enormously in these roles. A Scrum certificate is a good start, not a finish line.

So, how do you choose the right certification? Four questions.

The role comes before anything else: what role am I aiming for? Look at real job postings for that role in your target market. Which certifications appear repeatedly? That's your best evidence.

Then the level: what level am I at? Foundational or associate levels are the sensible entry point. Jumping straight to an expert certification without experience usually leads to frustration.

Then the cost: what's the total cost? Consider not only the exam fee, but also training materials, practice exams, possible retakes, and your time. And check whether the certification expires. Many must be renewed every few years, which means ongoing time and cost.

And support: will my employer support it? Many companies pay for certifications, especially if they're relevant to client projects. Always ask.

To make this practical, let's look at three people, and decide together whether a certification makes sense for each of them.

The first is Hoa, a third-year student who wants to become a front-end developer. She's considering a cloud certification because a friend said "certificates look good on a CV." My advice: probably not right now. For front-end roles, her time is much better spent building two or three strong projects. A cloud certification wouldn't hurt, but it's unlikely to be what gets her hired. Later, if she moves toward full-stack or DevOps work, it could become valuable.

The second is Nam, who has worked in IT support for two years and wants to move into cloud engineering. For him, an associate-level cloud certification is an excellent idea. It gives his learning a clear structure, it's directly relevant to his target role, and it signals to employers that he's serious about the transition. The key is to combine it with hands-on projects: deploying a real application, setting up networking and security, and documenting what he built.

The third is Linh, a business analyst who's often asked to facilitate her team's sprint meetings, and who wants to become a Scrum Master. A Scrum certification makes sense for her. It will deepen her understanding of the framework and adds a recognizable credential. But what will really make her successful is the experience she's already gaining, facilitating meetings, removing obstacles, and helping the team improve. In interviews, those stories will matter more than the certificate itself.

Notice the pattern. The same certification can be a great investment for one person and a distraction for another. The decision always starts with the role you're aiming for.

Now, a word on how to prepare properly.

The right way is to combine study with hands-on practice. For a cloud certification, create a free-tier account and actually build things. For a networking certification, use simulation tools or lab equipment. For Scrum, try applying the framework, even in a student project or a personal project.

And a warning about exam dumps, which are collections of leaked real exam questions. Using them violates the rules of most certification programs, can lead to your certification being revoked, and, worst of all, gives you a certificate without the skills. When you're in an interview or facing a real production problem, the certificate won't help you. Only real understanding will.

Once you've earned a certification, how you present it matters. On your CV, list it with the year, but put it next to evidence: the project where you used those skills, the system you deployed, or the problem you solved. In interviews, don't just say "I'm AWS certified." Say something like: "While preparing for the certification, I deployed a small web application with a database, set up access control so only the right services could reach the data, and configured alerts for unusual costs. The trickiest part was networking, and here's what I learned." That turns a line on your CV into a conversation about real skills, which is exactly what interviewers want.

And plan for the long term. Technology moves, and so do certifications. Many expire after two or three years, and the exam content changes as the platforms change. Rather than collecting a new certificate every few months, it's usually better to go deeper in one area, keep your main certification current, and let your projects show the rest.

Where does that leave us? Certifications are narrow, focused credentials that prove knowledge of a specific technology or practice. They're most valuable in cloud, networking, and security roles, in IT service companies, for career changers, and in organizations with formal requirements. They're less important for software developers, where portfolios matter more, and they're never a substitute for real experience. Choose certifications based on real job postings and your current level, consider the total cost and renewal requirements, and prepare with hands-on practice, never with shortcuts.

Think of a certification as a key that can help open a door. But once the door is open, it's your skills that keep you in the room.
