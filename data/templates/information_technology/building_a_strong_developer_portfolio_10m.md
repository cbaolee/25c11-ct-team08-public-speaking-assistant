Let's sit on the other side of the hiring table for a moment. When a company posts a junior developer position, it may receive dozens or even hundreds of applications. Many of them look very similar: the same degree, the same courses, the same list of technologies. A recruiter or engineer reviewing them often has only a minute or two for each one. So what makes someone stand out? Very often, it's a link to a project that clearly shows what they can do.

A portfolio is evidence. Your CV says, "I know React." Your portfolio shows a working application built with React, the code behind it, and how you think. That's far more convincing.

So, let's look at how to build one that really works. I'll cover what to include, how to choose projects, how to present each project, what reviewers look for in your code, and the most common mistakes.

First, what should a portfolio include?

At its core, a developer portfolio usually has three parts. A GitHub profile, or a similar platform, with your code. Three to five strong projects, with live demos whenever possible. And a simple personal page that brings everything together: a short introduction, your skills, your projects, and how to contact you. This page doesn't need to be fancy, but it should be clean, fast, and work well on a phone.

Second, how do you choose your projects?

The most important rule: quality over quantity. Three excellent projects are much better than fifteen small ones that were copied from tutorials. Reviewers see the same tutorial projects again and again: the same to-do list, the same weather app, the same clone of a famous website. Those projects show that you can follow instructions, but not that you can solve problems on your own.

So, aim for projects with these qualities.

They solve a real problem, ideally one you or someone you know actually has. For example, a booking tool for a small business run by a relative, an app that helps your club track attendance, or a tool that automates a boring task in your part-time job. Real problems lead to real decisions, and real decisions give you great stories for interviews.

They show depth. One project should go beyond the basics: user authentication, a database, handling errors properly, tests, and deployment. This shows that you understand what it takes to build something complete.

They show some range, but stay focused on the role you want. If you're applying for front-end roles, most projects should showcase front-end skills. If you're aiming for back-end, focus on APIs, databases, and system design.

And they're finished. A half-built project with a note saying "coming soon" often does more harm than good. It's better to have a smaller project that's complete and polished.

Third, how should you present each project?

This is where many developers lose points. They build something good, and then present it with no explanation. So for every project, write a clear README, the description file that appears on your repository's main page. A strong README answers these questions, in this order.

What is this project, in one or two sentences, and what problem does it solve?

Where can I see it? Include a link to a live demo and a few screenshots or a short animated preview.

What technologies did you use, and why did you choose them?

How do I run it locally? Give clear, tested setup steps.

What were the main challenges, and how did you solve them? This is the most underrated section. One paragraph about a difficult bug, a performance problem, or a design trade-off tells reviewers more about you than anything else.

And what would you improve next? This shows that you think critically about your own work.

Fourth, what do reviewers look for when they open your code?

They often look at a few quick signals. Is the project well organized, with a clear folder structure? Is the code readable, with meaningful names? Are there tests, even a few? Is there a sensible commit history, with clear commit messages over time, rather than one giant commit called "final"? Are secrets, such as passwords and API keys, kept out of the code? And does the live demo actually work? A broken demo link is one of the fastest ways to lose a reviewer's interest.

A simple way to check whether your portfolio works is the thirty-second test. Ask a friend who doesn't know your projects to open your portfolio, and give them just thirty seconds. Then ask: what kind of developer is this person? What's their best project? Did anything break or confuse you? If they can't answer the first two questions, your portfolio needs clearer focus. If they mention something broken or confusing, fix that first. It's a simple test, but it closely mimics what a busy recruiter actually does.

And a quick note for those of you who aren't aiming to be developers. Portfolios matter in other IT roles too, just in a different form. A data analyst's portfolio might be a set of dashboards and short written analyses of public data. A UX designer's portfolio is a set of case studies showing the research, the design process, and the final result. A QA tester's portfolio might include test plans, well-written bug reports for open-source projects, and a small automated test suite. Whatever your role, the principle is the same: show your work, and explain your thinking.

Fifth, the red flags reviewers see most often.

Red flag one: only tutorial projects. If you followed a tutorial, extend the project significantly with your own features, and say clearly in the README what you added.

Red flag two: too many unfinished repositories. You can archive or make private the experiments you don't want reviewers to see. Pin your best projects to the top of your profile.

Red flag three: no explanations. Code without context makes reviewers do all the work. Most won't.

Red flag four: never updating. Your portfolio should grow with you. When you build something better, replace weaker projects.

And red flag five: hiding teamwork. If you built something with classmates, include it, but be honest and specific about which parts you did. Teamwork is a valuable skill to show.

Finally, connect your portfolio to everything else. Put the link at the top of your CV and your professional profile. Mention specific projects in your cover letters, especially ones related to the company's field. And in interviews, be ready to walk through any project in detail: why you built it, how it works, what went wrong, and what you learned. Many interviews today start with exactly that conversation.

To compress it: a portfolio is evidence of what you can do. Include a code profile, three to five strong projects, and a simple personal page. Choose projects that solve real problems, show depth, and match the role you want. Present each one with a clear README, including challenges and next steps. Pay attention to code quality signals and working demos. And keep it updated as you grow.

If you don't have a portfolio yet, your challenge is simple: choose one small, real problem this week, and start building. In three months, you could have the project that gets you your first interview.
