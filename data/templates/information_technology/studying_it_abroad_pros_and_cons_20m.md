I'm sharing this because, when people talk about studying abroad, they often show only the beautiful photos: the campus, the snow, the new friends, the graduation ceremony. And those moments are real. But so are the lonely evenings, the money worries, and the moments when you wonder if you made the right choice. My goal today is to give you an honest picture, both the advantages and the challenges, then a way to compare destinations, and finally practical guidance on how to decide, how to prepare, how to survive your first months, and how to make the most of it if you go.

Let's start with the reasons to go.

Advantage one: the quality and style of education.

Many universities abroad, especially well-known ones, offer excellent IT programs with modern labs, strong research groups, and lecturers who are active in industry and research. But often, the bigger difference is the style of learning. In many countries, you're expected to ask questions, challenge ideas, work on open-ended projects, and manage your own learning. For some students, this is a shock at first. But it builds independence and critical thinking, which are exactly the skills the IT industry values most.

Advantage two: English, and communication across cultures.

In IT, English is the working language. Documentation, tools, conferences, and international teams all use it. Living and studying in an English-speaking environment, or even in an English-taught program in a non-English-speaking country, improves your English much faster than classes alone. And you learn something just as valuable: how to work with people from very different backgrounds, which is exactly what happens in global tech companies.

Advantage three: an international network.

Your classmates, lecturers, and internship colleagues may later become your colleagues, co-founders, or references, spread across many countries. In a global industry like IT, that network can open doors for decades.

Advantage four: career opportunities.

Studying abroad can give you access to internships and jobs at international companies, both in the country where you study and back home. Many countries offer some form of post-study work permission for international graduates, which lets you gain work experience after graduation. Those rules differ by country and change frequently, so always check the latest official government information rather than relying on what a friend heard a few years ago.

And advantage five: personal growth.

Living on your own in a foreign country forces you to grow up quickly: managing money, solving problems without your family nearby, adapting to new rules and habits, and becoming comfortable with uncertainty. Many people say this is the most valuable thing they took away, even more than the degree itself.

Now, the challenges.

Challenge one: cost.

This is often the biggest one. Tuition for international students can be very high in some countries, and living costs in major cities can be even higher. When you calculate, include everything: tuition, accommodation, food, transport, health insurance, books, visa fees, flights, and an emergency fund. And calculate for the whole program, not just the first year, because tuition and living costs usually rise over time.

The good news is that costs vary enormously between countries. Some countries, like Germany, charge little or no tuition at many public universities, although you still need to cover living costs. Others offer generous scholarships. So don't rule out studying abroad before you've researched carefully.

Challenge two: loneliness and culture shock.

Even the most confident people go through hard moments: missing family and food from home, feeling like an outsider, struggling with a new language in everyday life, or simply being tired of always having to explain yourself. Culture shock is real, and it often comes in waves. It helps to know this in advance, so you don't think something is wrong with you when it happens.

Challenge three: academic adjustment.

Many students are surprised by how different studying is. There's often more self-study, more group work, more presentations, and stricter rules on academic integrity. Copying even a few sentences without proper citation, or sharing code inappropriately, can lead to serious penalties. Learning these rules early is essential.

Challenge four: legal and work restrictions.

Student visas usually come with conditions, such as limits on how many hours you can work per week during the semester, requirements to maintain full-time study, and rules about what happens after graduation. Breaking these rules, even accidentally, can have serious consequences. And part-time work, while helpful, usually won't cover all your costs.

Challenge five: the job market for international graduates.

Finding a job abroad as an international graduate can be harder than many expect. Some employers are reluctant to sponsor work visas, and competition can be intense. It's possible, and many people succeed, but it requires planning from your first year: internships, networking, and strong projects.

And challenge six: the long-term question of staying or returning.

Some graduates build careers abroad. Others return home and bring valuable international experience. Both are valid choices, but it's worth thinking about your long-term goals and your family situation early, because they affect where you should study and what kind of experience you should aim for.

Before we talk about how to decide, let me say a few words about choosing a destination, because "abroad" can mean very different experiences.

Rather than telling you which country is best, I'd suggest comparing destinations on five factors.

The first is language. Studying in an English-speaking country, such as the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, or New Zealand, is the most direct way to strengthen your English. But many universities in countries like Germany, the Netherlands, Japan, South Korea, or Taiwan now offer IT programs taught in English. Keep in mind, though, that daily life, part-time jobs, and many local employers may still require the local language. If you plan to work there after graduation, learning that language is a serious advantage.

The second is cost, which we've already discussed. It varies enormously between countries, and between cities within the same country.

The third is the structure of the programs. For example, in the United Kingdom, many master's programs take just one year, which reduces cost but leaves less time for internships. In the United States, bachelor's programs are often more flexible in the first years, with general education courses before you specialize. And some universities, especially in Canada, are known for co-op programs, where you alternate between study terms and paid work terms with real companies.

The fourth is the local tech industry. Studying in or near a strong tech hub makes it much easier to find internships, attend meetups, and build connections. Ask yourself: which companies are nearby, and do they hire international students?

And the fifth is post-study work and long-term options. As I mentioned, rules for staying and working after graduation differ by country and change over time. If working abroad after graduation matters to you, research this carefully, using official government sources, before you choose.

There's no perfect country. Each one is a different combination of these factors. The best destination is the one where that combination fits your goals and your budget.

So, how do you decide whether studying abroad is right for you? Five questions can help.

Motive: why do you want to go? If the answer is "everyone else is going" or "it sounds exciting," dig deeper. Strong reasons might be a specific program, a field that's more advanced abroad, a clear career goal, or a desire to build an international career.

Money: what can you and your family realistically afford, including a safety margin? And what scholarships can reduce that cost?

Readiness: are you ready to be independent, and to manage stress far from home?

Level: should you go for a bachelor's degree, or wait for a master's? Going for a bachelor's gives you a longer, deeper experience abroad, but it's usually more expensive and you're younger when you leave. Many people choose to complete their bachelor's degree at home, gain a few years of work experience, and then go abroad for a master's, often with a scholarship. That path can be more affordable and more focused, because you already know what you want to specialize in.

And alternatives: are there options that give you some of the benefits at a lower cost? For example, joint programs where you study the first part at home and the second part abroad, exchange semesters, summer schools, or international programs taught in English at home.

If you decide to go, here's a practical preparation timeline. Ideally, start about eighteen months to two years before your intended start date.

First, research. Shortlist countries and programs. Look at the curriculum, not just the ranking. Check whether the program offers internships or co-op placements, which combine study with paid work, and look at what graduates actually do.

Second, tests. Most programs require an English test, such as IELTS or TOEFL. Some undergraduate programs in certain countries require tests like the SAT, and some graduate programs ask for the GRE. Give yourself time to retake a test if needed.

Third, scholarships. Many scholarship deadlines are much earlier than university application deadlines. There are government scholarships, such as Fulbright for the United States, Chevening for the United Kingdom, Australia Awards, DAAD scholarships for Germany, and MEXT scholarships for Japan, mostly for graduate study, as well as scholarships offered directly by universities. Each has its own requirements, so read them carefully.

Fourth, documents and applications: transcripts, recommendation letters, a personal statement explaining who you are and why this program fits you, and often a CV or portfolio. For IT programs, a portfolio of projects, for example on GitHub, can really strengthen your application.

And fifth, visa and logistics: financial proof, accommodation, health insurance, and flights. Visa processing can take weeks or months, so don't leave it to the last minute.

And once you're there, here are a few pieces of advice from my own experience. {{ argument_speaker_practical_advice }}

Beyond that, a few things apply to almost everyone. Start building your network and looking for internships from your first year, not your final year. Use the university's career services, international student office, and counseling services. Join clubs and communities, both international and local, not only groups from your home country. Track your budget carefully. And take care of your mental health. Asking for help is a sign of strength, not weakness.

Next, a survival guide for your first ninety days, because the beginning is usually the hardest part.

In your first week, focus on the essentials: register with the university, complete any visa or residence registration required by law, open a local bank account, get a local phone number, and understand your health insurance and where to go if you get sick. Keep copies of all your important documents, both printed and digital.

In your first month, focus on routines and support. Find out where the international student office, the career center, and the counseling service are. Learn the academic rules, especially about plagiarism and group work. Set up a simple budget and track your spending for the first few weeks; many students are shocked by how quickly small daily costs add up. And make at least a few friends, from your course, your accommodation, or a club. You don't need many. You need a few people you can call when something goes wrong.

By the end of your first three months, aim to feel settled in your studies. If you're struggling with a course, talk to the lecturer or a teaching assistant early, not the week before the exam. And start thinking about your career: attend a career fair, update your CV to the local format, and create a professional profile online.

And five mistakes that many students make.

The first is staying only within your own community. It's natural and comforting to spend time with people from home, but if that's all you do, you miss much of the value of studying abroad.

The second is waiting until the final year to think about jobs. By then, many internship deadlines have passed.

The third is underestimating costs, and then working too many hours to make up for it, which hurts grades and may break visa rules.

The fourth is hiding problems. Whether it's an academic issue, money, or mental health, problems usually grow when we hide them. Universities have support services precisely because these problems are common.

And the fifth is comparing yourself to others on social media. Everyone posts their best moments. Nobody posts the night they cried because they missed home. Your journey is your own.

The big picture: studying IT abroad can give you high-quality education, strong English, an international network, broader career opportunities, and enormous personal growth. But it also comes with real challenges: high costs, loneliness and culture shock, academic adjustment, legal restrictions, and a competitive job market. Whether it's right for you depends on your goals, your finances, and your readiness. If you decide to go, start early, research carefully, apply for scholarships, and plan your career from day one.

And if you decide not to go, or not yet, that's perfectly fine too. Many outstanding IT professionals built world-class careers without ever studying abroad. What matters most is not where you study, but how much you learn and grow.

Ask me anything, including the parts of studying abroad that never make it into the photos.
