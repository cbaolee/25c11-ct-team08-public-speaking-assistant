Part one: where does IT's footprint come from?

It's useful to divide the environmental footprint of digital technology into three main components.

The first is end-user devices: smartphones, laptops, desktops, tablets, televisions, and all the connected gadgets in our homes. For many of these devices, a large share of their lifetime greenhouse gas emissions comes not from using them, but from manufacturing them: mining raw materials, producing chips and screens, assembling, and transporting them. This is called "embodied carbon," and for devices like smartphones and laptops, life-cycle assessments published by manufacturers themselves often show that manufacturing accounts for the majority of their total emissions.

The second is data centers: the facilities that host servers and storage for websites, apps, cloud services, and AI. They consume electricity to run the IT equipment, and a significant additional amount to cool it.

And the third is networks: the infrastructure that moves data between devices and data centers, including mobile networks, fiber optic cables, and the equipment that connects them.

How big is the total? Estimates vary widely, because they depend on what's included and which methods are used. Many studies place the information and communication technology sector's share of global greenhouse gas emissions at a few percent, broadly comparable to commonly cited figures for the aviation industry. The exact number is debated, but there's broad agreement on two points: the footprint is significant, and the demand for digital services, especially driven by AI, is growing quickly. The International Energy Agency, for example, has projected substantial growth in electricity consumption from data centers in the coming years, with AI as a major driver.

And devices depend on minerals such as cobalt, lithium, and rare earth elements, whose extraction can involve serious environmental damage and, in some places, human rights concerns.

So, Green IT isn't only about carbon emissions. It's about energy, water, materials, waste, and the people affected along the entire supply chain.

Part two: devices and electronic waste.

According to the Global E-waste Monitor 2024, a report produced by the United Nations Institute for Training and Research and the International Telecommunication Union, the world generated about 62 million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022. That's one of the fastest-growing waste streams in the world. And less than a quarter of it was documented as being properly collected and recycled. The rest was thrown away with ordinary waste, stored in drawers and cupboards, or handled informally, often in conditions that expose workers, including children, to toxic substances such as lead and mercury.

At the same time, e-waste contains valuable materials, including gold, copper, and rare metals. When it's not recycled, those resources are lost, and more mining is needed to replace them.

What does this mean in practice? Because so much of a device's footprint comes from manufacturing, the single most effective thing we can usually do is simple: use devices for longer.

A concrete case: imagine a company with five hundred employees that replaces every laptop every three years. If it extends that cycle to four or five years, by upgrading batteries, adding memory, and repairing rather than replacing, it buys significantly fewer new laptops over a decade, avoiding all the manufacturing emissions, materials, and waste those laptops would have created. It also saves money. The same logic applies to your personal phone: keeping it for an extra year or two, with a new battery if needed, is one of the most effective environmental choices a typical person can make in their digital life.

The main strategies for devices apply to individuals and organizations alike.

First, buy less, and buy better. Choose devices that are durable, repairable, and supported with software updates for many years. Software support matters: a perfectly good device becomes unusable, or unsafe, when it stops receiving security updates.

Second, repair and upgrade. The "right to repair" movement has gained strength in recent years, and the European Union has adopted rules aimed at making products easier to repair, including requirements for spare parts and repair information for certain product categories. Repairability is increasingly something consumers and organizations can consider when buying.

Third, look at refurbished devices. For many uses, a professionally refurbished laptop or phone works just as well as a new one, at a lower cost and a much lower environmental impact.

Fourth, when a device truly reaches the end of its life, make sure it goes to a certified e-waste recycler, after securely erasing its data. Don't let it sit in a drawer for years, and never throw it in the ordinary trash.

And fifth, for organizations: build these into policy. Set longer default replacement cycles, create internal processes for redeploying older devices to roles that need less power, and include repairability, energy efficiency, and take-back programs in purchasing criteria.

A word about purchasing, because for most organizations, buying decisions are where the biggest environmental impact of IT is decided, often by people who never think of themselves as working on sustainability.

When an organization buys laptops, monitors, or servers, the traditional question is: "Which one is cheapest?" A better question is: "Which one has the lowest total cost over its whole life?" That includes energy use, repairability, expected lifetime, software support, and what happens at the end of its life. A slightly more expensive laptop that lasts five years instead of three, and can have its battery replaced, is often cheaper overall, as well as greener.

There are also independent ecolabels and certifications that can help, such as EPEAT, TCO Certified, and ENERGY STAR, which assess aspects like energy efficiency, hazardous substances, repairability, and responsible manufacturing, depending on the label. They're not perfect, but they give purchasing teams a practical starting point, instead of having to evaluate every product from scratch.

And for servers and network equipment, organizations can ask suppliers about take-back programs, refurbished options, and the expected support lifetime of the equipment, so that hardware isn't replaced simply because its software support ended.

The human side of devices matters too, because sustainability isn't only environmental. It's also social.

Our devices depend on minerals that are often mined in difficult conditions. Cobalt, used in lithium-ion batteries, is a well-known example: a large share of the world's supply comes from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where some of it is produced by small-scale, informal mining, and human rights organizations have documented dangerous working conditions and child labor in parts of that sector. Tin, tantalum, tungsten, and gold, which are also used in electronics, have been linked in some regions to the financing of armed conflict, which is why they're often called "conflict minerals."

In response, several governments have introduced rules requiring companies to examine their supply chains. In the United States, a provision of the Dodd-Frank Act in 2010 required certain companies to report on their use of conflict minerals, and the European Union has a regulation on conflict minerals that has applied since 2021, requiring importers to carry out due diligence. Many electronics manufacturers now publish information about their mineral sourcing.

As individuals, we can't personally audit a supply chain. But we can recognize that every device we buy has a story that begins long before it reaches the store. And once again, it points to the same conclusion: keeping devices longer, and recycling them properly so their materials can be recovered, reduces the demand for new mining, with all its environmental and human costs.

Software developers have a role here, too, which is often overlooked. When apps and websites become heavier and heavier, they force people to buy new devices just to run them smoothly. Building software that runs well on older and cheaper devices is both an accessibility issue and a sustainability issue.

Part three: data centers and infrastructure.

Let's move from our pockets to the buildings that power the cloud.

A data center's electricity goes to two main places: the IT equipment itself, meaning servers, storage, and network equipment, and the supporting infrastructure, mainly cooling, but also power conversion and lighting.

To measure how efficiently a data center uses energy, the industry commonly uses a metric called Power Usage Effectiveness, or PUE, introduced by an industry group called The Green Grid in 2007. PUE is calculated by dividing the total energy used by the facility by the energy used by the IT equipment alone. A PUE of 2.0 means that for every unit of energy used for computing, another full unit is used for cooling and other overhead. A PUE of 1.0 would be perfect, with no overhead at all. Many older or smaller facilities operate well above 1.5, while the most efficient large-scale data centers report values much closer to 1.0.

PUE is useful, but it has an important limitation that I want you to remember: it measures the efficiency of the building, not the efficiency of the computing. A data center with an excellent PUE can still be full of servers that are mostly idle, running inefficient software. So PUE tells you about the overhead, not about whether the work itself was necessary or efficient.

Greener data centers rely on a few strategies.

Efficient cooling is the classic lever. Techniques include separating hot and cold air flows, using outside air for cooling when the climate allows it, often called "free cooling," and liquid cooling, which is becoming more common for the very dense, high-power servers used for AI.

High utilization matters just as much. An idle server still consumes a significant share of its peak power. Virtualization, containers, and cloud computing allow many workloads to share fewer physical machines, which raises utilization and reduces the number of servers needed. This is one reason why moving from many small, underused on-premises server rooms to well-run, large-scale facilities can reduce energy use, although the actual benefit depends on how the cloud resources are then used.

Then there's clean energy. The carbon footprint of a data center depends enormously on the electricity that powers it. The same computation can have a very different footprint depending on whether the grid is powered mainly by coal or by renewables and other low-carbon sources. Many large technology companies have made commitments to renewable or carbon-free electricity. It's worth understanding the difference between two approaches: buying enough renewable energy over a year to match total consumption on paper, and the more demanding goal of matching consumption with carbon-free energy every hour, in the same grid region, which better reflects the actual impact on the grid.

And water awareness is growing in importance. Some cooling methods use large amounts of water. In regions facing water scarcity, data center designers increasingly have to balance energy efficiency against water use, and some operators now report water usage effectiveness alongside PUE.

Heat reuse completes the picture. Servers turn almost all the electricity they consume into heat. In some places, particularly in Nordic countries, that heat is captured and fed into district heating networks that warm homes and buildings. It's a powerful example of turning waste into a resource, although it requires the right location and infrastructure.

For those of you who don't run data centers, here's what this means. When you choose a cloud provider or a region, you can look at its energy mix and its published sustainability data. And whatever infrastructure you use, the most effective step is still to use less of it: fewer idle servers, right-sized machines, and less unnecessary data.

Before we leave infrastructure, a word about networks, the third component of IT's footprint, because they're often forgotten.

Over the past decades, the energy needed to transmit each unit of data has fallen dramatically, thanks to better equipment and technologies such as fiber optics. That's genuinely good news. But the total amount of data we send has grown enormously, and video makes up a large share of all internet traffic. So, once again, efficiency per unit has improved, while total usage has exploded.

There are also differences between types of connections. Fixed broadband, especially fiber, is generally more energy-efficient per unit of data than mobile networks, although mobile networks have also become much more efficient with each new generation. And a large part of network energy is used just to keep the network running, whether or not data is flowing, which means that the impact of one extra video or download is complicated to estimate, and simple claims like "one email equals so many grams of carbon" should be treated with caution.

The practical lesson is not that you should feel guilty about every message you send. It's that at the scale of systems and organizations, the amount of data we move matters. Applications that send less data, compress it well, and avoid unnecessary transfers reduce load on networks, devices, and data centers all at once.

Part four: green software.

Now we come to the area where many people in this room have the most direct influence: the software we write.

For a long time, software developers didn't need to think much about energy. Hardware kept getting faster and cheaper, so inefficient code was often "good enough." But software decides how much hardware we need, how hard it works, and for how long. Every unnecessary computation, every redundant data transfer, and every idle service running around the clock translates into real electricity, and real emissions.

In 2021, a group of organizations founded the Green Software Foundation, with the goal of building a community and standards for sustainable software. It promotes a simple way of thinking about green software, built on three ideas.

The first is energy efficiency: use the least amount of energy possible to do the work.

The second is hardware efficiency: use the least amount of physical hardware possible, because, as we discussed, manufacturing hardware has a large embodied footprint. Software that keeps servers busy doing useful work, and that runs well on older devices, reduces the need for new hardware.

And the third is carbon awareness: do more when electricity is cleaner, and less when it's dirtier. The carbon intensity of electricity, meaning how much carbon dioxide is emitted per unit of electricity, changes depending on the time of day, the weather, and the location. On a sunny, windy afternoon, a grid may be powered largely by renewables. On a still evening, it may rely more on fossil fuels. Software that can be flexible about when or where it runs can take advantage of that.

The foundation has also developed a specification called the Software Carbon Intensity, or SCI, which is a method for calculating the carbon emissions of a piece of software per unit of useful work, for example per user, per transaction, or per API call. The idea of measuring "per unit of work" is important, because it allows a growing product to show that it's becoming more efficient, even as total usage grows.

So what does green software look like in practice? The main techniques, from the most common to the more advanced.

Efficient code and algorithms. This is computer science at its most practical. Choosing an algorithm with better complexity, avoiding repeated work, and using efficient data structures can reduce computation dramatically. A classic example is the database query in a loop: fetching data one record at a time, a thousand times, instead of once with a single, well-designed query. It's slow for the user, and it wastes energy on every request.

Caching. If the same result is requested again and again, compute it once and store it. Many applications recompute or re-fetch the same data thousands of times a day.

Right-sizing and autoscaling. Many applications run on servers far larger than they need, "just in case." Choosing appropriately sized resources, and scaling automatically up when demand rises and down when it falls, can significantly cut both energy use and costs.

Turning things off. Development and test environments, demo systems, and forgotten experiments often run around the clock, even when nobody uses them at night or on weekends. Scheduling them to shut down outside working hours is one of the easiest wins available.

Managing data. Storing data costs energy, and backing it up multiplies that. Many organizations keep enormous amounts of data that nobody will ever use again: old logs, duplicate files, and abandoned datasets. Setting data retention policies, deleting what's not needed, and moving rarely used data to lower-energy storage tiers all help.

Reducing data transfer. Every byte sent over a network uses energy, on servers, in the network, and on the user's device. Compressing images and videos, loading content only when needed, and avoiding unnecessary scripts and tracking tools make websites and apps lighter. And lighter applications also load faster and work better on slow connections and older devices, which helps users everywhere.

And carbon-aware scheduling. For work that doesn't need to happen immediately, such as large batch jobs, data processing, backups, or training machine learning models, you can schedule it for times or regions where electricity is cleaner. Several tools and services now provide information on grid carbon intensity, which makes this increasingly practical.

An example shows how these ideas add up.

Imagine a university's online learning platform. Every night, it generates detailed reports for every course, for thousands of courses, whether or not anyone will read them. It stores every version of every report forever. Its video lectures are uploaded in the highest possible quality, and streamed at that quality to every student, including those watching on small phone screens with weak connections. Its test servers run all the time. And its main servers are sized for the busiest day of the exam period, all year round.

Now imagine a review of this platform with green software in mind. The team generates reports only for courses where an instructor has requested them, and only when data has changed. They keep reports for one academic year, and archive older ones. They encode videos at several quality levels, and stream the one that fits each student's screen and connection. They schedule test servers to turn off at night and on weekends. And they use autoscaling, so that extra capacity is added during exam periods and removed afterward.

No single change is dramatic. But together, they can substantially reduce the computing, storage, and network resources the platform needs. And notice something important: almost every one of these changes also saves money, and many improve the experience for users, especially those with older phones or weak internet connections. That's a pattern you'll see again and again. In software, sustainability and good engineering usually point in the same direction.

Since so many of you build websites and web applications, a bit more on sustainable web design, because it's one of the areas where individual developers can make a visible difference.

Over the past decade or so, the average web page has become much heavier. Data collected by projects such as the HTTP Archive, which regularly analyzes millions of websites, shows that typical page sizes have grown substantially, driven by larger images, more JavaScript, web fonts, videos, and third-party scripts for advertising and analytics. Every visitor downloads all of that, again and again.

A practical checklist for lighter websites: Optimize images: use modern formats, the right dimensions for each screen, and compression. Load images and videos only when they're about to appear on screen, which is called lazy loading. Avoid autoplaying videos. Question every third-party script: each analytics tool, chat widget, or tracking pixel adds weight and often slows down the page. Limit custom fonts to what you really need. Use caching so that returning visitors don't download the same files again. And regularly measure your page weight and performance with standard tools, so that it doesn't grow quietly over time.

The beautiful thing about this checklist is that it's the same as a checklist for a fast, accessible website. Faster pages rank better in search engines, convert better, and work better for users with older phones or expensive mobile data. So when you make a website lighter, you're helping the planet, your users, and your business at the same time.

There's one more software practice I want to highlight, because it connects software directly to the device problem we discussed earlier: designing software for longevity.

When an app stops supporting older operating system versions, or becomes too heavy to run smoothly on older phones, users are effectively pushed to buy new devices, even when their current ones work perfectly well. Sometimes that's unavoidable, for example for security reasons. But often, it's the result of choices that nobody examined: adding a heavy library for a small feature, raising the minimum supported version because it's slightly more convenient, or never testing on older hardware.

If you build apps, keep these questions in mind as part of your normal process. What's the oldest device and operating system version that a meaningful number of our users still have? Do we test on it? When we raise our minimum requirements, do we know how many users we'll leave behind, and do we have a good reason? And are we measuring how our app's size and memory use change from version to version?

These questions also matter for inclusion. Users with older or cheaper devices are often those with lower incomes, in rural areas, or in developing countries. Software that works well for them is not only greener. It's fairer.

Part five: artificial intelligence and sustainability.

No discussion of Green IT today would be complete without AI, because it affects both sides of our topic: it increases the footprint of IT, and it can help reduce emissions elsewhere.

Let's start with the footprint. Training large AI models requires enormous amounts of computation, often running on thousands of specialized processors for weeks or months. But training is only part of the story. Once a model is deployed, it's used, potentially by millions of people, millions of times a day. This is called inference, and as AI becomes part of more and more products, the total energy used for inference can become larger than the energy used for training.

This is why the growth of AI has become a major factor in projections of data center energy demand, and why some technology companies have reported that their emissions or energy use have increased in recent years, partly because of AI, despite their sustainability commitments.

What can we do about it? The same principles of green software apply, with some AI-specific additions.

The simplest step is to use the right model for the task. A very large, general model is not always necessary. For many tasks, such as classification, extraction, or routing, a smaller or specialized model can perform well, using a fraction of the computation. A useful habit is to start with the smallest model that might work, measure its performance, and only move to a larger one if needed.

Avoid unnecessary calls, too. Caching common answers, avoiding repeated processing of the same content, and not calling an AI model for tasks that simple code can do reliably all reduce waste.

Models themselves can be made more efficient. Techniques such as quantization, which stores model weights with lower precision, and distillation, which trains a smaller model to imitate a larger one, can reduce the computation needed, often with only a modest loss in quality.

And training can be carbon-aware. Training jobs are often flexible in timing and location, which makes them good candidates for scheduling in cleaner grid regions or times.

Finally, measure and report. If we don't measure the energy and emissions of AI features, we can't make informed decisions about them.

Now, the other side: AI and digital technology as tools for sustainability. There are many promising applications. AI can help forecast electricity demand and the output of solar and wind power, making it easier to integrate renewables into the grid. It can optimize heating and cooling in buildings. It can improve logistics routes, reducing fuel use. It can support precision agriculture, using less water and fertilizer. And it can help scientists analyze climate data and satellite images, for example to monitor deforestation.

A well-known example comes from data centers themselves. In 2016, DeepMind and Google reported that a machine learning system that recommended cooling settings for Google's data centers had reduced the energy used for cooling by up to around forty percent in their tests. It's a good example of AI being used to reduce the footprint of the very infrastructure it runs on.

But I'd encourage healthy skepticism here too. Claims that "AI will solve climate change" deserve the same scrutiny as any other technology claim. The question is always: does this specific application deliver a measurable reduction in emissions or resource use, larger than its own footprint? Sometimes the answer is clearly yes. Sometimes it isn't.

Part six: measuring honestly, and avoiding greenwashing.

You can't improve what you don't measure. But measurement in sustainability is full of traps, so, the basics.

Most organizations report their greenhouse gas emissions using the Greenhouse Gas Protocol, which divides emissions into three scopes. Scope one covers direct emissions from sources the organization owns, such as fuel burned in its own generators or vehicles. Scope two covers indirect emissions from the electricity, heating, and cooling it buys. And scope three covers everything else in its value chain, both upstream and downstream: the manufacturing of the products it buys, including laptops and servers, business travel, employee commuting, and, for many technology companies, the use of their products by customers.

Why does this matter for IT? Because the embodied emissions of the devices and servers an organization buys fall into scope three, and so do the emissions from cloud services it uses. For many technology organizations, scope three is by far the largest part of their footprint, and also the hardest to measure. An organization that reports only scopes one and two can look very green, while most of its real impact remains invisible.

For cloud users, the major cloud providers now offer carbon footprint dashboards or reports for their customers. These are useful starting points, though their methods differ, so it's worth understanding what each one includes before comparing them.

Now, three common forms of greenwashing, meaning claims that make something look more environmentally friendly than it really is.

The first is relying on offsets instead of reductions. Carbon offsets, such as paying for tree planting or other projects elsewhere, can play a role, but their quality varies greatly, and some have been found to deliver much less benefit than claimed. Reducing your own emissions should always come first.

The second is selective reporting: highlighting one impressive metric, such as a great PUE or a renewable energy purchase, while ignoring growing total consumption, water use, or embodied emissions.

And the third is confusing efficiency with impact. This brings me to one of the most important ideas in this whole topic: the rebound effect. When a technology becomes more efficient, it often becomes cheaper to use, and so people use more of it. This idea goes back to the economist William Stanley Jevons, who observed in the nineteenth century that more efficient steam engines led to more coal being used, not less. In IT, we see it constantly: as computing, storage, and bandwidth become cheaper and more efficient, we use vastly more of them. Higher video quality, more data stored "just in case," more AI features. So efficiency improvements are necessary, but on their own, they're not sufficient. We also need to ask whether we need everything we're building and storing.

To be credible, sustainability claims should be specific, measurable, transparent about methods and limitations, and focused on actual reductions over time.

How can an organization turn all of this into a real program, rather than a one-time initiative? A simple, realistic path over one year.

In the first quarter, establish a baseline. Make an inventory of devices, servers, and cloud resources. Collect data from cloud carbon dashboards and energy bills. It won't be perfect, and that's fine. The goal is a starting point.

In the second quarter, go for quick wins. Turn off idle environments, delete unused resources and data, right-size oversized servers, and extend the default device replacement cycle. These steps usually save money, which builds support for the program.

In the third quarter, change the policies. Add sustainability criteria to purchasing, define data retention rules, include efficiency in code review guidelines and architecture decisions, and choose cleaner regions for flexible workloads where possible.

And in the fourth quarter, build it into the culture. Report progress openly, celebrate teams that reduced waste, train developers in green software practices, and set targets for the next year based on what you've learned.

The key is to treat sustainability like security or reliability: not as a side project, but as a normal part of how good engineering work is done.

Part seven: what you can do, starting next week.

Concrete actions, depending on your role. Please choose at least one that fits you.

If you're a software developer:

Treat efficiency as part of quality. Look for the obvious waste: queries in loops, missing caches, oversized images, unnecessary libraries, and repeated computation. Make sure your applications run well on older devices and slower connections. When you build features that use AI, choose the smallest model that does the job well, and avoid unnecessary calls. And when you review someone else's code, it's perfectly reasonable to ask: "Is there a lighter way to do this?"

If you're an architect, a DevOps engineer, or you manage infrastructure:

Measure utilization, and act on it. Right-size resources, use autoscaling, and turn off non-production environments when they're not in use. Set data retention and lifecycle policies. Weigh the carbon intensity of regions when choosing where to run flexible workloads. And make energy, cost, and carbon visible to the teams that create them, because people can't optimize what they can't see.

If you're a manager or a leader:

Include sustainability in your technical decisions and purchasing criteria. Extend device lifecycles, and choose repairable equipment and responsible recycling partners. Ask your cloud providers for carbon data, and start measuring, even imperfectly. Set realistic targets, focused on actual reductions rather than offsets. And give your teams time to address waste, because, like technical debt, it doesn't disappear on its own.

If you're a student:

Learn the fundamentals of efficiency, such as algorithms, data structures, databases, and networking, because they're also the fundamentals of green software. Build sustainability into your projects: measure the size of your web pages, the performance of your queries, or the energy use of your models, and discuss it in your reports. It's a valuable and increasingly relevant skill, and very few students can talk about it. And if you're interested in this field, there's growing demand for people who combine technology with sustainability expertise, from energy and climate data to sustainable software engineering.

And for all of us, as individual users of technology:

Keep your devices longer, repair them when possible, and recycle them properly at the end of their life. Clean up data you don't need, such as old files and duplicate photos in cloud storage. Stream video at the quality you actually need. Turn off devices you're not using. And remember that the most sustainable device is the one you already own.

Before we wrap up, a few questions that come up often, because there are many myths in this area.

"Should I delete my old emails to save the planet?" Storing old emails does use some energy, but for an individual, the effect is very small compared with keeping your phone for an extra year. Cleaning up data is a good habit, and it matters a lot at the scale of organizations, where large amounts of unused data are stored and backed up. Just don't let small gestures distract you from the bigger decisions.

"Is dark mode greener?" On screens with OLED technology, which many modern phones use, dark pixels use less power, so dark mode can save some battery, particularly at high brightness. On other screen types, the difference is small. It's a nice bonus, not a major climate action.

"Is the cloud greener than running our own servers?" Often it can be, because large cloud providers typically achieve higher utilization and more efficient facilities than small server rooms, and many invest heavily in clean energy. But it's not automatic. If moving to the cloud leads you to provision far more resources than you need, and leave them running, the benefit disappears. The cloud gives you the tools to be efficient. You still have to use them.

"Is video streaming bad for the environment?" Estimates of the footprint of streaming have varied widely, and some early, very high estimates were later strongly criticized. The impact depends on the device, the network, the video quality, and the electricity mix. Streaming at a reasonable quality, especially on a small screen, is a reasonable choice. But at the scale of a platform, choices like default video quality and autoplay matter a great deal, because they're multiplied across millions of users.

And finally: "Does any of this matter, compared with industry, transport, or energy production?" Yes, for two reasons. First, the footprint of IT is significant and growing, so it deserves attention in its own right. And second, the skills we've discussed today, measuring, optimizing, and designing efficient systems, are exactly the skills needed to reduce emissions in every other sector. Digital technology runs the energy grid, logistics, buildings, and factories. The more efficient and thoughtful we are as technologists, the more we can help everyone else.

Bringing everything together.

We started by recognizing that digital technology is not weightless. It has a real footprint, in energy, carbon, water, materials, and waste, and that footprint is growing, especially with AI.

We looked at devices, where most of the footprint often comes from manufacturing, and where the most effective strategy is to use them longer, repair them, and recycle them responsibly.

We looked at data centers, where efficiency, clean energy, utilization, water, and heat reuse all matter, and where PUE is a useful but limited measure.

We looked at software, where three principles, energy efficiency, hardware efficiency, and carbon awareness, translate into practical techniques that also tend to save money and improve the user experience.

We looked at AI, which both increases IT's footprint and offers powerful tools for sustainability, and where choosing the right model for the task matters enormously.

And we discussed how to measure honestly, how to avoid greenwashing, and why efficiency alone isn't enough because of the rebound effect.
