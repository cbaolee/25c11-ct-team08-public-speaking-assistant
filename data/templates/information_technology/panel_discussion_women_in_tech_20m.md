I'd like to begin with a short story from computing history, because it's one that many people still don't know.

In 1843, Ada Lovelace published notes on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine, including a method for calculating Bernoulli numbers, which is often described as the first published computer program. A century later, when ENIAC, one of the first general-purpose electronic computers, was unveiled in 1946, the people who had programmed it were a team of six women. Grace Hopper developed one of the first compilers and helped shape COBOL, a language that still runs parts of the world's banking systems today. And Margaret Hamilton led the team that developed the onboard flight software for the Apollo missions, and helped popularize the term "software engineering."

In other words, women were not latecomers to computing. They were there at the very beginning. And yet, today, women remain significantly underrepresented in many parts of the technology industry, especially in engineering and leadership roles. In the United States, for example, the share of computer science degrees earned by women peaked in the mid-1980s and then declined, and it still hasn't returned to that level.

Let's talk about the challenges. Research on this topic points to barriers at several stages: in childhood and school, where computing is often still seen as a "boys' subject"; in hiring, where unconscious bias can affect how candidates are evaluated; and in the workplace, where women are more likely to report being interrupted, having their expertise questioned, or being passed over for high-visibility projects. Many women also leave the industry mid-career, not because they lose interest in technology, but because of workplace culture or a lack of flexibility.

[Round four: the role of everyone, about three minutes]

This next question is for everyone in the room, including the men here today. Building a more inclusive industry isn't only a "women's issue." Teams with diverse perspectives tend to make better decisions and build products that work for more people. And there are concrete examples of what happens when teams lack diversity. For example, some early automated systems, such as certain face recognition and hiring tools, were found to perform worse for women, or to disadvantage them, partly because they were built and tested on data that didn't represent everyone.
