"Startups are where you really learn." "Big tech is the only place with real engineering." "Outsourcing is just for beginners." If you're an IT student, you've probably heard all three, often from people who've only ever worked in one of them. Good {{ time_of_day }}, everyone. I'm {{ speaker_name }}, and {{ speaker_background }} Today, I'd like to help you with a decision that almost every student and junior developer faces sooner or later: startup, big tech, or outsourcing company?

Each of those statements contains a little truth, and a lot of exaggeration. The reality is that each type of company is a different environment, with its own strengths, weaknesses, and hidden costs. And the best choice depends far more on you, and on the specific company, than on the category.

The plan for today: first, I'll describe what each type of company actually is, and what a typical working day might look like there. Then, I'll compare them across seven dimensions that matter for your career. After that, I'll bust a few common myths, give you the questions to ask in an interview to see what a company is really like, and finish with a simple way to decide based on where you are right now.

Let's start with clear definitions, because people use these words loosely.

A startup is a young company trying to find a product that people will pay for, and to grow quickly. It's usually small, from a handful of people to a few hundred. It's often funded by investors, which means it's under pressure to grow fast, and it may not be profitable yet. The defining feature is uncertainty: the product, the market, and even the company's survival are not guaranteed.

A big tech company, in this talk, means a large, established technology company that builds its own products at scale, often used by millions of people. These companies have thousands of engineers, mature processes, strong internal tools, and usually significant resources. I'm also including large product companies that aren't household names, but operate at a similar scale and maturity.

And an outsourcing company, sometimes called an IT services company, builds software for other businesses. A client, often in another country, needs an application, a system, or extra engineers. The outsourcing company provides a team. Some outsourcing companies work on fixed projects with a defined scope. Others provide engineers who join the client's team long term, which is sometimes called team augmentation or a dedicated team model. Outsourcing companies range from small agencies to very large firms with tens of thousands of employees.

Now, a typical day in each.

Imagine a developer at a startup of thirty people. In the morning, there's a short stand-up with the whole engineering team, maybe eight people. A customer reported a bug last night, and since there's no separate support team, the developer investigates it directly, finds the cause, and deploys a fix before lunch. In the afternoon, the founder comes over with an idea for a new feature, because a big potential customer asked for it. There's no detailed specification, just a conversation and a rough sketch. The developer builds a first version over the next few days, touching the front end, the back end, and the database, because there's nobody else to do those parts. By the end of the week, real customers are using it. It's exciting, it's messy, and next week the priorities might completely change.

Now imagine a developer at a big tech company. The team is one of hundreds, responsible for one part of a large product, let's say the notification system. The morning starts with reviewing a teammate's code, using internal tools that check style, run thousands of automated tests, and estimate performance impact. The developer is working on a change that will affect millions of users, so it's carefully designed, written up in a short design document, reviewed by several engineers, and rolled out gradually, first to a tiny percentage of users, while metrics are monitored. The afternoon includes a meeting with two other teams whose systems depend on this one. Progress feels slower, but the scale is enormous, the engineering standards are high, and there are experienced people to learn from at every step.

And now imagine a developer at an outsourcing company. They're on a team of six building a mobile banking feature for a client abroad. The day starts with a call with the client's product owner, in English, to clarify requirements. The developer then works on tasks from a backlog that the client has prioritized, follows the client's coding standards, and submits code for review. In the afternoon, the project manager reminds the team that the sprint demo is in two days, and the client expects the feature to be ready. In six months, this project may end, and the developer might move to a completely different client, in a different industry, with a different technology stack.

Three very different days. None of them is "better" in general. Each one trains different muscles.

Now let's compare them carefully, across seven dimensions.

Dimension one: learning and mentorship.

In big tech, learning tends to be deep and structured. You're surrounded by experienced engineers, code review standards are high, and there's usually documentation, onboarding programs, and internal training. You learn how software is built properly at scale: testing, monitoring, design reviews, and reliability. The downside is that you may work on a narrow piece of a large system for a long time.

In a startup, learning is broad and fast. You touch everything, and you learn by doing, often by making mistakes in production. But mentorship can be thin. If the team is small and everyone is busy, nobody may have time to review your code carefully or explain why something is a bad idea. A startup with strong senior engineers can be an incredible school. A startup without them can teach you bad habits very efficiently.

In outsourcing, learning is often about variety and process. You may work with several industries, clients, and technologies in a few years, and you'll get strong practice in following requirements, working in agile teams, and communicating with clients in English. The quality of technical mentorship varies a lot between companies, and even between projects inside the same company.

Dimension two: scope and ownership.

At a startup, your scope is usually huge. A junior might own an entire feature, from the database to the user interface, and talk directly to customers. That's a fantastic opportunity to grow ownership, but it can also be overwhelming.

At big tech, your individual scope starts narrow, but the impact of that narrow piece can be enormous, because millions of people use it. Ownership grows step by step as you become more senior.

At an outsourcing company, the client usually owns the product and makes the key decisions. Your scope is defined by the contract and the backlog. You can grow ownership by becoming the person the client trusts, but you rarely decide what gets built, or why.

Dimension three: speed and process.

Startups move fast, with minimal process. Decisions are made in minutes, and code can go to production the same day. The cost is that quality, documentation, and testing are often sacrificed, and technical debt piles up quickly.

Big tech moves more carefully. There are design reviews, testing requirements, launch checklists, and coordination between teams. Some people find this frustrating. Others realize it's the reason those products rarely break.

Outsourcing companies usually follow a structured delivery process, often a form of agile with sprints, estimates, and demos, because they need to deliver predictably to clients. The pace is driven by deadlines and contracts, which can sometimes mean intense periods before a release.

Dimension four: compensation and benefits.

I'm not going to quote numbers, because they change by country, by year, and by company. But I can describe the structures, which matter as much as the amounts.

Big tech generally offers competitive salaries, strong benefits, and sometimes stock or bonuses as part of the package. It's usually the most financially predictable of the three.

Startups vary enormously. Some pay below market and offer equity, meaning a small share of the company, which could be worth a lot if the company succeeds, and is very often worth nothing. Others, especially well-funded ones, pay competitively. A good rule: treat startup equity like a lottery ticket. It's a nice possibility, but don't accept a salary you can't live on because of it.

Outsourcing companies usually offer stable salaries with clear pay bands tied to levels, plus bonuses and allowances in some companies. Pay often grows with your English skills, your technical level, and the clients you work with.

Dimension five: stability and risk.

Startups carry the highest risk. Many startups fail, or run out of money before finding a sustainable business. Working at one means accepting that the company might shrink, change direction, or close.

Big tech is usually more stable, but not immune. In recent years, many large technology companies have gone through significant layoffs. So "big" doesn't mean "guaranteed."

Outsourcing companies depend on clients. When the global economy slows, clients cut budgets, and projects end. Larger outsourcing firms with many clients are usually more resilient than small ones that depend on one or two.

Dimension six: career growth and your future options.

Big tech offers clear career ladders, with defined levels and expectations, and having a well-known company on your CV opens many doors later.

Startups offer fast, but less structured, growth. You might become a team lead after two years simply because the company grew around you. That experience can be very valuable, but titles from small startups don't always translate directly to larger companies.

Outsourcing companies often have clear levels and promotion cycles, and some offer paths into roles like business analyst, project manager, or solution architect. Experience with international clients and strong English can also open doors to working abroad or for foreign companies remotely.

And dimension seven: culture and work-life balance.

This varies more between individual companies, and even between teams, than between categories. There are calm startups and burnout startups, relaxed big tech teams and intense ones, outsourcing companies with excellent cultures and some with constant overtime. So never assume. Always ask, and always talk to people who actually work there.

A few myths deserve debunking.

Myth one: "Outsourcing experience isn't real engineering." Not true. Many outsourcing teams build complex, critical systems for banks, healthcare providers, and logistics companies. What matters is the specific project and team. A developer who spent three years building a payment system for an international client, with high quality standards, has real, valuable experience. A developer who spent three years doing repetitive small tasks without growth has less. That's true in every type of company.

Myth two: "You'll learn the most at a startup." Sometimes yes, sometimes no. You'll learn the most where you face challenging problems and have people to learn from. A startup with no senior engineers might teach you speed, but not good engineering.

Myth three: "Big tech is only for geniuses." Big tech interviews are demanding, and preparation matters. But the people who get in are mostly people who prepared seriously and consistently, with strong fundamentals in data structures, algorithms, and system design. It's a skill you can build.

Myth four: "Your first job decides your whole career." It really doesn't. Many people start in outsourcing, move to a startup, then to big tech, or the other way around. Your first job is a starting point, not a life sentence.

Then comes a very practical part. Since the category tells you only part of the story, how do you find out what a specific company is really like? At the end of most interviews, you'll be asked, "Do you have any questions for us?" This is your chance. Some questions worth asking, depending on the type of company.

For any company, ask: "What does onboarding look like for a new developer?" "How are code reviews done on this team?" "What would a typical week look like for me?" And: "What would you expect me to achieve in my first three to six months?"

For a startup, also ask: "How long can the company operate with its current funding?" That might feel bold, but it's a completely legitimate question, and a good startup will answer honestly. Also ask: "Who would be my mentor, and how many senior engineers are on the team?"

For big tech, ask: "What does the team own, and how does this work connect to the overall product?" And: "How do engineers here grow to the next level?"

For an outsourcing company, ask: "Which client and project would I join, and what's the typical project length?" "Do developers communicate directly with the client?" And: "How does the company handle the time between projects?"

And beyond interviews, talk to current or former employees. Ask them about overtime, learning, and why people leave. Their answers are often more revealing than any job description.

Since your first job isn't a life sentence, how do people typically move between these three worlds, and what does each move require?

Moving from outsourcing to big tech usually requires two things. First, interview preparation, because big tech interviews focus heavily on data structures, algorithms, and system design, which daily project work may not practice. Second, being able to explain the impact of your work, not just the tasks. "I implemented screens from the client's backlog" is weak. "I redesigned the data sync for a logistics app used by two thousand drivers, which cut sync failures significantly" is strong. So while you're in outsourcing, keep notes on the problems you solved and the results.

Moving from big tech to a startup is usually easy to get hired for, but hard to adjust to. People used to excellent internal tools and dedicated platform teams suddenly have to set up deployments, monitoring, and databases themselves, and make decisions with incomplete information. The ones who succeed adapt quickly and accept "good enough for now."

And moving from a startup to a larger company often means accepting more process and narrower scope, but in return, gaining depth, mentorship, and stability. Startup veterans bring valuable ownership and speed, but should be ready to learn proper testing, code review standards, and working across many teams.

The skills that transfer everywhere are the same: strong fundamentals, clear communication, reliability, and a habit of learning continuously. Invest in those, and every door stays open.

So, finally, how do you decide? Instead of asking "Which type of company is best?", ask "What do I need most at this stage of my career?"

If you're a fresh graduate and your top priority is building strong engineering fundamentals, a place with good mentorship and code review matters most. That might be a big tech company, a well-run outsourcing company, or a startup with experienced engineers. The mentorship matters more than the logo.

If you want to grow your English, communication, and exposure to different industries, a good outsourcing company can be an excellent choice.

If you thrive on ownership, speed, and uncertainty, and you can handle some financial risk, a startup can accelerate your growth dramatically, especially after you have a solid foundation.

If you want stability, structured growth, and to work at a very large scale, big tech is a strong goal, and it's worth preparing for seriously.

And for every option, use three filters. First: will I learn from the people around me? Second: will I work on problems that stretch me? Third: can I live comfortably and sustainably with this salary, workload, and level of risk? If a company passes all three, it's probably a good choice, whatever category it belongs to.

Collecting the threads: startups offer speed, broad ownership, and fast learning, with high uncertainty and sometimes thin mentorship. Big tech offers scale, high engineering standards, structured growth, and stability, with narrower scope and slower pace. Outsourcing offers variety, client experience, structured delivery, and strong communication practice, with less control over the product. Culture and learning depend far more on the specific company and team than on the category. So ask good questions, talk to real employees, and choose based on what you need right now. And remember, you can always change direction later.
