Few topics in the tech industry generate as much passion as this one: should we work remotely, or in the office? Some people say they'll never go back to commuting. Others say remote work is slowly destroying team culture.

Today, I'd like to compare remote and office work fairly, look at the hybrid models in between, and share what actually seems to make each one work. Because I believe the most important question isn't "remote or office?" It's "how do we work well, wherever we are?"

Let's start with the case for remote work.

First, focus. Software development requires long stretches of deep concentration. Open-plan offices, with conversations, interruptions, and "quick questions," can make that very difficult. Many developers report that they do their most focused work at home.

Second, time and money. No commute means more time for rest, family, exercise, or learning, and less money spent on transport. In large cities, where a commute can take an hour or more each way, that adds up to many hours every week.

Third, access to talent and opportunity. Companies can hire the best people regardless of where they live. And developers can work for companies in other cities or countries without moving, which can open up significant opportunities, especially for people outside major tech hubs.

And fourth, flexibility. Remote work can make it easier to manage family responsibilities, health conditions, or personal routines. For many people, that flexibility is the single most important benefit.

Now, the case for the office.

The biggest advantage is learning, especially for juniors. A lot of learning in software development happens informally: overhearing how a senior engineer debugs a problem, turning around to ask a quick question, or joining a whiteboard discussion. Remotely, juniors often hesitate to "bother" people with a message, and they can stay stuck for much longer. Many managers and researchers have raised concerns that early-career employees are the ones who lose most from fully remote work.

Collaboration on complex, ambiguous problems matters too. Brainstorming a new architecture, resolving a difficult disagreement, or planning a product from scratch is often faster and richer in person. Video calls work well for structured meetings, but less well for spontaneous, messy, creative discussions.

And then there are relationships and trust. It's easier to build trust with people you've had lunch with. Teams that rarely meet can become more transactional, and conflicts can escalate more easily in writing, where tone is easy to misread.

Boundaries and wellbeing round out the list. For some people, working from home blurs the line between work and life. The laptop is always there, and the workday never really ends. Others feel isolated and lonely without the daily social contact of an office.

So what does the evidence say? It's mixed, and it depends a lot on the type of work, the person, and, most importantly, how well the organization manages it. Studies have found productivity benefits in some settings and costs in others. One consistent finding, though, is that many employees value flexibility highly, and that hybrid arrangements, with some days in the office and some at home, can improve retention without clearly harming performance, when they're well organized.

An example shows how the same policy can succeed or fail.

Imagine two companies, both with a hybrid policy: three days in the office, two at home.

At the first company, each person chooses their own office days. So, on any given day, half the team is at home. People commute to the office, then spend the day on video calls with colleagues who aren't there, wearing headphones in a noisy open space. Everyone is frustrated, and the office days feel pointless.

At the second company, each team agrees on the same two "anchor days" in the office. Those days are reserved for collaboration: planning, design discussions, pair programming, mentoring, and team lunches. The remote days are protected for deep, focused work, with fewer meetings. Decisions and important discussions are always written down, so nobody misses information because of where they were sitting.

Same policy on paper. Very different results. The difference wasn't where people worked. It was whether the organization designed how they worked.

So, five practices that work in any model, remote, hybrid, or office.

First, be intentional about what each place is for. Use time together for collaboration and connection, and time apart for focus.

Second, write things down. Decisions, designs, and meeting notes should be documented and accessible to everyone. This is good practice even in a fully in-office team, and it's essential for remote and hybrid ones.

Third, invest deliberately in juniors. Pair them with mentors, schedule regular check-ins, encourage questions explicitly, and consider bringing new hires together in person, especially during their first months.

Fourth, measure outcomes, not presence. Judge people by the quality and impact of their work, not by how many hours they're visible online or at their desk.

And fifth, protect boundaries. Agree on reasonable working hours and response-time expectations, especially across time zones, and respect them.

Writing things down came up in almost every practice, so a moment on the skill that makes remote and hybrid teams work: asynchronous communication. That simply means communication that doesn't require everyone to be available at the same time, like messages, documents, and recorded videos, rather than meetings.

A common situation: a developer is stuck, and sends a message to a colleague: "Hi, are you there?" Then they wait. Two hours later, the colleague, who was in focused work, replies: "Yes?" The developer then explains the problem, and the colleague, now in a meeting, replies an hour later. Half a day has passed for a five-minute question.

Now compare it with this message: "Hi Lan, I'm working on the payment retry feature. When a payment times out, should we retry automatically, or mark it as failed and let the user try again? I lean toward marking it failed, because retrying could charge the user twice. If I don't hear from you by three, I'll go with that, and we can change it later." This message can be answered in one reply, whenever Lan is free, and work continues either way.

Good asynchronous communication follows a few simple habits: put all the context in the first message, state the question or decision clearly, propose a default, and say when you need an answer. It sounds small, but in a remote team, it can save hours every day, and it's just as useful in the office.

And for you as an individual, some advice. If you work remotely, create a routine, a dedicated workspace if possible, and make an extra effort to communicate proactively and to build relationships, including occasional in-person meetups. If you're early in your career, think carefully before choosing a fully remote job. The flexibility is attractive, but the learning you gain from working near experienced people is very valuable at that stage. And whatever your arrangement, notice what helps you do your best work, and talk openly with your manager about it.

Where I land: remote work offers focus, flexibility, time savings, and access to opportunity. Office work offers easier learning, richer collaboration, and stronger relationships. Hybrid can offer the best of both, or the worst of both, depending entirely on how it's designed. The real answer isn't a location. It's intention: deciding together what kind of work happens where, writing things down, supporting the people who need it most, and judging work by its results.
