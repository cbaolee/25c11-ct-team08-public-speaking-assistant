At the heart of everything we do is a simple idea: we want our graduates not only to master today's technology, but to be able to learn tomorrow's technology on their own. Now, let's look at what you can study with us.

{{ argument_programs_overview }}

Whichever program you choose, I'd like to explain how your four years will typically be structured, because many students and parents ask us: "What exactly will I learn, and when?"

In your first year, you'll build your foundation. That includes programming fundamentals, mathematics such as calculus, linear algebra, and discrete mathematics, an introduction to computer systems, and general education courses, including English and soft skills. For many students, the first year is the biggest adjustment, because university learning is more independent than high school. That's why we provide academic advisors and peer mentors to help you settle in.

In your second year, you'll study the core of computing: data structures and algorithms, object-oriented programming, databases, computer networks, operating systems, and software engineering basics. This is where you'll start building larger projects, often in teams.

In your third year, you'll go deeper into your chosen specialization, for example, artificial intelligence, software engineering, data science, information systems, or computer networks and security, depending on your program. You'll choose elective courses based on your interests, and many students join a research group or a lecturer's project at this stage.

And in your fourth year, you'll bring everything together through an internship at a company and a graduation project or thesis, where you'll solve a real problem from start to finish, under the guidance of a supervisor.

How we teach matters as much as what we teach. In our faculty, learning doesn't just happen in lecture halls. Many courses include lab sessions, where you write code and work with real tools. Most core courses include group projects, because teamwork is a daily reality in the IT industry. And we encourage students to go beyond the curriculum through competitions, research, and personal projects.

Speaking of going beyond the curriculum, let me introduce our labs and research activities. {{ argument_labs_and_research }}

What I'd like students to know is that research isn't only for PhD students. Undergraduate students who are curious and motivated are welcome to join research groups, often starting from their second or third year. Some of them co-author scientific papers before they graduate, which is a big advantage if you're interested in graduate study, at home or abroad.

Now, let's talk about student life, because university is about much more than classes.

Our students are active in many clubs and communities. {{ argument_student_clubs_and_activities }}

Through these activities, students make lifelong friends, develop leadership skills, and often discover what they're truly passionate about. A good thing for new students to know: some of the most important things you'll learn at university, you'll learn outside the classroom.

To help you picture what studying here is really like, let me describe a typical day for a second-year student.

The day might start with a morning lecture on data structures and algorithms, where the lecturer explains a new concept and works through examples. After a short break, there's a lab session: students sit at computers, implement what they just learned, and ask the teaching assistants for help when they get stuck. Lunch is often a mix of food and conversation in the canteen, sometimes continuing a discussion about the morning's lab.

In the afternoon, there might be a databases class, followed by a group meeting for a course project, where four students divide tasks, review each other's code, and plan the next steps. In the evening, some students go to a club activity or a practice session for a programming contest, some study in the library, and some just rest. Learning to manage your own time is one of the most important skills you'll develop here.

For the students here who are planning to join us, let me also give you some advice on how to prepare, starting this summer.

First, strengthen your math and English, because they'll make your first year much smoother. Second, try a beginner programming course, so your first programming class isn't your very first experience of coding. Third, build good study habits: plan your week, take notes in your own words, and don't be afraid to ask questions. And fourth, take care of yourself. Starting university is exciting, but it's also a big change. Arrive rested, curious, and ready to make new friends.

And what kind of students are we looking for? Of course, good academic results matter. But beyond grades, we're looking for students who are curious, who enjoy solving problems, and who are willing to work hard when things get difficult. You don't need to know how to program before you arrive. Many of our best students wrote their first line of code in their first semester. What matters most is your attitude and your willingness to learn.

Now, the questions that students and parents ask most often.

The first question is: "Do I need to be very good at math?" You need a solid foundation, and you'll study a fair amount of math in your first two years, because it trains logical thinking and supports areas like artificial intelligence and data science. But you don't need to be a math competition champion. Consistent effort matters more than natural talent, and we offer support for students who need it.

The second question is: "Do I need to know programming before I start?" No. Our programs start from the basics. Of course, if you have time before university, trying a beginner course in Python or a similar language will make your first semester easier.

The third question is: "How important is English?" Very important. Much of the technical documentation and many tools in IT are in English, and some of our courses use English materials. If your English isn't strong yet, don't worry, but start improving it now. It will make a big difference.

The fourth question is: "Will AI replace IT jobs?" AI is changing how IT work is done, and we've updated our curriculum to reflect that, including courses and projects involving AI tools. But AI also creates new needs: people who can design systems, judge whether AI outputs are correct and safe, and apply technology to real problems. Our goal is to prepare students to work with AI, not to compete against it.

And the fifth question, usually from parents: "Will my child be supported, especially in the first year?" Yes. Every student has an academic advisor, and we have peer mentoring programs where senior students help freshmen adjust. We also provide student services for counseling and wellbeing. University is a big change, and we don't expect students to handle it all alone.
