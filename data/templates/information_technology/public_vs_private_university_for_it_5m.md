I'm {{ argument_speaker_name }}, and {{ argument_speaker_background }} Today, let's look at a choice many students and parents struggle with when deciding where to study IT: a public university or a private one?

I want to say upfront: I'm not here to tell you that one type is better than the other. There are excellent and weak schools in both groups. What I'd like to do is give you a fair comparison and a practical checklist, so you can make the choice that fits your situation.

Let's start with public universities.

Public universities are funded and managed, at least partly, by the state. In many countries, including Vietnam, the most established IT faculties are often found at public universities, many with long histories and strong reputations.

Their main strengths usually include lower tuition fees, which can make a big difference for many families. They often have a strong academic foundation, with a lot of emphasis on mathematics, theory, and fundamentals, which pays off in the long term, especially if you're interested in research, artificial intelligence, or further study abroad. Many have experienced lecturers, research labs, and large alumni networks working across the IT industry.

But public universities also have some common challenges. Entrance competition can be very tough. Class sizes can be large, which means less individual attention. Facilities vary and may be older at some schools. And because programs are often more theory-oriented, students sometimes need to take extra initiative to build practical, industry-ready skills through projects and internships.

Private universities work differently. They are funded mainly by tuition fees and private investment. In recent years, many have invested heavily in IT programs.

Their main strengths often include modern facilities and campuses, smaller classes in some programs, and curricula that are frequently updated to match industry trends. Many private universities have strong links with companies, offering internships, company projects, and sometimes direct recruitment. And admission requirements can be more flexible.

But there are challenges, too. Tuition fees are usually significantly higher, so it's important to think about the total cost over four years, not just the first year. Quality varies a lot between institutions. Some private universities are excellent, while others are still building their reputation and academic depth.

So how should you decide? Instead of focusing on "public" or "private," I'd suggest looking at six practical factors for each specific school.

The curriculum matters most. Look at the actual list of courses. Is there a solid foundation in programming, data structures, algorithms, and mathematics? Are there practical courses and real projects?

Look at the lecturers, too. What are their qualifications and industry experience? Do they publish research or work on real-world projects?

Check the outcomes. Where do graduates work? What percentage find IT jobs within six months? Ask for real data, not just marketing brochures.

Industry connections matter as well. Are there internships, company partnerships, and career events?

Calculate the total cost. Consider tuition for all four years, possible fee increases, living costs, and available scholarships.

And finally, the environment. Visit the campus if you can. Talk to current students. Ask them what they like and what they wish were different.

The comparison in brief: public universities often offer lower fees, strong academic foundations, and established reputations, but with tough competition and sometimes less individual attention. Private universities often offer modern facilities, close industry links, and updated curricula, but at a higher cost, and with quality that varies more between schools. The best choice depends on the specific program, your goals, and your family's situation. So compare schools based on curriculum, lecturers, outcomes, industry connections, cost, and environment, and remember that wherever you study, what you do with your time there matters most.
