I'd like to start with a scene that many people in our industry will recognize.

It's eleven at night. A developer is still at their laptop. The release is tomorrow, and there are three bugs left. They haven't exercised in weeks. They used to love programming, and spent weekends building side projects for fun. Now, they feel a knot in their stomach every time they open their work chat. They're irritated with their teammates, they've stopped joining team lunches, and they can't remember the last time they finished a task and felt proud of it. They tell themselves it's just a busy period, and that it will get better after the release. But they've been telling themselves that for eight months.

That developer is experiencing burnout. And if you've ever felt something similar, you're far from alone.

Today, I want to talk about burnout and mental health in the IT industry: what burnout actually is, why our industry is particularly vulnerable to it, how to recognize the warning signs in yourself and others, and, most importantly, what individuals, managers, and organizations can do about it.

Let's start with a clear definition, because the word "burnout" is often used loosely.

In 2019, the World Health Organization included burnout in its International Classification of Diseases, ICD-11. But it's important to understand how. It's not classified as a medical condition. It's described as an "occupational phenomenon": a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. The WHO definition is specifically about work, not about stress in other areas of life.

According to that definition, burnout has three dimensions. These are based largely on decades of research by the psychologist Christina Maslach and her colleagues.

The first is exhaustion: feeling drained of energy, physically and emotionally, in a way that rest doesn't seem to fix. A weekend off isn't enough anymore.

The second is increased mental distance from one's job, or feelings of negativism or cynicism about it. You stop caring. You become detached, irritable, or sarcastic about your work, your colleagues, or your customers. "What's the point?" becomes a frequent thought.

And the third is reduced professional efficacy: feeling that you're no longer effective, that your work doesn't matter, or that you're not achieving anything, even when you're working very hard.

Notice that burnout isn't the same as simply being tired, or having a stressful week. Stress is often about too much: too much work, too much pressure. Burnout tends to feel more like not enough: not enough energy, not enough motivation, not enough hope that things will change.

It's also important to say that burnout is different from mental health conditions such as depression or anxiety disorders, although they can overlap, and burnout can contribute to them. If someone's low mood, anxiety, or loss of interest extends beyond work and affects every part of their life, it's important to talk to a qualified health professional. Recognizing burnout is not a replacement for proper diagnosis or care.

Now, why is the IT industry particularly vulnerable?

Researchers who study burnout have found that it's driven less by individual weakness and more by the conditions of work. Maslach and her colleague Michael Leiter identified six areas where a mismatch between a person and their job increases the risk of burnout: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values. Let's look at how each one shows up in tech.

Workload. Tight deadlines, constantly shifting priorities, and the culture of "crunch" before releases, which is especially notorious in parts of the game industry, can create sustained periods of overwork. And unlike many jobs, software work is never really "finished." There's always another bug, another feature, another improvement.

Control. Developers often have little influence over deadlines that were promised to customers before they were even consulted. Being responsible for outcomes without having control over the conditions is a classic recipe for stress.

Reward. Much of the most important engineering work is invisible: preventing outages, improving reliability, paying down technical debt, mentoring others. When only new features are celebrated, people doing essential invisible work can feel unappreciated.

Community. Remote work, distributed teams, and long hours of solitary concentration can lead to isolation. And in some teams, a culture of blame after incidents makes people afraid to admit mistakes or ask for help.

Fairness. Unclear promotion criteria, unequal distribution of on-call duties or difficult tasks, and pay differences that feel unjustified all erode trust.

And values. Being asked to build features you believe are harmful, or to cut corners on quality or security in ways you're not comfortable with, creates a painful conflict between what you do and what you believe.

On top of these, our industry has some specific pressures. On-call duties, where you might be woken up at three in the morning by an alert. Being "always on" through work chat on your phone. The pressure to constantly learn new technologies to stay relevant, which, as we know, never stops. And impostor syndrome, which is common in tech: the persistent feeling that you're not as competent as others think, and that you'll eventually be "found out," even when there's clear evidence of your ability.

Let's talk about warning signs, because burnout usually develops gradually, and it's much easier to address early.

In yourself, watch for: feeling tired most of the time, even after rest; trouble sleeping, or sleeping much more than usual; dreading the start of the workweek; becoming more irritable or cynical; withdrawing from colleagues; finding it harder to concentrate; taking much longer to complete tasks that used to be easy; or relying more on caffeine, alcohol, or other substances to get through the day. Physical signs, such as frequent headaches, stomach problems, or getting sick more often, can also be part of the picture.

In colleagues, you might notice: someone who used to be engaged becoming quiet in meetings; a normally careful person making more mistakes; someone sending messages late at night, every night; increased conflict; or someone saying things like "I don't care anymore," or "Nothing I do makes a difference."

Now, what can we do about it? I'll address three levels: individuals, managers, and organizations. Because burnout is mainly caused by work conditions, it can't be solved only by telling individuals to do yoga or meditate. Self-care helps, but if the workload is impossible, no amount of breathing exercises will fix it. We need action at every level.

Let's start with what you can do as an individual.

Protect the basics. Sleep, movement, food, and time outdoors are not luxuries. They're the foundation of your ability to think clearly and handle stress. When we're under pressure, these are usually the first things we sacrifice, and that makes everything else harder.

Set boundaries, and communicate them. Turn off work notifications outside working hours, if your role allows it. Decide on a time when your workday ends. And if you're asked to take on more than you can realistically do, say so, clearly and early. A useful phrase is: "I can do this, but it means that this other task will be delayed. Which one is the priority?" That turns an impossible demand into a prioritization conversation.

Reconnect with meaning. Notice the impact of your work: the user whose problem you solved, the colleague you helped. Some people find it helpful to write down, at the end of each week, one thing they did that mattered.

Stay connected. Talk to colleagues, friends, and family. Isolation makes burnout worse, and simply saying "I'm struggling" to someone you trust can be a huge relief.

Rest properly. Take your vacation days, and actually disconnect during them. A holiday where you check work messages every day isn't really a holiday.

Keep learning in a sustainable way. You don't need to master every new framework. Choose what's genuinely useful for your goals, and let go of the rest without guilt.

And ask for help. Talk to your manager, use your company's employee assistance program if it has one, or speak to a mental health professional. Asking for help is a sign of strength and self-awareness, not of weakness.

Before we move to managers, something for everyone, because you don't need to be a manager to make a difference. What should you do if you're worried about a colleague?

Start by choosing a private, relaxed moment, not a team meeting or a busy chat channel. Describe what you've noticed, without judging: "I've noticed you've been online very late most nights this month," rather than "You look terrible." Then ask an open question, such as "How are you doing, really?", and give them time to answer. Listen more than you talk. You don't need to fix anything, and you don't need to have the perfect words. Often, the most helpful thing is simply that someone noticed and cared.

There are also a few things to avoid. Avoid minimizing, like "Everyone's tired, just push through." Avoid comparing, like "When I was a junior, we worked much harder." And avoid immediately offering solutions before you understand the situation.

If they share that they're struggling, you can gently encourage them to talk to their manager, or to a professional, and offer practical help, such as reviewing a task together or covering a meeting. And if you're ever concerned that someone might be at risk of harming themselves, take it seriously: don't keep it to yourself, and help them get professional or emergency support immediately.

Now, what can managers and team leads do? You have enormous influence over the six factors I described earlier.

First, manage workload realistically. Involve engineers in estimates. Push back on unrealistic deadlines instead of passing the pressure straight down. And treat crunch as a planning failure to learn from, not as a normal way of working.

Second, give people control. Let teams make decisions about how they work, and involve them in decisions that affect them.

Third, recognize invisible work. Publicly appreciate the person who prevented an outage, improved documentation, or helped a colleague, not only the person who shipped the flashy feature.

Fourth, build psychological safety. That means a team climate where people feel safe to admit mistakes, ask questions, and raise concerns without fear of humiliation or punishment. Research at Google, in a project known as Project Aristotle, found psychological safety to be the most important factor distinguishing its most effective teams. Practices like blameless post-incident reviews, which focus on what in the system allowed a mistake to happen rather than on who to blame, are a concrete way to build it.

Fifth, check in regularly, and really listen. In one-on-one meetings, ask not only about tasks, but about how people are doing. And if you notice warning signs, have a private, caring conversation. You might say: "I've noticed you seem more tired lately, and you've been working late a lot. How are you doing? Is there anything I can help with?" You don't need to diagnose anything. Your role is to notice, to care, and to help the person access support.

And sixth, model healthy behavior. If you send messages at midnight, your team will feel they need to respond at midnight, whatever you say. If you take vacations and disconnect, they'll feel permitted to do the same.

Finally, what can organizations do?

They can make sure staffing matches the work, rather than relying on permanent overtime. They can design fair and sustainable on-call rotations, with compensation or time off after difficult nights. They can make promotion criteria transparent, and recognize many kinds of contribution. They can provide access to confidential mental health support, and make it clear that using it carries no stigma. They can train managers to recognize and respond to burnout. And they can measure wellbeing seriously, through regular, anonymous surveys, and then actually act on the results.

And for those who are already experiencing burnout, I want to say something about recovery, because it's often misunderstood.

Recovery from burnout usually takes longer than people expect. It's not solved by a single long weekend. It often takes weeks or months, and it's rarely a straight line: there will be good days and bad days. It typically involves two things at the same time: rest, to restore your energy, and change, to address the conditions that caused the burnout in the first place. Rest without change often leads straight back to burnout. That change might be a conversation with your manager about workload, a different role or team, new boundaries, or, sometimes, a new job.

A few closing words about stigma. In many workplaces, and in many cultures, talking about mental health is still difficult. People worry that admitting they're struggling will be seen as weakness, or will hurt their career. That silence is part of the problem. Each time someone speaks openly, whether a junior developer or a senior leader, it becomes a little easier for the next person.

The main points, gathered: burnout is a syndrome caused by chronic workplace stress that hasn't been managed, recognized by the WHO as an occupational phenomenon, with three dimensions: exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy. Our industry is particularly vulnerable because of workload, lack of control, invisible work, isolation, constant learning pressure, and always-on culture. The warning signs are often gradual, so noticing them early matters. And the solutions exist at every level: individuals protecting their basics and setting boundaries, managers managing workload and building psychological safety, and organizations creating sustainable systems.
