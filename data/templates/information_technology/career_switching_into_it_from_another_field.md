Before I tell you anything about IT, I'd like to tell you who I was before it. My name is {{ speaker_name }}. {{ speaker_switch_story }}

I'm telling you this because I know many of you are in a similar place right now. Maybe you're an accountant, a teacher, an engineer, a salesperson, or a marketer. Maybe you're thirty, or thirty-five, or older. And you're wondering: "Is it too late for me to move into IT? Will anyone hire me?"

The answer is: it's not too late, it's definitely possible, but it's not easy, and it works best with a smart plan. So today, I'd like to share that plan: how to choose the right target role, how to use the experience you already have, how to learn efficiently, how to manage the financial side, and how to land your first job.

Let's start with the most important mindset shift: you're not starting from zero.

Career changers often think: "Fresh graduates have four years of IT education. I'm so far behind." But you have things many fresh graduates don't have. You know how a real workplace functions. You've dealt with difficult customers, deadlines, and managers. You can communicate professionally. And you understand a specific industry from the inside. Those are exactly the skills that many IT teams struggle to find.

The smartest strategy isn't to throw away your past. It's to build a bridge from your past to your new career.

That brings us to step one: choose a target role that connects with your background.

IT is not one job. And some roles are much more natural first steps for certain backgrounds. A few examples.

If you come from accounting or finance, data analysis is often a natural move. You already work with numbers, spreadsheets, and business reports. Adding SQL and visualization tools builds directly on what you know. And companies in banking and finance value analysts who understand their domain.

If you come from teaching, you're good at explaining complex ideas and structuring information. Roles like business analyst, quality assurance tester, technical trainer, or technical writer can be great fits, and many teachers also become excellent developers.

If you come from marketing or design, you might move into front-end development, user experience design, or marketing analytics, where your understanding of users and communication is a real advantage.

If you come from engineering, logistics, or manufacturing, you already think in systems and processes. Software development, data analysis, or IT roles in your own industry, such as working with ERP systems, can be strong choices.

And if you come from customer service or sales, you understand customers deeply. Roles like IT support, customer success for software products, quality assurance, or business analysis can be excellent starting points, and many people later move further into technical roles from there.

The key idea: look for the overlap between what you already know and what IT needs. That overlap is where you'll stand out.

Step two: learn efficiently, with a clear plan.

As a career changer, your time is limited. You may have a full-time job, a family, or both. So you need to be focused.

Choose one target role, and learn the core skills for that role, not everything in IT. Use a structured program, whether it's a reputable online course, a part-time program, or a bootcamp, so you don't waste time deciding what to learn next. And protect regular study time, even if it's only one hour a day on weekdays and a longer session at the weekend. Consistency beats intensity.

Most importantly, build projects that use your previous experience. If you were an accountant, build a dashboard that analyzes expenses. If you were a teacher, build a simple quiz app for students. If you worked in logistics, analyze delivery data. These projects show employers not only that you can do the technical work, but that you bring something unique.

Step three: plan the financial side carefully.

This is the part many people underestimate. Switching careers often means a period of study, and sometimes a lower salary at the start, because you're entering at a junior level.

So, before you resign from anything, prepare. Build some savings. Consider learning while you're still employed. And think about the transition: could you move gradually, for example, by taking on technical tasks in your current job first, or doing small freelance projects on the side? A gradual transition is often less risky than a dramatic jump.

Step four: get your first IT job using your advantages.

Four strategies work particularly well for career changers.

First, look inside your current company. This is often the easiest door. Your employer already trusts you and knows your work. Volunteer for projects involving data, automation, or new systems. Talk to the IT team. Many people make their first move internally.

Second, target companies in your previous industry. A hospital software company might value a former nurse. A fintech startup might value a former banker. Your domain knowledge is a competitive advantage that fresh graduates simply don't have.

Third, network deliberately. Join meetups, online communities, and events. Talk to people who work in your target role. Ask them about their work, not for a job. Many opportunities come through people, not job portals.

And fourth, tell your story well. In your CV and interviews, don't hide your previous career. Explain it as a strength: "I spent six years in accounting, so I understand exactly what finance teams need from their reports. Now I combine that with SQL and Power BI." That's a compelling story.

To make this concrete, consider a realistic six-month transition plan for someone working full-time who wants to move from accounting into data analysis.

In months one and two, they study for about seven hours a week: advanced Excel, pivot tables, and the basics of SQL. At work, they start automating one monthly report that used to take them a full day.

In months three and four, they go deeper into SQL and learn Power BI. They build their first portfolio project: a dashboard analyzing public data on household spending, and they write a short summary of the findings. At work, they offer to help the finance team build a simple dashboard.

In month five, they complete a second project using a sample sales dataset, answering ten business questions with SQL. They update their CV and online profile, and start attending a data community meetup once a month.

And in month six, they start applying, both internally, to the company's data or reporting team, and externally, to analyst roles in finance and banking, where their accounting background is a clear advantage.

Notice three things about this plan. It doesn't require quitting a job. It uses the current job as a training ground. And every step builds evidence that an employer can see.

Now, the doubts that almost every career changer faces.

"Am I too old?" Age can be a factor in some companies, and it's fair to admit that. But many companies value maturity, reliability, and professional experience. And IT has many people who started their careers later and built very successful ones.

"Everyone around me seems smarter and younger." That feeling has a name: imposter syndrome. Almost everyone in IT feels it at some point, including people with twenty years of experience. It doesn't mean you don't belong. It means you're learning.

"What if I fail?" Then you'll have learned valuable skills that make you better in your current field too. Data skills make an accountant more valuable. Technical skills make a marketer more effective. There's very little downside to learning.

A few common mistakes to avoid. Quitting your job too early, without savings or a plan. Trying to learn everything at once. Collecting certificates instead of building projects. Hiding your previous career instead of using it. And giving up after the first few rejections. Rejections are normal. Almost every career changer hears "no" many times before hearing "yes."
