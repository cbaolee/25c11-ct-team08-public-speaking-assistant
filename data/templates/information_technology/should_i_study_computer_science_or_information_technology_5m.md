Let's keep it simple.

Computer Science is about how computers and software work, and how to make them work better. It asks questions like: How can we solve this problem efficiently? How do we design an algorithm that can handle a billion users? How can a machine learn from data? It's the science behind the technology.

Information Technology is about how to use and manage technology to solve real problems for people and organizations. It asks questions like: How do we set up a secure network for this company? How do we choose, install, and maintain the right systems? How do we keep everything running smoothly for hundreds of users?

Now, let's look at what you'll actually study.

In a Computer Science program, you can expect a strong focus on programming and theory: data structures and algorithms, computer architecture, operating systems, theory of computation, and areas like artificial intelligence, machine learning, computer graphics, and compilers. And you can expect quite a lot of mathematics: discrete mathematics, linear algebra, probability, and statistics, and sometimes calculus. Math is a real part of the program, not just a side topic.

In an Information Technology program, you'll usually still learn programming, but with more emphasis on practical systems: computer networks, databases, system administration, cloud infrastructure, cybersecurity, web development, and how to manage IT projects and support users in organizations. The math is generally lighter, and there's often more hands-on lab work with real tools and systems.

What about careers?

Computer Science graduates often become software engineers, back-end or systems developers, AI and machine learning engineers, data scientists, researchers, or game and graphics developers. These roles usually involve designing and building complex software.

Information Technology graduates often become system administrators, network engineers, cloud engineers, cybersecurity specialists, IT support and infrastructure specialists, database administrators, and later, IT managers. These roles usually involve building, running, securing, and supporting the technology that organizations rely on.

So, which one is right for you? Three questions can help.

First, what kind of problems excite you? If you enjoy puzzles, logic, and asking "how can I make this smarter or faster?", Computer Science may suit you. If you enjoy making things work in the real world, setting things up, fixing problems, and helping people use technology, Information Technology may be a better fit.

Second, how do you feel about mathematics? You don't need to be a math genius for Computer Science, but you should be comfortable with it and willing to work hard on it. If math really isn't your thing, an IT program may be a more comfortable path.

And third, what kind of work do you imagine yourself doing in five years? Writing complex code? Doing research in AI? Protecting a company from cyberattacks? Running the cloud systems for a large business? Working backward from that picture often makes the choice clearer.

One practical tip before I finish. The names of programs can be confusing, because different universities use them differently. In some universities, "Information Technology" is the name of a broad faculty that includes Computer Science, Software Engineering, Information Systems, and other majors. In others, it's a specific major. So don't decide based only on the name. Look at the actual curriculum: which courses are required, how much math there is, and what recent graduates are doing now.

The bottom line: Computer Science focuses on the science of computing, algorithms, and building complex software, with more math and theory. Information Technology focuses on applying and managing technology for people and organizations, with more hands-on systems work. Both lead to strong careers. The best choice is the one that matches what genuinely interests you, so look closely at the curriculum, think about the problems you'd love to solve, and talk to current students and graduates.
