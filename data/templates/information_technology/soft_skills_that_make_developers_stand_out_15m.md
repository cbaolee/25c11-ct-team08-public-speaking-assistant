Two candidates apply for the same developer position. Their technical skills are very similar. Both solve the coding problem. Both have decent projects. But in the interview, the first candidate answers questions with one-word replies, struggles to explain their own project, and gets defensive when the interviewer points out a small bug. The second candidate thinks out loud, asks clarifying questions, explains their project clearly, and says, "Good catch, I didn't think about that case. Let me fix it."

Who do you think got the job? Almost every time, it's the second one. Not because they were a better programmer, but because the team could already imagine working with them.

The term "soft skills" sometimes makes these abilities sound less important, as if they were optional extras. But in modern software development, where almost everything is built by teams, they're anything but soft. Some people prefer to call them "core skills" or "professional skills," and I think that's more accurate.

Today, I'll share seven soft skills that make developers stand out. For each one, I'll explain what it looks like in practice, and give you one small habit you can start using right away.

Skill one: clear communication, especially in writing.

Developers communicate all day long: in chat messages, pull request descriptions, bug reports, documentation, and emails. In remote and international teams, much of this happens in writing.

Clear communication means the other person understands you the first time, without needing to ask three follow-up questions. Compare these two messages. "Login doesn't work." And: "Since this morning's release, users who log in with Google get an error page. Email login still works. I've attached a screenshot and the error from the logs." The second message saves everyone time, and it shows professionalism.

The habit: before you send an important message, reread it once and ask, "Could someone understand this without any extra context?" And put the main point first.

Skill two: active listening.

Many problems in software projects come from misunderstanding what someone actually needs. Active listening means focusing fully on the other person, asking questions to clarify, and checking your understanding before you start working.

When a product manager describes a feature, a developer who listens actively might say: "Let me make sure I understand. You want users to be able to export their reports, mainly so they can share them with their managers. Is that right? Do they need it as a PDF, or would a spreadsheet work?" That one question might change the whole solution.

The habit: at the end of any requirement discussion, summarize what you heard in one or two sentences, and ask, "Did I get that right?"

Skill three: collaboration and teamwork.

Great software is rarely built by one person. Collaboration means sharing information openly, helping teammates when they're stuck, giving credit generously, and putting the team's success above your own ego.

It also means being easy to work with. That might sound simple, but it's powerful. People notice who responds to messages, who keeps promises, who's pleasant in code reviews, and who makes meetings better rather than longer.

The habit: once a week, help a teammate with something, even something small, like reviewing their code promptly or answering a question in the team chat.

Skill four: giving and receiving feedback well.

Code reviews, retrospectives, and one-on-one meetings are all about feedback. Developers who can receive feedback without becoming defensive learn dramatically faster. And developers who can give feedback kindly and clearly help the whole team improve.

When receiving feedback, listen fully, ask for examples if something isn't clear, thank the person, and remember that criticism of your code isn't criticism of you. When giving feedback, be specific, focus on the code or the behavior rather than the person, and explain why something matters. For example, instead of "This is wrong," try: "This might cause a problem when the list is empty. What do you think about adding a check here?"

The habit: the next time someone comments on your work, start your reply with "Thanks, good point," and genuinely consider it before responding.

Skill five: time management and prioritization.

Developers face constant interruptions: messages, meetings, urgent bugs, and questions from colleagues. Those who stand out can protect focused time for deep work, estimate realistically, and prioritize the tasks that matter most rather than the ones that shout the loudest.

And just as importantly, they communicate when things change. If a task is taking longer than expected, they say so early, not on the day of the deadline. Being reliable doesn't mean never being late. It means never surprising people. A good delay message has three parts: what's late and by how much, why, and what you propose. Something like: "The export feature will be about two days late, because the old report code turned out to need a rewrite. I can either ship a basic version on Friday and the full one on Tuesday, or ship everything on Tuesday. Which works better for you?" A message like that turns bad news into a decision the team can make together, and it builds trust instead of eroding it.

The habit: at the start of each day, write down the one or two most important things you need to finish, and block time for them before checking every message.

Skill six: problem framing and critical thinking.

Strong developers don't just solve the problem they're given. They make sure it's the right problem. They ask: What's the real goal? Is there a simpler way? What could go wrong? What assumptions are we making?

For example, if a manager asks for a feature to send reminder emails every day, a developer with strong critical thinking might ask: "Do users actually want daily emails, or would they prefer a notification only when something changes?" That question can save weeks of work, and improve the product.

The habit: before starting any significant task, write one sentence describing the actual goal, not the solution. "The goal is to help users not miss deadlines," rather than "Build daily reminder emails."

Skill seven: empathy, for users and for teammates.

Empathy means understanding how others think and feel. For users, it means remembering that not everyone uses your app the way you do. Some are older, some have slow internet, some have disabilities, and some are using your app while stressed or in a hurry. Developers with empathy build products that are easier and kinder to use.

For teammates, empathy means understanding that the designer who changed the layout again may be under pressure from a client, or that the junior asking the same question twice may be overwhelmed. Responding with patience instead of frustration builds trust.

The habit: once in a while, watch a real user use something you built, without helping them. It's humbling, and it changes how you think about your work forever.

Job interviews test these skills too, and many candidates don't realize they're being assessed on them.

Most companies include behavioral questions, such as: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate," "Tell me about a project that didn't go as planned," or "Tell me about a time you received difficult feedback." These questions are designed to see how you actually behave, based on real past situations.

A simple and widely used structure for answering is called STAR: Situation, Task, Action, and Result. First, briefly describe the situation. Then, what you were responsible for. Then, what you specifically did, and this should be the longest part. And finally, the result, ideally with something you learned.

For example: "In my second-year group project, two teammates disagreed about which database to use, and the discussion was delaying our work. I was responsible for the back end. I suggested we each list our requirements and compare both options against them in a short meeting. We chose the one that fit our needs better, and the teammate whose option wasn't chosen took responsibility for setting it up. We finished on time, and I learned that agreeing on criteria first makes disagreements much less personal."

That answer shows collaboration, communication, and problem framing, all in under a minute. So prepare three or four stories like this before any interview, from projects, part-time jobs, clubs, or volunteer work. They're as important as practicing coding questions.

Now, you might be wondering: "How can I practice these skills while I'm still a student, or early in my career?" Here are a few practical ideas.

Use group projects as training grounds. Volunteer to be the person who talks to the "client" or the lecturer, writes the meeting notes, or presents the project.

Join clubs, volunteer projects, or hackathons. These are safe environments to practice teamwork, leadership, and communication under pressure.

Write publicly. A short blog post explaining something you learned, or a clear README for your project, trains your writing.

Practice presenting. Explain your project to a friend in two minutes. Then again in one minute. Then to someone who knows nothing about programming.

And ask for feedback on your soft skills, not just your code. Ask a teammate: "Was my explanation in the meeting clear?" or "Is there anything I could do to make working with me easier?" It takes courage, but the answers are incredibly valuable.

Let's run through the seven skills one more time. Clear communication, especially in writing. Active listening. Collaboration and teamwork. Giving and receiving feedback well. Time management and prioritization. Problem framing and critical thinking. And empathy, for users and teammates.

Technical skills get you into the room. Soft skills determine how far you go once you're there. And unlike some technologies, which may be outdated in five years, these skills will stay valuable for your entire career, in any company, any country, and any role.

So, a challenge for you: pick one of the seven habits I mentioned today, just one, and practice it every day for the next two weeks. You'll be surprised by how quickly people notice the difference.
