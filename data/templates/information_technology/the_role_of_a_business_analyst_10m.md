There's a story that's been told in software companies for decades, usually with a funny cartoon of a tree swing. The customer explains what they want. The salesperson promises something slightly different. The designer draws something else. The developers build something else again. And in the end, what the customer actually needed was a simple tire hanging from a tree branch.

Good {{ argument_time_of_day }}, everyone. My name is {{ argument_speaker_name }}. {{ argument_speaker_background }} And today's topic is the person whose job is to make sure that cartoon never describes your project: the business analyst, often called the BA. It's a role that's essential in almost every software project, but that many people, even in IT, don't fully understand.

The cartoon is a joke, but it describes a very real problem. A huge number of software projects fail or go over budget not because the developers can't code, but because the team built the wrong thing. The business needs were misunderstood, incomplete, or changed without anyone noticing.

That is the problem a business analyst exists to solve.

So, what is a business analyst?

In simple terms, a business analyst is a bridge between the business side and the technical side. On one side, you have people who understand the business: managers, staff, customers. They know what problems they have, but they often can't describe them in a way that developers can build from. On the other side, you have the technical team, who know how to build software, but don't always understand the business deeply.

The BA stands in the middle. They understand the business needs, analyze them, and translate them into clear requirements that the technical team can build and test. And then they make sure that what gets built actually solves the original problem.

You can think of a BA as a kind of translator, but a translator who also asks: "Is this really what you need? And why?"

Now, what does a BA actually do every day? Let me describe the main activities.

The first is gathering requirements, which BAs often call elicitation. This means interviewing stakeholders, running workshops, observing how people actually work, and studying existing documents and systems. A good BA asks a lot of questions, and especially the question "why?", because what people ask for is not always what they need.

The second is analysis. Once the information is collected, the BA looks for patterns, gaps, conflicts, and priorities. For example, the sales team might want one thing, and the finance team something that contradicts it. The BA helps resolve those conflicts and decide what matters most.

The third is documenting requirements, in a way that's clear for everyone. Depending on the project, this might be user stories, like "As a customer, I want to track my order, so that I know when it will arrive," with clear acceptance criteria. It might be use cases, process diagrams showing how work flows from one step to the next, or simple wireframes, which are sketches of what a screen might look like.

The fourth is communication and validation. The BA constantly checks with stakeholders: "Is this what you meant?" They answer developers' questions during development, and they help testers understand what "correct" looks like.

And the fifth is supporting the solution. That includes helping with user acceptance testing, preparing training materials, and after launch, checking whether the solution actually delivered the expected value.

Which skills does a good BA need?

The most important skills are not technical. Communication comes first: listening carefully, asking good questions, and explaining complex ideas simply, to both a CEO and a developer. Next comes analytical thinking: breaking big, messy problems into clear, logical pieces. Then there's curiosity, and a healthy habit of asking "why?" until you reach the real need.

But technical understanding matters too. A BA doesn't usually write production code, but it helps a lot to understand how software is built, how databases work, and what is easy or difficult for developers. Many BAs also use basic SQL to explore data, and tools for diagrams, wireframes, and managing requirements.

And finally, domain knowledge. A BA in a bank needs to understand banking. A BA in healthcare needs to understand how hospitals work. That's why many BAs specialize in one industry over time.

People often ask how the BA role differs from similar roles. The differences are worth a minute.

A project manager focuses on how and when the project is delivered: the plan, the budget, the schedule, the risks. A BA focuses on what should be built and why.

A product owner, in agile teams, is responsible for deciding priorities and maximizing the value of the product. In some companies, the BA supports the product owner. In others, especially smaller ones, the same person does both roles.

And a developer builds the solution, while the BA makes sure everyone agrees on what the solution should do.

So, what does a BA career look like?

Many people start as a junior BA, often coming from IT, business, economics, or even from testing or customer support roles. Over time, you can grow into a senior or lead BA, handling larger and more complex projects. From there, many BAs move into roles like product owner, product manager, solution consultant, or project manager, because the skills overlap a lot.

If you're interested in this path, there are also internationally recognized certifications, such as those from the International Institute of Business Analysis, known as IIBA. They're not required to get started, but they can help you structure your learning and show your commitment.

Before I finish, let me clear up two common myths.

Myth one: "A BA just writes documents." Not true. Documents are a tool, not the goal. The real value of a BA is in understanding problems deeply and helping people agree on the right solution. Many of the most important moments in a BA's work happen in conversations, not in documents.

Myth two: "You need to be a great programmer to be a BA." Also not true. Technical understanding helps, but your ability to listen, think clearly, and communicate matters much more.

So, if you're a student wondering whether this role might suit you, here are a few questions to ask yourself. Do you enjoy understanding how things work, and why people do what they do? Do you like solving puzzles, but also talking to people? Do friends often come to you to help them explain or organize their ideas? If you answered yes, the BA path might be a great fit for you.

And here's how you can start today: practice writing user stories for apps you use every day, learn basic SQL and simple diagramming, and in your next group project, volunteer to be the person who talks with the "client" and clarifies the requirements.

The core idea: a business analyst is a bridge between business and technology. They discover what people really need, analyze and document it clearly, and make sure the final solution actually solves the problem. It's a role that combines communication, analysis, and technology, and it's one of the most valuable roles in any software project, because building the right thing matters just as much as building the thing right.
