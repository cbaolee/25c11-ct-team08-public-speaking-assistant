And I'd like to begin with something I truly believe: if you're here today, your child is lucky. You're taking the time to understand their choices, not just to approve or reject them. That already makes a big difference.

Parents whose children want to study IT are in many different places. Some are excited. Many are worried. And almost all of them ask similar questions. So today, I'd like to go through the eight concerns that come up most often, and answer each one as plainly as I can. I won't tell you that IT is perfect, because no career is. But I hope to give you a clearer, calmer picture, so you can have a good conversation with your child when you get home. After that, I'll share some practical ways you can support your child, whatever they decide.

Concern number one: "Is IT just a trend? Isn't the market already full of IT graduates?"

This is a very reasonable worry. You may have read news about technology companies laying off staff, or heard that many graduates struggle to find their first job.

The picture is mixed. Technology is not a trend. It has become part of every industry: banking, healthcare, retail, manufacturing, education, government, and agriculture. Almost every organization now needs people who can build, run, secure, and improve digital systems. That long-term need is not going away.

At the same time, the job market does go through ups and downs, and the entry level has become more competitive in recent years. Companies are more selective. They don't just want a diploma. They want graduates who can actually do the work.

So what does this mean for your child? It means that the students who do well are those who use their university years actively: building real projects, doing internships, improving their English, and developing teamwork and communication skills. A student who does all of this has strong prospects. A student who only attends classes and passes exams may find it harder. In other words, the field has a future, but success is not automatic.

Concern number two: "Won't artificial intelligence replace programmers?"

AI tools can now write simple code, answer technical questions, and speed up many tasks. That's real, and it is changing how IT people work. Some simple, repetitive tasks will need fewer people.

But let me give you an analogy. When calculators and spreadsheet software became common, many people thought accountants would disappear. Instead, accountants stopped doing so much manual arithmetic and focused more on analysis, advice, and decision-making. Something similar is happening in IT. Someone still needs to understand what the business needs, design the system, check whether the AI's code is correct and secure, connect everything together, and take responsibility when something goes wrong.

So the question is not really "Will there be IT jobs?" but "What kind of IT professional will be needed?" The answer is: people with strong fundamentals, good judgment, and the ability to use AI tools well. That's exactly what a good education, and a curious, hard-working student, can provide.

Concern number three: "My child isn't very good at math. Can they still succeed?"

It depends on the area, but in most cases, yes.

Some fields, like artificial intelligence, data science, and certain research areas, do require strong mathematics. But many IT roles, such as web development, testing, IT support, user experience design, or business analysis, rely more on logical thinking, attention to detail, and communication than on advanced math.

Concern number four: "My daughter wants to study IT. Is it a good field for girls?"

Absolutely. There's no evidence that girls are less capable in IT. Some of the most important pioneers of computing were women, and today women work successfully in every area of the field, as developers, data scientists, designers, security experts, and leaders.

It's true that, in many countries, women are still a minority in IT classes and teams. Sometimes that can feel isolating, so encouragement from family matters a lot. Many universities and companies now actively support women in technology through clubs, mentoring programs, and scholarships. If your daughter is interested, please encourage her. The field needs her perspective.

Concern number five: "Will my child spend their whole life in front of a screen? What about their health? And isn't IT just an excuse to play games?"

These are fair concerns. Most IT work does involve long hours at a computer. That's why it's important to build healthy habits early: regular breaks, exercise, good posture, enough sleep, and time with family and friends offline.

As for games, there's a big difference between playing games and building software. If your child says they want to study IT, a simple and kind way to check their motivation is to ask: "What would you like to build?" and encourage them to try a free beginner programming course. If they enjoy creating things, even when it's difficult, that's a very good sign. If they lose interest as soon as it stops being entertainment, that's useful information too, for both of you.

Concern number six: "Is studying IT worth the cost?"

University is a significant investment, especially for programs with higher tuition. I'd encourage you to think about it in two ways.

First, compare the total cost over four years, including living costs, not just the first year's tuition. And look at scholarships and financial support, which many universities offer.

Second, the return on the investment depends largely on how your child uses those four years. It's the reason I keep emphasizing projects, internships, and skills. They turn a degree into a career.

Concern number seven: "Is IT a stable career? Wouldn't a more traditional, secure job be better?"

The truth is that IT careers are usually less about having one job for life, and more about having skills that are valued in many places. A person with strong IT skills can work in a bank, a hospital, a factory, a startup, a government agency, or a foreign company, and sometimes even remotely. In that sense, IT skills can provide a different kind of security: not the security of one employer, but the security of being useful to many.

That said, IT people do need to keep learning throughout their careers, because technology changes. A child who enjoys learning will see that as exciting. A child who strongly prefers routine may find it tiring, and that's worth discussing honestly.

And concern number eight: "What if my child is only choosing IT because their friends are, or because they heard it pays well?"

This is an important question, and it's worth exploring gently. Following friends or salary trends is not a strong enough reason to spend four years studying something. Students who are genuinely interested tend to work harder, recover from difficulties better, and enjoy their careers more.

So, instead of saying "yes" or "no," try asking open questions. "What part of IT interests you?" "Which job would you like to do?" "Have you tried programming? How did it feel?" And encourage them to test their interest before choosing: a short online course, a summer workshop, a conversation with someone who works in IT, or a visit to a university Open Day.

There are practical ways you can support your child, whatever path they choose.

First, listen before you decide. Ask your child to explain why they're interested in IT, and listen without interrupting. You might be surprised by how much they've already thought about it.

Second, explore together. Read about different IT jobs together. Visit an Open Day together. If you know someone who works in IT, a relative, a neighbor, a friend's son or daughter, ask if your child can talk with them for half an hour. Real conversations are often more valuable than any brochure.

Third, encourage creating, not just consuming. If your child spends time on a computer, encourage them to build something: a small game, a website, a useful tool. Show interest in what they create, even if you don't understand the technical details. Your encouragement matters more than your expertise.

And fourth, support English learning. It's one of the most valuable investments for any IT career.

A common complaint from parents: "I'd like to talk with my child about this, but every time I try, it turns into an argument." So, a simple way to have that conversation.

Choose a relaxed moment, not right after a bad exam result, and not in front of other relatives. Start with curiosity rather than conclusions. Instead of "IT is too risky," try: "Tell me what you find interesting about IT." Listen fully before sharing your view.

Then share your concerns honestly, as concerns, not as decisions. For example: "I'm a bit worried about the job market. Can we look into it together?" This invites your child to find answers with you, instead of defending themselves against you.

Next, agree on a small, concrete step. For example, your child completes a free beginner course over the next month, or you visit two universities together, or they talk to someone who works in IT. Small experiments give both of you real information, which is much more useful than arguing about guesses.

And finally, agree to talk again after that step. A decision this important doesn't have to be made in one evening. A series of calm conversations almost always leads to a better decision, and a stronger relationship, than one big argument.
