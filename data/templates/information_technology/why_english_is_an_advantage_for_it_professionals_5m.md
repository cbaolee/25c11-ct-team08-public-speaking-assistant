Hi everyone. I'm {{ argument_speaker_name }}, and I'd like to convince you, in just a few minutes, that English might be the most valuable skill you can add to your IT toolkit, even more than the next programming language.

A simple fact: the world of technology runs in English.

Programming languages use English keywords: if, else, while, return. The official documentation for almost every major framework, library, and cloud service is written first, and sometimes only, in English. Error messages are in English. Most of the answers you'll find online when you're stuck are in English. And the newest information, release notes, research papers, and conference talks, usually appears in English long before it's translated, if it ever is.

So, a developer who reads English comfortably learns faster, solves problems faster, and stays up to date more easily. That's advantage number one: access to knowledge. Think about the last time you were stuck on an error. The fastest answer was probably on a developer forum or in an official issue tracker, written in English, sometimes just a few hours after the problem first appeared. A developer who can read that comfortably fixes the problem in minutes. One who has to wait for a translated tutorial may wait for weeks, or never find it at all.

Advantage number two: opportunities. Many companies work with international clients or are part of global teams. Developers who can join meetings, write clear messages, and explain technical ideas in English are often the ones chosen for more interesting projects, promoted to lead roles, or offered positions abroad or remote jobs with international companies. {{ argument_personal_example }}

And advantage number three: communication is part of engineering. Writing a clear bug report, a good pull request description, or technical documentation is a core professional skill. In global teams, all of that happens in English. Imagine a daily meeting with colleagues in three countries. The developer who can explain, in two clear sentences, what's blocking them and what they need gets help the same day. The one who stays silent because they're unsure of their English often waits, and the whole team slows down. Over months, that difference shapes who is trusted with bigger responsibilities.

Now, the good news: you don't need perfect English. You need useful English. Many excellent engineers in international teams have strong accents and make small grammar mistakes, and nobody minds. What matters is being clear: short sentences, the right technical words, and the confidence to ask, "Could you repeat that?" when you don't understand. Four simple habits will build it.

First, read official documentation in English, even when a translation exists. Start slowly. It gets easier every week.

Second, write in English as part of your daily work: commit messages, code comments, and notes.

Third, listen to technical talks and podcasts in English, with subtitles at first.

And fourth, practice speaking: explain your project in English to a friend, record yourself, or join an English-speaking meetup. It will feel uncomfortable at first. That's normal.

A small bonus habit: keep a personal list of technical words and phrases you meet, like "deprecated," "backward compatible," or "race condition," with one example sentence each. Ten minutes a week with that list will make documentation, meetings, and job interviews noticeably easier within a few months.

Programming languages change every few years. English stays useful for your entire career. So start today. Read one page of documentation in English tonight.
