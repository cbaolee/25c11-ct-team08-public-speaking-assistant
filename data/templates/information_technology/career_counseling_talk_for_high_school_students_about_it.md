Whatever the reason, I hope that by the end of today, you'll have a much clearer picture of what a career in IT really looks like, so you can decide for yourself whether it's for you.

Today's journey has a few stops. We'll clear up some common myths about IT, discover how IT is hidden in almost everything around you, and explore the different jobs in IT, because there are many more than you think. We'll talk about the skills you need, and what you can start doing right now, in high school, and we'll look at the challenges, because a fair picture includes them. And finally, I'll give you a simple action plan for the next few months, and answer your questions.

Let's start with the myths.

Myth number one: "IT means sitting alone in a dark room typing code all day."

You've probably seen this in movies: a person in a hoodie, alone, typing very fast on a black screen full of green text. In reality, most IT work is teamwork. Developers spend a lot of time talking with each other, with designers, with customers, and with managers. They discuss ideas, draw diagrams on whiteboards, and solve problems together. Communication is one of the most important skills in IT. And most offices have the lights on.

Myth number two: "You have to be a math genius."

Math helps, and for some areas, like artificial intelligence or data science, it's really important. But for many IT jobs, what matters most is logical thinking: breaking a big problem into small steps, and thinking clearly about what should happen in each situation. If you can follow a recipe, plan a trip, or figure out the rules of a new game, you're already using that kind of thinking. And logical thinking can be trained, just like a muscle.

Myth number three: "IT is only for boys."

Absolutely not. In fact, one of the first people in history to write what we'd now call a computer program was a woman, Ada Lovelace, back in the eighteen forties. Today, women work in every area of IT, as developers, designers, data scientists, security experts, and leaders of major technology companies. Teams with different kinds of people build better products, because they understand more kinds of users. So if you're a girl interested in technology, this field needs you.

Myth number four: "If I love playing games, I'll love working in IT."

Maybe! But playing games and making games, or making software, are very different. Playing is about enjoying the result. Building is about patience, solving problems, and fixing things that don't work. Some gamers become great developers. Others discover they prefer playing. The only way to know is to try building something yourself, and I'll show you how later.

And myth number five: "Artificial intelligence will do all the programming, so there won't be any IT jobs left."

AI tools are definitely changing how people work in IT. They can write simple code, explain errors, and save a lot of time. But someone still needs to understand the problem, design the solution, check whether the AI's answer is correct, make sure it's secure, and decide what to build in the first place. So the jobs are changing, but people who understand both technology and real-world problems will still be very much needed. In fact, learning how to work well with AI will be one of the most valuable skills of your generation.

Now, let's talk about where IT is in your daily life.

Think about your morning. Your alarm went off on your phone. Maybe you checked messages, scrolled through social media, or watched a short video. Maybe you ordered breakfast through a delivery app, or paid for it by scanning a QR code. Maybe you took a ride-hailing motorbike to school, and the app showed you the driver's location in real time.

Every one of those moments was built by teams of IT people. Someone designed the screen you tapped. Someone wrote the code that sends your message across the country in less than a second. Someone built the system that calculates the price of your ride. Someone protects your bank account from hackers. And someone analyzes millions of data points to decide which video to show you next.

And it's not just apps. Hospitals use IT to store medical records and analyze X-rays. Farmers use sensors and data to decide when to water their crops. Banks, airlines, factories, schools, and government offices all run on software. That's why IT people work in almost every industry, not just in technology companies.

What about the jobs themselves? When most people hear "IT," they think of just one job: programmer. But the IT world is like a big city with many different neighborhoods. Let me introduce you to eight of them. As I describe each one, think about which ones sound interesting to you.

The first is the software developer, also called a software engineer. Developers write the code that makes apps, websites, and systems work. Some focus on what you see on the screen, called front-end developers. Others work on the logic and data behind the scenes, called back-end developers. And some build mobile apps or games. A typical day might include writing code, fixing bugs, and discussing with the team how to build a new feature.

The second is the UI and UX designer. UI stands for user interface, and UX stands for user experience. These designers decide how an app looks and how it feels to use. Where should the button go? What color should it be? How can we make this so easy that even your grandparents can use it? If you love both art and technology, and you're good at understanding how people think, this could be your neighborhood.

The third is the data analyst or data scientist. These people find meaning in data. For example: which products sell best on rainy days? Which students might need extra help? Which song should we recommend next? If you like math, patterns, and asking "why?", this is a great fit.

The fourth is the artificial intelligence engineer. They build systems that can recognize images, understand language, or make predictions, like the AI in translation apps or chatbots. This field needs strong math and programming, and it's one of the fastest-growing areas.

The fifth is the cybersecurity specialist. These are the digital guardians. They protect systems and data from hackers, investigate attacks, and sometimes even hack into systems, with permission, to find weaknesses before criminals do. If you enjoy puzzles, detective stories, and thinking like an attacker while staying on the good side, this might be for you.

The sixth is the network and cloud engineer. They build and maintain the "roads" that data travels on, and the huge data centers and cloud systems that keep apps running day and night. Without them, nothing works.

The seventh is the tester, also called a quality assurance engineer. Testers try to find problems before users do. They think about every way something could go wrong. If you're the kind of person who always notices small mistakes, you might be great at this.

And the eighth is the business analyst or product manager. These people stand between the customers and the technical team. They figure out what users really need and decide what should be built next. If you're good at communicating, organizing, and seeing the big picture, but you don't necessarily want to code all day, this could be your path.

And there are many more: game developers, technical writers, IT teachers, IT project managers, technology entrepreneurs. The point is that IT isn't one job. It's a whole world of jobs, for many different personalities and talents.

To make these jobs feel more real, let me take you through a typical day for two imagined professionals.

Meet Lan, a back-end developer at an online shopping company. Her day starts at nine with a fifteen-minute stand-up meeting, where everyone on her team quickly shares what they did yesterday, what they're doing today, and whether anything is blocking them. Today, Lan is building a new feature that lets customers track their parcels in real time. She spends the morning writing code, and around eleven, she gets stuck on a strange error. She tries a few things, searches online, and finally asks a senior colleague, who helps her spot the problem in five minutes. After lunch, she reviews a teammate's code and leaves some suggestions. In the afternoon, she joins a short meeting with a designer and a product manager to discuss how the tracking screen should look. Before she leaves, she writes automated tests to make sure her code works, and sends it for review. Tomorrow, if everything passes, thousands of customers will be able to use her feature.

Now meet Nam, a cybersecurity analyst at a bank. His day starts by checking a dashboard that shows alerts from the bank's systems. Most alerts are harmless, but one looks unusual: someone has tried to log in to an employee's account many times from another country. Nam investigates, confirms it's a password-guessing attack, blocks the source, and makes sure the employee changes their password and turns on two-step verification. Later, he helps prepare a training session to teach bank staff how to recognize scam emails. In the afternoon, he reads about a newly discovered software weakness and checks whether the bank's systems are affected. It's a quiet day, which, in cybersecurity, is a good day.

Notice what both of them have in common. Neither of them spends the whole day alone typing code. Both of them solve problems, ask for help, learn something new, and work with other people. That's what most IT jobs actually look like.

What skills do you need to succeed in IT? They fall into three kinds.

The first kind is thinking skills. Logical thinking, which we already talked about. Problem solving: not giving up when something doesn't work, but trying different approaches. And curiosity: the habit of asking "how does this work?" and "can I make it better?"

The second kind is technical skills. Programming, of course, and depending on your path, things like math, databases, design tools, or networking. But here's the good news: you don't need to know any of this yet. Universities and courses will teach you. What matters now is building your foundation and your interest.

The third kind is people skills, and many students are surprised by this. Communication: explaining your ideas clearly, both speaking and writing. Teamwork: working well with others, including people who think differently from you. And English. In IT, English is extremely important, because most documentation, tools, online courses, and international teams use English. Many IT professionals say that improving their English was one of the best investments they ever made.

So, what can you do right now, while you're still in high school?

First, take your math and English seriously. You don't have to be the best in the class, but these two subjects will make your future studies much easier.

Second, try programming for real. There are many free and beginner-friendly ways to start. If you've never coded before, block-based tools like Scratch, created by MIT, let you build games and animations by dragging and dropping blocks, without worrying about typing mistakes. When you're ready for real code, Python is a great first language: it's simple to read and used everywhere, from websites to artificial intelligence. There are many free online courses and websites with small programming challenges.

Third, build something small that you care about. A simple game. A website about your favorite band. A program that calculates your grade average. A quiz for your classmates. It doesn't need to be perfect. Building something real teaches you more than watching a hundred videos.

Fourth, join activities. School IT clubs, programming competitions, informatics olympiads, science fairs, hackathons for students, or summer coding camps. These are great for learning, making friends with similar interests, and showing universities that you're genuinely motivated.

And fifth, use technology as a creator, not just as a consumer. Every time you use an app, ask yourself: how would I build this? What would I change? That small habit trains you to think like an IT professional.

Now, the challenges of working in IT. Every career has some, and you deserve to hear them.

First, you never stop learning. Technology changes very fast. The tools people used a decade ago are very different from the ones used today. If you love learning new things, that's exciting. If you prefer to learn something once and use it forever, it can be tiring.

Second, it can be frustrating. Sometimes you'll spend hours, even days, trying to find one small mistake in your code. Patience is essential. The good news is that the feeling when you finally solve it is amazing.

Third, the work involves a lot of sitting and screen time, so you have to take care of your health: exercise, take breaks, and protect your eyes and your back.

Fourth, getting your first job takes effort. IT is a popular field, so you'll need to stand out through real projects, internships, and good skills, not just a diploma.

And fifth, as we discussed, AI and other changes mean that some tasks will disappear and new ones will appear. The people who do best are those who stay curious and adaptable.

I'm not telling you this to scare you. Every career has challenges. I'm telling you so you can make an informed choice.

Mostly A: you might enjoy software development, building apps, websites, or games. Mostly B: you might enjoy UI and UX design, or product roles, where creativity meets technology. Mostly C: you might enjoy data science, data analysis, or artificial intelligence. Mostly D: you might enjoy cybersecurity.

And if your answers were mixed, that's great too. Many IT jobs combine these areas, and many people move between them during their careers. Of course, this is just a fun quiz, not a scientific test. But I hope it gives you a starting point to explore.

Before the action plan, five questions that high school students ask again and again.

The first: "Is it too late to start if I've never coded and I'm already in my last year of high school?" Not at all. Many IT professionals wrote their first line of code at university, or even later. Universities start from the basics. Starting a little earlier simply makes your first semester more comfortable.

The second: "Do I need an expensive computer?" No. For learning to program, an ordinary laptop is enough. Many beginner tools even run directly in a web browser. Later, for specific areas like game development or artificial intelligence, you may need more powerful hardware, but universities often provide labs and cloud resources for that.

The third: "Should I learn a programming language before university? Which one?" It's a good idea, but not required. If you want to start, Python is a friendly first choice. The most important thing isn't the language. It's learning how to think step by step, and that transfers to every language you'll learn later.

The fourth: "Can I still study IT if I'm stronger in languages or arts than in math and science?" Yes, especially in roles like user experience design, technical writing, product management, or business analysis, which combine technology with communication and creativity. You'll still need to handle some math at university, but your other strengths can become real advantages.

And the fifth: "Will I be able to work from home, or work for foreign companies?" Many IT jobs offer some flexibility, and some companies hire remote workers from other countries. But that usually comes after you've built solid skills, experience, and good English. It's a reward for growing, not a starting point.

So, let me give you a simple action plan for the next three months.

Month one: explore. Look up three IT jobs from today's list that interested you. Watch a "day in the life" video for each one, or better yet, ask someone who works in that field. Write down what you like and don't like about each.

Month two: try. Complete one free beginner programming course, for example, with Scratch or Python. Aim for just thirty minutes, three or four times a week.

Month three: build and share. Create one small project, anything you like, and show it to a friend, a family member, or a teacher. Ask for their feedback.

At the end of these three months, ask yourself one question: "Did I enjoy this, even when it was hard?" If the answer is yes, IT might be a wonderful path for you. And if the answer is no, that's valuable too. You've learned something important about yourself, and you can explore other paths with confidence.

Before I go, let me remind you of what we talked about today. IT is not about sitting alone in a dark room. It's teamwork, creativity, and problem solving. It's hidden in almost everything you do, and it offers many different jobs, for builders, designers, analysts, guardians, and communicators. To prepare, focus on math, English, logical thinking, and curiosity, and start building small things now. And remember that the field has real challenges, like constant learning and frustration, but also huge rewards.

Most importantly, you don't have to decide your whole future today. You just have to take the next small step.

Now, your questions. Don't be shy: if you're wondering about something, someone else in the room is wondering about it too.
