Four years, or a few months? That question is asked more and more often, by high school graduates, by university students thinking about dropping out, and by people who want to switch careers: "Do I really need a four-year university degree to work in IT? Or can I just do a coding bootcamp?" Good {{ argument_time_of_day }}, everyone. I'm {{ argument_speaker_name }}, and {{ argument_speaker_background }}

It's a fair question. Over the past fifteen years, coding bootcamps have grown around the world, promising to turn beginners into job-ready developers in just a few months. At the same time, university degrees take four years and cost a lot of money and time. So which path makes more sense?

The answer is: it depends on who you are, where you're starting from, and where you want to go. So instead of giving you a single verdict, I'd like to give you a fair comparison, a few realistic scenarios, and a checklist you can use to make your own decision.

Before any comparison, it helps to be sure we're comparing the right things.

A university degree in IT, such as Computer Science, Software Engineering, or Information Systems, usually takes about four years. It combines programming with mathematics, theory, and broader subjects: algorithms, data structures, operating systems, networks, databases, software engineering, and often electives in areas like artificial intelligence or security. You also take general education courses, do group projects, and often an internship and a final thesis.

A coding bootcamp is a short, intensive training program, typically lasting somewhere between three and nine months, full-time or part-time. Bootcamps focus on practical, job-ready skills for a specific role, most commonly web development, but also data analysis, UX design, or cybersecurity. You learn a specific set of tools, build several projects, and often receive career support, such as help with your portfolio, your CV, and interview practice.

In a sentence: a degree aims to give you a broad, deep foundation for a long career. A bootcamp aims to get you into a specific first job as quickly as possible.

Now let's look at the strengths and weaknesses of each.

On the university side, the first strength is depth. Universities teach you why things work, not just how to use today's tools. Algorithms, data structures, and how computers actually work may feel abstract at first, but they're exactly what lets you understand new technologies quickly ten years from now, when today's popular frameworks have been replaced.

The second strength is breadth and options. A degree opens doors to many paths: software development, but also research, artificial intelligence, data science, security, systems, or further study. Some fields, especially research and advanced AI roles, strongly prefer, or require, a degree, often a master's or a PhD.

The third is recognition. Some employers, especially large corporations, government organizations, and some multinational companies, still require a degree for certain positions. And if you ever want to work abroad, many countries' work visa rules consider whether you have a degree in a relevant field.

The fourth is time to grow. Four years gives you time to explore, make mistakes, join clubs and competitions, build friendships and networks, and mature, which is especially valuable if you're starting right after high school.

But a degree has real weaknesses, too. It takes four years, and it costs tuition and living expenses during that time, plus the income you're not earning. Some courses can feel distant from day-to-day industry work, and some curricula don't keep up with the latest tools. And a degree doesn't automatically make you job-ready. Students who only attend classes and pass exams, without building projects or doing internships, often struggle to find their first job.

Now, let's look at the coding bootcamp.

The first strength is speed. In a few months, you can go from beginner to having a portfolio of working projects. For someone who needs to start earning soon, or who is changing careers in their late twenties or thirties, that's a huge advantage.

The second strength is practical focus. Bootcamps teach the tools and workflows used in real companies right now, and you spend most of your time building things, often in teams, with deadlines, which feels much closer to a real job.

The third is career support. Good bootcamps help you prepare your portfolio, practice technical interviews, and sometimes connect you directly with hiring companies.

But bootcamps have significant weaknesses as well.

The biggest one is depth. In a few months, there's simply no time to cover fundamentals like algorithms, data structures, or how systems work in detail. Many bootcamp graduates find that they need to keep studying these on their own, especially when they want to move into more senior or more technical roles.

The second is intensity. Bootcamps are demanding. Learning a whole new field in a few months requires long hours and strong self-discipline, and not everyone keeps up.

The third is that quality varies enormously. Some bootcamps are excellent, with experienced instructors and strong employer networks. Others are mostly marketing. Be very careful with promises like "guaranteed job," and always read the conditions in the small print.

And the fourth is the job market itself. In recent years, competition for entry-level developer jobs has become tougher in many markets. That affects everyone, but candidates without a degree and with only a few months of experience can find it particularly challenging to get past the first screening. A bootcamp can get you ready to compete, but it can't guarantee that the market will be waiting for you.

Three scenarios make this more concrete.

Scenario one: an eighteen-year-old who has just finished high school, is interested in technology, and whose family can support four years of study. For this person, I'd generally recommend a university degree. They have time, the degree keeps the most doors open, and they can add practical skills along the way through projects, internships, and clubs. If they want, they can even take a short bootcamp-style course during a summer break to boost their practical skills.

Scenario two: a twenty-eight-year-old who works in marketing, already has a degree in another field, and wants to become a front-end developer. For this person, a good bootcamp, or a structured self-study program, can make a lot of sense. They already have a degree, which helps with employers and visas, plus professional experience and soft skills. What they need is focused, practical training and a portfolio, and they probably can't afford to stop working for four years.

Scenario three: a second-year IT student who feels their university courses are too theoretical and is thinking of dropping out to join a bootcamp. Here, I'd usually advise caution. The student is already halfway to a degree. In most cases, it's better to finish the degree and fill the practical gap with personal projects, internships, online courses, or a part-time bootcamp, rather than give up the degree entirely.

These are just examples, of course. But notice the pattern: the right answer depends on age, existing education, financial situation, and goals.

So, if you're considering a bootcamp, here are five questions you should always ask before you pay.

The first question: what are the real outcomes? Ask for data: how many graduates found a job in the field within six months, what kinds of roles, and how was that number calculated? Be suspicious of numbers without explanations.

The second: who are the instructors? Do they have real industry experience?

The third: what exactly is in the curriculum, and how much time is spent building real projects?

The fourth: what does career support actually include?

And the fifth: what are the payment terms, refund policy, and conditions of any "job guarantee"?

And if you're choosing a university, ask similar questions: what does the curriculum look like, what do recent graduates do, and how does the university connect students with industry?

Now, let's look at this from the other side of the table. What do employers actually look for when they hire someone for an entry-level IT role?

Hiring managers tend to give a surprisingly consistent answer. First, evidence that you can actually build things: real projects, ideally ones you can explain in depth, including the problems you faced and the decisions you made. Second, solid fundamentals: can you reason about a problem, write clean code, and understand basic concepts like data structures, databases, and how the web works? Third, the ability to learn quickly, because whatever tools you know today, the company will probably use something slightly different. And fourth, soft skills: communication, teamwork, and how you respond to feedback.

Notice that none of these are guaranteed by a degree, and none of them are guaranteed by a bootcamp. The degree or the bootcamp certificate may help you get an interview. But what happens in the interview depends on what you can actually do.

So whichever path you choose, here's how to make it work.

If you choose a university degree, don't wait until your final year to become job-ready. From your first or second year, build personal projects outside class. Take part in hackathons, coding competitions, and student clubs. Contribute to an open-source project, even with small fixes. Look for internships early, even short or unpaid ones at first if that's realistic for you. And take your fundamentals seriously. Those "boring" theory courses are exactly what make you stand out later.

If you choose a bootcamp, prepare before day one. Many successful bootcamp students learn the basics of programming for free online before they start, so they can keep up with the pace. During the bootcamp, treat it like a full-time job, and go beyond the minimum on every project. And after you graduate, keep going: continue building projects, study the fundamentals the bootcamp didn't have time for, and stay active in the developer community. The first job search after a bootcamp can take months, and persistence is often what separates the people who make it from those who give up.

One last thought: I'd like to challenge the idea that this is an either-or choice. Increasingly, people combine paths. University students take practical online courses or summer bootcamps. Bootcamp graduates continue with online degrees or self-study in computer science fundamentals. And people already working in IT keep learning through certifications and courses throughout their careers.

So, what's the verdict? A university degree offers depth, breadth, and wide recognition, at the cost of time and money, and it works best when combined with practical experience. A coding bootcamp offers speed, practical focus, and career support, but with less depth, high intensity, and very variable quality. A degree tends to suit young people starting out and those aiming for research or highly technical roles. A bootcamp tends to suit career changers who already have education and work experience and need a fast, focused route.

And whatever path you choose, remember this: in IT, nobody stops learning after graduation or after a bootcamp. The path you choose is just your starting point. What matters most is your curiosity, your discipline, and the projects you build along the way.

Whichever path you choose, choose it with your eyes open.
