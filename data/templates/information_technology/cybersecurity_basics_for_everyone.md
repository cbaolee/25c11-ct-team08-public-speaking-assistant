A short text message arrives on your phone: "Your parcel could not be delivered. Please confirm your address within 24 hours using this link." Would you click it? Every day, somewhere in the world, people do, and some of them lose their savings because of it. Good {{ time_of_day }}, everyone. I'm {{ speaker_name }}, and today we're going to talk about cybersecurity. But don't worry: this isn't a talk for hackers or IT experts. This is a talk for everyone who has a phone, an email address, a bank account, or a social media profile. In other words, all of us.

A show of hands, please. How many of you think that hackers would never bother attacking you, because you're not rich, famous, or important enough?

That's one of the most common, and most dangerous, beliefs about cybersecurity. Here's the reality: most attacks today are not targeted at one special person. They're automated. Criminals send millions of fake emails and messages, and try millions of stolen passwords, using software that runs day and night. They're not looking for you specifically. They're looking for anyone who makes a mistake. Your email account, your social media profile, your bank app, and even your contact list all have value to them.

The good news is that you don't need to be a technical expert to protect yourself. A handful of simple habits can block the vast majority of attacks. Today, I'll share six of them, and by the end, I hope you'll go home and put at least two or three into practice.

Habit number one: use strong and unique passwords.

Every year, security companies publish lists of the most common passwords, and every year, the top of the list looks almost the same: "one two three four five six," "password," "qwerty." Passwords like these can be guessed in less than a second.

What makes a strong password? The most important factor is length. A long password is far harder to crack than a short, complicated one. One easy method is to use a passphrase: several random words strung together, like "purple tiger drinks coffee on Mondays." It's long, easy for you to remember, and very hard for a computer to guess.

But strength is only half the story. The other half is uniqueness. And this is where most people go wrong. Say you use the same password for a small online shop, your email, and your bank. One day, that small shop gets hacked, and its list of passwords is leaked. Criminals will immediately try that email and password combination on email services, banks, and social networks. This attack is called credential stuffing, and it's extremely common. If you reuse passwords, one leak can open every door you have.

"But I can't remember fifty different passwords!" you might say. And you're right, nobody can. A password manager solves exactly this. A password manager is an app that creates strong, unique passwords for every account and remembers them for you. You only need to remember one strong master password. Many browsers and phones now have a password manager built in, and there are also dedicated, well-known ones.

Habit number two: turn on two-factor authentication.

If I could ask you to do only one thing after this talk, it would be this.

Two-factor authentication, sometimes called multi-factor authentication or two-step verification, means that logging in requires two things: something you know, like your password, and something you have, like your phone. So even if a criminal steals your password, they still can't get in without your phone.

There are a few types. A code sent by text message is better than nothing. A code from an authenticator app is stronger, because text messages can sometimes be intercepted or redirected. And many services now support passkeys, which let you log in with your fingerprint or face on your device, without typing a password at all. They're both easier and much harder to phish.

Start with your most important accounts: your main email, because it can be used to reset all your other passwords, then your banking apps, and your social media.

And one golden rule here: never, ever share a verification code with anyone. No real bank, company, or government office will call you and ask you to read out the code you just received. If someone asks, it's a scam. Every time.

Habit number three: learn to recognize phishing.

Phishing is when criminals pretend to be someone you trust, a bank, a delivery company, your boss, even a friend, to trick you into clicking a link, opening an attachment, sending money, or giving away your information. It's the most common starting point for cyberattacks, both against individuals and against large companies.

Phishing messages often share the same warning signs, and four of them matter most.

First, urgency or fear. "Your account will be locked in twenty-four hours." "You have an unpaid fine." "Your package could not be delivered." The goal is to make you panic and act before you think.

Second, something that's too good to be true. "Congratulations, you've won a prize." "You've been selected for a high-paying part-time job."

Third, requests for sensitive information or payment: passwords, verification codes, card numbers, or money transfers.

And fourth, small details that don't look quite right: a sender's address with a strange spelling, a link that leads to a slightly different website name, or unusual grammar.

What should you do when you receive a suspicious message? Pause. Don't click the link. Instead, contact the organization directly, using a phone number or app you already know is real, not the contact details in the message. If the message seems to come from a friend asking for money, call them to check. It takes thirty seconds, and it can save you a lot of money and stress.

A newer twist: criminals are now using artificial intelligence to write more convincing messages, and even to imitate people's voices in phone calls. We can no longer rely only on bad grammar to spot a scam. The habit of pausing and verifying through another channel is more important than ever.

Habit number four: keep your software updated.

We've all done it: we see "update available" and click "remind me tomorrow." And then tomorrow becomes next month.

But updates are not just about new features. Many of them fix security holes that criminals already know about and are actively exploiting. When you delay an update, you leave a door open that the whole world knows about. So turn on automatic updates for your phone, your computer, your browser, and your apps. And be careful to download apps only from official app stores or official websites.

Habit number five: back up your important data.

Imagine your laptop is stolen tomorrow, or your phone falls into a river, or a type of malicious software called ransomware locks all your files and demands payment. Would you lose your photos, your documents, your graduation thesis?

Backups are your safety net. A simple, widely recommended approach is the three-two-one rule: keep three copies of your important data, on two different types of storage, with one copy kept somewhere else, for example in the cloud. And remember to check once in a while that your backup actually works. A backup you've never tested is just a hope.

Habit number six: be careful about what you share, and where you connect.

Criminals often do their homework on social media. Your birthday, your pet's name, your school, your mother's maiden name, the fact that you're on vacation right now: all of these can be used to guess passwords, answer security questions, or make a scam message sound believable. So take a few minutes to review your privacy settings, and think twice before posting personal details publicly.

Also, be careful on public Wi-Fi, like in cafés and airports. Most important websites today use encrypted connections, which helps a lot, but it's still wise to avoid doing sensitive things like online banking on unknown networks, and to watch out for fake networks with names that look official. If you can, use your mobile data instead for anything sensitive.

And if something does go wrong? Maybe you clicked a bad link, or you notice strange activity on your account. First, don't panic, and don't hide it out of embarrassment. Fast action matters more than anything else. Change your password immediately, starting from a device you trust. If it involves your bank, call the bank's official hotline right away to lock your card or account. Turn on two-factor authentication if it wasn't on already. And if it involves a work or school account, report it to your IT team as soon as possible. {{ it_support_contact }}

Remember: security teams would much rather hear about a mistake five minutes after it happens than discover it five weeks later.

Six habits, then. Long, unique passwords, ideally kept in a password manager. Two-factor authentication, with your codes never shared. A pause, and a check through another channel, whenever a message pushes you to hurry. Updates installed, not postponed. Backups that follow the three-two-one rule. And care about what you share online and where you connect.

None of these habits require technical skills. They just require a little attention, and a little time today to set things up.

A small challenge, then. Before you go to sleep tonight, pick just one account, ideally your main email, and turn on two-factor authentication. It takes about five minutes, and it could stop the most common attacks against that account.

Cybersecurity isn't only the job of IT professionals. Every one of us is part of the defense, for ourselves, our families, and the organizations we belong to.
