The agenda for the day.

{{ argument_agenda_details }}

You'll notice that each session follows the same rhythm: a short explanation and live demo from me, then hands-on practice in pairs, and then a quick review where we look at a solution together and discuss common mistakes. That rhythm matters, because research on learning consistently shows that we remember far more of what we actively practice than of what we just hear.

The sessions also build on each other. Everything you write this morning becomes part of the project you'll finish this afternoon, so if you get stuck early, don't quietly give up on that part. Ask for help, or copy in the provided solution and keep going, and come back to the tricky part later if you have time. Finishing the whole project, with a few gaps you understand, is far more valuable than a perfect first exercise and an empty afternoon. And if you finish an exercise early, try the bonus challenge, or help a neighbor.

Now, about working in pairs. For most exercises, you'll work with a partner, using a technique called pair programming. One person, the driver, types the code. The other, the navigator, reviews each line, thinks about the next step, and spots mistakes. Every fifteen minutes or so, you'll switch roles. I know it can feel a bit uncomfortable at first, especially if you're used to working alone. But pair programming is widely used in professional teams, because two people thinking together catch more mistakes and learn faster. And explaining your thinking out loud is one of the best ways to really understand something.

A few ground rules for today, to make sure everyone has a good experience.

First, there are no stupid questions. If you're confused, I can almost guarantee someone else is too. Asking helps everyone.

Second, be kind to your partner. You may have very different levels of experience. If you're more experienced, resist the urge to grab the keyboard. Explain instead. If you're less experienced, don't be afraid to drive. That's exactly how you learn.

Third, errors are your friends. You'll see many error messages today. Don't panic. Read them carefully. They usually tell you exactly what's wrong, and where. Learning to read error messages is one of the most valuable skills you'll practice today.
