I'm {{ argument_speaker_name }}, and {{ argument_speaker_background }} Today, let's compare these two very different ways of working in IT: full-time employment and freelancing.

On social media, freelancing often looks like a dream: working from a beach, choosing your own hours, picking exciting clients, and earning more than your friends in offices. Meanwhile, full-time jobs are sometimes described as boring and restrictive. But the reality is more nuanced. Both paths have real advantages and real costs. And the right choice depends a lot on your skills, your stage of career, your personality, and your financial situation.

So today, I'll compare them across several dimensions, share what it actually takes to succeed as a freelancer, and give you a few questions to help you decide what's right for you, right now.

Let's start with full-time employment.

As a full-time employee, you work for one company, usually with a fixed salary, defined working hours, and a team around you.

The biggest advantage is stability. You know how much you'll earn each month. In many countries, you also receive benefits such as social insurance, health insurance, paid leave, and sometimes bonuses or training budgets.

The second advantage is learning, especially early in your career. In a good company, you work alongside more experienced engineers, receive code reviews, learn established processes, and work on larger systems than you could build alone. For juniors, this kind of structured learning is extremely valuable, and hard to replace.

And the third is career progression: clear paths toward senior, lead, or management roles, and a professional network inside the company.

The downsides? Less control over what you work on, and when. Your income is usually tied to a salary range rather than directly to how much you produce. And you depend on one employer, which, as layoffs in the tech industry have shown, isn't always as secure as it seems.

Now, let's look at freelancing.

As a freelancer, you work independently for multiple clients, usually paid per project or per hour, through your own network or through online freelancing platforms.

The biggest advantage is freedom. You choose your clients, your projects, your schedule, and often your location.

The second is income potential. Experienced freelancers with in-demand skills and a strong reputation can earn more than they would in a salaried job, because they can set their own rates and take on several clients.

And the third is variety. You may work on many different projects and industries, which can be exciting and can broaden your skills quickly.

But the downsides are significant, and often underestimated.

Income is irregular. Some months are full, some are empty. And clients sometimes pay late, or not at all.

There are no automatic benefits. You must plan your own insurance, savings for holidays, sick days, retirement, and taxes. A freelance rate that looks high often turns out to be much less impressive once you subtract all of that.

You're also running a business, not just writing code. You spend a lot of time finding clients, writing proposals, negotiating, handling contracts, invoicing, and managing client expectations. Many freelancers say that a significant part of their working time is unpaid business work.

And it can be isolating. There's no team to brainstorm with, no senior colleague to review your code, and no one to cover for you if you're sick.

So, what does it take to succeed as a freelancer? Five things.

First, strong, proven skills. Clients pay freelancers to solve problems without supervision. That's why freelancing is usually much easier after a few years of professional experience.

Second, a specialty. "I build websites" is crowded. "I build fast online stores for small fashion brands" or "I build booking systems for clinics" is easier to market, and easier to charge well for.

Third, business and communication skills. Writing clear proposals, managing scope, setting expectations, and saying no to unreasonable requests.

Fourth, written contracts. Always agree in writing on scope, timeline, price, payment terms, what happens if requirements change, and who owns the code. Many freelancers ask for part of the payment upfront, and split larger projects into milestones with payment for each.

And fifth, financial discipline. Keep a reserve that can cover several months of living costs, set aside money for taxes, and plan for quiet periods.

A common example shows how a first freelance project can go wrong, and how it could go right.

A developer agrees with a friend of a friend to build a website for a small restaurant. The agreement is made in a short chat: "A simple website with the menu and online booking, for a fixed price." No written scope, no milestones, no upfront payment.

Then the requests start. Can we add online ordering? Can the menu be in two languages? Can it connect to our delivery partner? Each request seems small, so the developer says yes. Two months later, the project is three times bigger than planned, the client is unhappy that it's late, and the developer has earned far less per hour than at their old job. And the final payment is delayed because the client "is still testing."

Now imagine the same project done professionally. Before starting, the developer writes a one-page agreement listing exactly what's included: the pages, the booking feature, one language, and two rounds of design changes. It states the price, a timeline, and payment in three parts: thirty percent upfront, forty percent when the design is approved, and the rest at launch. It also says that new features, like online ordering, will be quoted separately.

When the client asks for online ordering, the developer doesn't say "no." They say: "Great idea. That's not included in our current agreement, but I can add it as a second phase. Here's the estimate." The client feels respected, the developer is paid fairly, and the relationship stays positive.

The technical skills in both versions are identical. The difference is entirely in business skills. And that's the part of freelancing nobody posts on social media.

So, how do you decide? Four questions.

Career stage: where are you in your career? If you're a junior, a full-time role usually offers the mentorship and structured learning that will make you much stronger later. Many successful freelancers started with several years in companies.

Risk: how much financial risk can you handle? If you have family responsibilities or no savings, irregular income may be stressful.

Business appetite: do you enjoy the business side, selling, negotiating, and managing clients? If not, freelancing may feel exhausting.

And working style: how do you work best? With a team and structure, or independently, managing yourself?

{{ argument_local_context_note }}

Weighing it up: full-time employment offers stability, benefits, structured learning, and a team, at the cost of less freedom and less control over your income. Freelancing offers freedom, variety, and higher income potential, at the cost of irregular income, no automatic benefits, business responsibilities, and isolation. For most people, full-time work is a strong foundation early in their career, and freelancing becomes a realistic option once they have proven skills, a specialty, a network, and a financial safety net. And there's no rule that says you have to choose forever. Many people move between the two during their careers.
