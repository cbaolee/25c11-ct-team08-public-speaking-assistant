Let's start with the myths.

Myth number one: "IT means sitting alone in a dark room typing code all day." You've probably seen this in movies: a person in a hoodie, alone, typing very fast on a black screen full of green text. In reality, most IT work is teamwork. Developers spend a lot of time talking with each other, with designers, with customers, and with managers. Communication is one of the most important skills in IT. And most offices have the lights on.

Myth number two: "You have to be a math genius." Math helps, and for some areas, like artificial intelligence or data science, it's really important. But for many IT jobs, what matters most is logical thinking: breaking a big problem into small steps, and thinking clearly about what should happen in each situation. If you can follow a recipe, plan a trip, or figure out the rules of a new game, you're already using that kind of thinking. And it can be trained, just like a muscle.

Myth number three: "IT is only for boys." Absolutely not. One of the first people in history to write what we'd now call a computer program was a woman, Ada Lovelace, back in the eighteen forties. Today, women work in every area of IT, as developers, designers, data scientists, security experts, and leaders of major technology companies. So if you're a girl interested in technology, this field needs you.

Myth number four: "If I love playing games, I'll love working in IT." Maybe! But playing games and making software are very different. Playing is about enjoying the result. Building is about patience, solving problems, and fixing things that don't work. Some gamers become great developers. Others discover they prefer playing. The only way to know is to try building something yourself, and I'll show you how later.

And myth number five: "Artificial intelligence will do all the programming, so there won't be any IT jobs left." AI tools are definitely changing how people work in IT. They can write simple code, explain errors, and save a lot of time. But someone still needs to understand the problem, design the solution, check whether the AI's answer is correct and secure, and decide what to build in the first place. The jobs are changing, but people who understand both technology and real-world problems will still be very much needed. In fact, learning how to work well with AI will be one of the most valuable skills of your generation.

So where is IT in your life? Think about your morning. Your alarm went off on your phone. Maybe you checked messages, watched a short video, or paid for breakfast by scanning a QR code. Every one of those moments was built by teams of IT people. Someone designed the screen you tapped. Someone wrote the code that sends your message across the country in less than a second. Someone protects your bank account from hackers. And it's not just apps: hospitals, farms, banks, airlines, and schools all run on software. That's why IT people work in almost every industry.

What about the jobs themselves? When most people hear "IT," they think of just one job: programmer. But the IT world is like a big city with many different neighborhoods. Let me introduce you to eight of them. As I describe each one, think about which ones sound interesting to you.

The first is the software developer. Developers write the code that makes apps, websites, and systems work. Some focus on what you see on the screen, called front-end developers. Others work on the logic and data behind the scenes, called back-end developers. And some build mobile apps or games.

The second is the UI and UX designer. These designers decide how an app looks and how it feels to use. Where should the button go? How can we make this so easy that even your grandparents can use it? If you love both art and technology, this could be your neighborhood.

The third is the data analyst or data scientist. These people find meaning in data. Which products sell best on rainy days? Which students might need extra help? If you like math, patterns, and asking "why?", this is a great fit.

The fourth is the artificial intelligence engineer. They build systems that recognize images, understand language, or make predictions, like the AI in translation apps or chatbots. This field needs strong math and programming, and it's one of the fastest-growing areas.

The fifth is the cybersecurity specialist. These are the digital guardians. They protect systems and data from hackers, investigate attacks, and sometimes even hack into systems, with permission, to find weaknesses before criminals do. If you enjoy puzzles and detective stories, this might be for you.

The sixth is the network and cloud engineer. They build and maintain the "roads" that data travels on, and the huge data centers that keep apps running day and night.

The seventh is the tester, or quality assurance engineer. Testers try to find problems before users do. If you're the kind of person who always notices small mistakes, you might be great at this.

And the eighth is the business analyst or product manager. These people stand between the customers and the technical team, figuring out what users really need and what should be built next. If you're good at communicating and seeing the big picture, but don't necessarily want to code all day, this could be your path.

And there are many more: game developers, technical writers, IT teachers, technology entrepreneurs. The point is that IT isn't one job. It's a whole world of jobs, for many different personalities and talents.

What skills do you need to succeed? They fall into three kinds. Thinking skills: logical thinking, problem solving, and curiosity, the habit of asking "how does this work?" and "can I make it better?" Technical skills: programming and, depending on your path, things like math, databases, or design tools. The good news is that you don't need to know any of this yet; universities and courses will teach you. And people skills: communication, teamwork, and English. In IT, English is extremely important, because most documentation, tools, and online courses use it.

So, what can you do right now, while you're still in high school?

First, take your math and English seriously. You don't have to be the best in the class, but these two subjects will make your future studies much easier.

Second, try programming for real. If you've never coded before, block-based tools like Scratch, created by MIT, let you build games and animations by dragging and dropping blocks. When you're ready for real code, Python is a great first language: it's simple to read and used everywhere, from websites to artificial intelligence.

Third, build something small that you care about. A simple game. A website about your favorite band. A program that calculates your grade average. Building something real teaches you more than watching a hundred videos.

And fourth, join activities: school IT clubs, programming competitions, informatics olympiads, science fairs, or summer coding camps. They're great for learning, making friends with similar interests, and showing universities that you're genuinely motivated.

Now, the challenges, because every career has some, and you deserve to hear them. You never stop learning: technology changes very fast, which is exciting if you love learning, and tiring if you don't. It can be frustrating: sometimes you'll spend hours trying to find one small mistake in your code, so patience is essential. The work involves a lot of screen time, so you have to take care of your health. And getting your first job takes effort: you'll need real projects and good skills, not just a diploma. I'm not telling you this to scare you. I'm telling you so you can make an informed choice.

Now, let's have some fun. It's quiz time! I'm going to ask you four quick questions. For each one, choose A, B, C, or D, and remember your answers. Ready?

Question one: your friends are planning a trip. What do you naturally do? A: research the best route and build a step-by-step plan. B: make the group chat look nice and design a cool trip poster. C: compare prices and figure out the cheapest option with a spreadsheet. D: check that the hotel is safe, the booking site is real, and nobody's getting scammed.

Question two: which school activity sounds most fun? A: a robotics or programming competition. B: designing the yearbook or a poster for a school event. C: a math or science olympiad. D: an escape room or a detective game.

Question three: when something is broken, what do you do? A: try to fix it myself, step by step. B: think about how it could look or work better. C: look for a pattern: when does it break, and why? D: wonder who broke it, and whether it could happen again.

And question four: which superpower would you choose? A: build anything you imagine. B: know exactly how people feel when they use something. C: see the future in numbers. D: protect anyone from danger.

Okay, take a few seconds to count which letter you chose the most.

Mostly A: you might enjoy software development, building apps, websites, or games. Mostly B: you might enjoy UI and UX design, or product roles, where creativity meets technology. Mostly C: you might enjoy data science, data analysis, or artificial intelligence. Mostly D: you might enjoy cybersecurity.

And if your answers were mixed, that's great too. Many IT jobs combine these areas, and many people move between them during their careers. Of course, this is just a fun quiz, not a scientific test. But I hope it gives you a starting point to explore.

Let me give you a simple action plan for the next three months.

Month one: explore. Look up three IT jobs from today's list that interested you. Watch a "day in the life" video for each one, or better yet, ask someone who works in that field.

Month two: try. Complete one free beginner programming course, for example with Scratch or Python. Aim for just thirty minutes, three or four times a week.

Month three: build and share. Create one small project, anything you like, and show it to a friend, a family member, or a teacher.

At the end of these three months, ask yourself one question: "Did I enjoy this, even when it was hard?" If the answer is yes, IT might be a wonderful path for you. And if the answer is no, that's valuable too. You've learned something important about yourself.

You don't have to decide your whole future today. You just have to take the next small step.

Now, your questions. Don't be shy: if you're wondering about something, someone else in the room is wondering about it too.
