Even enthusiastic spectators fall into five common blind spots when watching foil and sabre fencing.

Misstep one: assuming whichever colored light turns on a split second earlier automatically wins the point. Under right-of-way, the second light to fire wins every time if that fencer held valid priority.

Misstep two: thinking two lights flashing together means both fencers receive a point. That simultaneous double-score rule exists only in épée; in foil and sabre, two lights mean the referee must assign priority to one fencer or declare a simultaneous reset with zero points awarded.

Misstep three: believing fencing is purely about fast hand speed while ignoring footwork. In reality, ninety percent of right-of-way battles are won or lost in the legs—controlling the half-step distance that makes an attack either reach or fall short.

Misstep four: assuming referees guess wildly during blindingly fast exchanges. Elite referees track shoulder initiation, footwork cadence, and blade acoustics simultaneously, breaking complex two-second exchanges into clear cause-and-effect sequences.

Misstep five: viewing right-of-way as arbitrary bureaucracy rather than martial logic. Every convention in the rulebook traces directly back to teaching duelists how to protect themselves before striking.

"Why doesn't fencing just use épée's first-to-touch rule for all three weapons?" Because removing right-of-way turns bouts into cautious, defensive waiting games punctuated by mutual double-hits, whereas right-of-way rewards proactive offensive initiative, blade-parrying artistry, and explosive footwork exchanges.
