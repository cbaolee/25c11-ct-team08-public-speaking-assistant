Even passionate hockey fans fall into five common blind spots when watching composite stick kick points.

Misstep one: assuming a higher flex number—like one hundred flex—always shoots harder than a seventy-five-flex stick. If an athlete lacks the body mass and downward leverage to bend a one-hundred-flex shaft, the stick acts like a rigid iron pipe and actually shoots slower than a properly flexed seventy-five-flex shaft.

Misstep two: thinking players bend their stick by hitting the puck itself harder. In modern shooting mechanics, the stick bends primarily because the player presses the blade downward into the ice slightly behind the puck before contact—using ice friction as the fulcrum to load the shaft.

Misstep three: believing all sticks used by players on the same NHL line have identical kick points and curves. Professional players customize exact flex ratings, kick-point tapers, shaft grip textures, and blade curves uniquely to their individual shooting biomechanics.

Misstep four: assuming cutting a stick shorter makes it easier to bend because it looks smaller. Shortening a beam reduces its bending lever arm—making a cut stick noticeably stiffer, not softer.

Misstep five: viewing stick technology solely as brute power enhancement rather than a timing-and-deception tool that lets shooters release pucks without changing their shoulder posture.
