First, breaking down the core elements of Reverse-VH post sealing and pad geometry.

In the Reverse-VH stance, the goaltender drops their post-side leg pad flat along the ice—wedging the toe of their skate blade or shin-pad channel snugly against the inside base of the metal goal post—while keeping their back-side leg upright with the skate blade planted firmly on the ice behind them like a loaded spring. Simultaneously, the goalie leans their torso and shoulder directly against the vertical post, tucking their glove or blocker tight to their ribs. Fifth, the common traps or myths surrounding Reverse-VH goaltending mechanics.

Myth one is that dropping into Reverse VH makes a goalie completely motionless and trapped against the post. Because the back-side skate remains planted vertically on the ice, a well-trained goalie actually pushes across the crease faster out of Reverse VH than out of a conventional double-knee butterfly.

Myth two is that Reverse VH should be used every single time an opponent skates near the corner of the rink. Using Reverse VH when the puck carrier sits higher in the circle drops the goalie's shoulders too low and invites high short-side snipes over the shoulder.

Myth three is assuming modern oversized goalie leg pads seal the ice automatically without technique. Without active hip rotation and square toe-to-post alignment, subtle gaps open instantly between the pad heel and the goal post.

Myth four is believing goalies stay glued firmly against the post until the puck physically crosses the goal line. Great goalies detach from the post a split second before a pass reaches the slot, gaining precious inches of depth to cut down the shooter's angle.

Myth five is thinking Reverse VH is purely a passive blocking posture rather than an active spring-loaded launch platform for lateral crease recovery.
