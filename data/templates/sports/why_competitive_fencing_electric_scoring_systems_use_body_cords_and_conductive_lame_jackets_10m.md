Second, comparing approaches and philosophies across épée, foil, and sabre electrical architectures.

Each of the three Olympic fencing weapons uses a distinctly clever circuit design tailored to its specific rules. Épée uses a normally open circuit: the blade tip houses a tiny spring-loaded plunger button that closes the circuit only when pressed inward with more than seven hundred fifty grams of force—meaning any valid touch anywhere on the opponent's body completes the loop and turns on a colored scoring lamp. Sabre requires no moving tip plunger at all because cutting hits score with the blade edge; any metallic contact between the steel sabre blade and the opponent's conductive lamé jacket or conductive mask completes the circuit immediately. Fifth, the common traps or myths surrounding electric fencing equipment.

Myth one is that fencers receive mild electric shocks when hit by an opponent's blade. The scoring box operates on ultra-low, harmless five-to-twelve-volt direct-current logic signals—athletes feel only the physical bend of the steel blade, never electricity.

Myth two is that the coiled wire running down a fencer's back pulls on them or restricts movement. The waist cord connects to a counter-balanced spring spool reel at the end of the strip that pays out and retracts line smoothly at any sprint speed without tugging.

Myth three is assuming modern wireless scoring systems have replaced wired body cords everywhere. While wireless telemetry vests appear in major televised Olympic finals for clean visual presentation, wired body cords and spool reels remain the universal gold standard across training clubs and world tournaments due to zero radio interference and instant reliability.

Myth four is believing any touch against a conductive lamé jacket scores automatically even if the blade hits flat without depressing the tip in foil or épée. In foil and épée, the tip plunger must depress linearly with calibrated spring force; slapping flat with the side of the blade will not break the tip switch.

Myth five is thinking conductive lamé vests last indefinitely without care. Sweat salts corrode fine stainless-steel and silver-plated threads rapidly, requiring fencers to rinse and air-dry lamés after every tournament to maintain low electrical resistance.
