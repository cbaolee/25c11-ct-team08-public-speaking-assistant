Fifth, the common traps or myths surrounding keirin derny pacing.

Myth one is that the derny is an electric bicycle or high-powered racing motorcycle. Traditional dernys are lightweight, small-displacement motorized bicycles whose pacers actually pedal continuously alongside the small motor to regulate speed smoothly to exact lap-split targets.

Myth two is that riders are forbidden from passing each other while the derny is still on the track. Riders can pass each other freely throughout the paced laps—they are simply prohibited from passing the derny's rear wheel until the pacer exits the track.

Myth three is assuming the rider who draws starting position one behind the derny wins almost every race automatically. Because position one becomes an immediate target for early ambushes, savvy sprinters often prefer sitting second or third wheel—letting the leader absorb wind and early attacks before launching a late slingshot.

Myth four is believing physical bumping and shoulder contact are illegal fouls in keirin. Controlled hip-and-shoulder contact while holding a straight line is a normal, legal part of high-speed keirin positioning—only head-butting, grabbing jerseys, or swerving abruptly across a rival's front wheel triggers relegation.

Myth five is thinking keirin is purely a test of raw leg strength rather than tactical timing. Deciding whether to launch a long two-and-a-half-lap sprint from the front or wait for a one-lap draft slingshot requires split-second race reading under intense lactic pressure.
