First, the underlying kinematic principle behind attacking the hurdle rather than jumping over it.

In projectile physics, vertical flight duration depends strictly on how high an athlete's center of gravity rises at takeoff. Elite sprint hurdlers minimize vertical lift by driving their lead knee upward fast toward the barrier while snapping the lower leg forward into a slightly bent, fast-snapping extension—paired with an aggressive forward torso lean that drops the chest toward the thigh. Simultaneously, the trail leg whips flat and horizontal around the side of the barrier rather than tucking underneath the hips. Second, how lead-leg attack and rapid snap-down play out in real Olympic competition.

Between barriers, elite hurdlers run an exact three-stride rhythm—requiring explosive horizontal step length without floating vertically. If a hurdler takes off too close to a hurdle, they are forced to jump steeply upward to clear the top bar, floating in the air and landing heavy on their heels with lost horizontal momentum. Top champions take off nearly two meters ahead of the barrier, grazing the top wooden edge lightly with their trailing ankle or shorts—which is completely legal under World Athletics rules as long as the hurdle is not knocked down deliberately with a hand or foot—and immediately pawing the track backward upon landing to maintain full sprint velocity.
