First, breaking down the core elements of VAR intervention protocols and threshold design.

To prevent video reviews from halting a ninety-minute match every thirty seconds over minor midfield throw-ins or routine fouls, International Football Association Board—IFAB—protocols restrict VAR intervention strictly to four game-changing categories: goals and offenses leading up to a goal, penalty-area spot-kick decisions, direct red-card incidents, and cases of mistaken identity. Furthermore, for subjective judgment calls like fouls or handballs, the VAR team may only recommend an on-field review if the replay reveals a clear and obvious error—preserving the on-field referee's primary authority rather than re-refereeing every contact from a remote booth. Fifth, the common traps or myths surrounding VAR protocols in international football.

Myth one is that the video official sitting in the remote control room overrules the on-field referee directly. For all subjective decisions—fouls, handballs, and red cards—the VAR can only recommend a review; the center referee alone makes the final call after viewing the pitch-side monitor.

Myth two is that coaches or team captains can request a limited number of VAR challenges per half like tennis or NFL football. Under current IFAB laws, every goal, penalty, and direct red card is checked automatically by the VAR team without coach challenge flags.

Myth three is assuming slow-motion replay always makes foul decisions clearer and fairer. Viewing a collision at twenty percent speed can make an accidental follow-through look malicious, which is why protocols instruct referees to watch impacts at full broadcast speed first to judge force and intent accurately.

Myth four is believing VAR removes every single debatable decision from football. Because laws governing handball silhouette and careless versus reckless contact remain inherently interpretive, video review eliminates factual errors while still leaving room for human judgment.

Myth five is thinking VAR pauses shrink total active ball-in-play time during a match. Because referees add exact VAR check durations onto stoppage time, net active playing minutes remain preserved even when total broadcast duration extends slightly.
