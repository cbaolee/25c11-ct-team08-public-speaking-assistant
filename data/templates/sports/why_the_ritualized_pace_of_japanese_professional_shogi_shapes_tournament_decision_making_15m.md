Once a player exhausts their main multi-hour thinking bank, shogi does not end abruptly on a sudden-death flag fall; instead, the player enters byōyomi—a strict sixty-second-per-move countdown read aloud calmly by the official timekeeper in ten-second intervals down to the final ten seconds.

"Why does shogi allow captured pieces to be dropped back onto the board instead of removing them permanently?" Historically, shogi's drop rule emerged in sixteenth-century Japan—reflecting the mercenary warfare era where captured soldiers switched allegiance and fought for their new commander—and mathematically, it prevents drawn endgames by keeping total material constant on the board.

"What happens if a player makes an illegal move—such as dropping a pawn onto a file that already has an unpromoted pawn—during a fast byōyomi countdown?" Under strict Japanese Shogi Association rules, any illegal move—called hansoku—results in immediate loss of the game on the spot, making calm verification essential even when the timekeeper reaches fifty-nine seconds.
