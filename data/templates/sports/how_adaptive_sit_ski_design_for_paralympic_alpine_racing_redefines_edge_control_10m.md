Begin at the turn initiation above the gate, where the sit-skier unweights the single ski by snapping their torso upright and extending both handheld outriggers forward—allowing the cambered race ski to flatten briefly and roll smoothly onto its new uphill edge.

Fifth, the common traps or myths surrounding Paralympic sit-ski edge control.

Myth one is that sit-skiers steer primarily by dragging their handheld outriggers in the snow like rudders. Dragging outriggers heavily actually creates braking friction and slows the ski down; elite racers carve turns almost entirely by tilting the single ski onto its steel edge through torso angulation.

Myth two is that racing on one ski instead of two makes sit-skiing inherently slower than standing alpine skiing. On steep, icy super-G and downhill courses, a single weighted ski carries lower snow-contact friction and compact aerodynamic drag, allowing sit-skiers to match or even exceed standing speeds on straightaways.

Myth three is assuming a stiffer, rock-hard shock absorber always gives better control on icy World Cup slopes. Overly stiff suspension bounces the rigid frame off ice bumps, causing the edge to lose contact and slide wide of the gate line.

Myth four is believing every sit-skier uses the exact same seat angle and ski length. Technicians spend hours adjusting forward seat ramp angles by single degrees to balance tip bite against tail release for each individual athlete.

Myth five is viewing sit-ski technology merely as assistive equipment rather than cutting-edge alpine engineering. Suspension linkages and vibration-damping composites pioneered in Paralympic sit-skiing have directly influenced modern ski-boot and binding vibration-control systems across recreational and Olympic alpine gear.
