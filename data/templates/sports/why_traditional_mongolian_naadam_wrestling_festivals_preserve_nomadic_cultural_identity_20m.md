Stage three: symbolic attire encoding history, humility, and fairness.

A Naadam wrestler wears a tight, open-fronted long-sleeved jacket called a zodog, brief trunks called shuudag, and upturned traditional leather boots called gutal. According to beloved Mongolian oral history, the open chest of the zodog originated after a disguised female wrestler defeated every male opponent at a royal Naadam—prompting a rule ensuring chest visibility while honoring her legendary prowess.

Equally practical, the heavy stitched fabric of the zodog provides reliable handgrips for upright throws without tearing, while the upturned toes of the gutal boots slide smoothly through grass without snagging ankles during sweeping leg trips.

Naadam operates as a pure single-elimination bracket starting with five hundred twelve—or in landmark anniversary years, one thousand twenty-four—competitors. There are no seeded protected brackets where stars avoid early elimination; after the third round, higher-ranked wrestlers earn the honor of choosing their opponents publicly, testing tactical judgment alongside physical skill.

Even appreciative international visitors fall prey to five common misconceptions about Mongolian Naadam wrestling.

Misstep one: assuming wrestlers must pin both shoulders flat to the ground like in Olympic wrestling. In Mongolian Bökh, touching any part of the body above the knee or elbow to the grass—even a fingertip or grazed knee—ends the bout immediately.

Misstep two: thinking the eagle dance is merely a flashy victory celebration performed after winning. The devekh is a sacred invocation performed before entering the field, during ceremonial pauses, and after victory to honor sky, earth, and opponent alike.

Misstep three: believing Naadam is only for professional full-time athletes living in the capital city. Any citizen may enter local Naadam brackets, allowing rural herders to test themselves directly against national champions on equal ground.

Misstep four: assuming having no time limits makes every match drag on endlessly. Because a single grazed knee ends the bout, many Naadam matches finish in explosive bursts under thirty seconds.

Misstep five: viewing Naadam merely as a nostalgic tourist pageant. For Mongolians across every generation, Naadam remains the primary civic milestone of the year—anchoring family reunions, provincial identity, and living oral history.
