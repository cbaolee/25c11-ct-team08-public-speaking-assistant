Every regulation sled rides on two parallel stainless-steel runners housed inside a precision aluminum or carbon-fiber blade holder—known as a chassis—bolted directly beneath the composite bucket seat. Second, comparing approaches and philosophies across positions and equipment eras.

Early adaptive sleds in the nineteen-seventies and eighties used heavy welded steel frames with flat, uncontoured runners that tracked straight like railroad tracks, forcing players to brake abruptly and pivot slowly. Modern Paralympic sleds pair ultra-light aerospace aluminum and carbon-fiber buckets with adjustable modular blade holders where technicians tune runner spacing—typically between one and a half and three inches apart—and rocker radius to match each athlete's role. Fifth, the common traps or myths surrounding sled hockey blades and equipment.

Myth one is that sled hockey players steer primarily by dragging their sticks in the ice like rudders. While sticks provide occasional tight pivot assists, high-speed carving turns come almost entirely from hip lean and weight transfer across contoured twin steel blades.

Myth two is that wider blade spacing always makes a sled better because it prevents tipping over. Overly wide runners prevent the athlete from rolling the sled onto its carving edges, forcing slow, wide skidding turns that forfeit speed in tight zones.

Myth three is assuming every player on the team uses the exact same blade hollow radius. Just like stand-up NHL skaters, sled hockey athletes customize hollow depths—choosing shallower hollows for faster glide on soft ice and deeper hollows for sharper edge bite on hard, cold rink surfaces.

Myth four is thinking the low seat height makes sled hockey less physical than stand-up ice hockey. Because athletes accelerate rapidly using powerful upper-body strokes and collide with rigid composite buckets inches off the ice, full-force body checking is a core, thrilling part of the game.

Myth five is viewing adaptive equipment simply as medical mobility aids rather than high-performance sports engineering. Modern sled chassis and blade metallurgy undergo wind-tunnel, finite-element stress, and ice-friction testing worthy of Olympic speed skating.
