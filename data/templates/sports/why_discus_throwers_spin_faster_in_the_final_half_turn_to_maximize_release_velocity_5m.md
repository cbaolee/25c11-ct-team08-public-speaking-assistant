First, the underlying angular-momentum principle behind accelerating in the final half-turn.

A great discus throw works like cracking a bullwhip: energy begins in the heavy, grounded lower body and accelerates progressively into lighter, faster-moving upper limbs. During the opening entry out of the back of the circle, the thrower keeps the disc trailing wide behind the hip—creating a large moment of inertia and wide rotational radius while building linear momentum across the circle. The instant the feet plant into the double-support power position at the front of the circle, the hips snap violently forward ahead of the shoulders, stretching the chest and core muscles like coiled rubber bands before whipping the long throwing arm around in a high-velocity arc. Second, how late-turn acceleration plays out in real Olympic competition.

Championship throwers deliberately pace the first turn smoothly so their feet land in the front power position ahead of their upper torso, unleashing maximum rotational whip cleanly inside the toe-board boundary without fouling.
