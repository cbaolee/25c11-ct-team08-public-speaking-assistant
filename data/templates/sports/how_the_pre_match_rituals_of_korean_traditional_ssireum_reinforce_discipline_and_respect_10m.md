Third, how a Korean ssireum bout works step by step from pre-match ritual to sand finish.

Begin at the arena bow and sand-circle entry, where both wrestlers step barefoot into the eight-meter circular sand pit—the morae-pan—exchanging formal bows toward the judges and each other to acknowledge shared respect for the arena and spectators.

Execute the kneeling satba grip ceremony, where both athletes drop to their right knees shoulder-to-shoulder, secure each other's waist and thigh sashes cleanly, and rest their chins lightly near each other's right shoulder while the referee adjusts hip alignment to millimeter parity.

Rise together on the referee's stand command—ireo-seo—lifting hips simultaneously without pulling or leaning prematurely so neither wrestler steals momentum before the official start whistle.

Explode into decisive multi-directional throws the instant the whistle blows, chaining inside leg trips—anjjari—hip lifts—baejigi—and sweeping ankle hooks—deulbaejigi—until one athlete's hand or torso touches the sand, followed immediately by helping the fallen rival up and exchanging a respectful closing bow.

Fifth, the common traps or myths surrounding Korean ssireum rituals and rules.

Myth one is that kneeling shoulder-to-shoulder before the whistle is a passive rest break without tactical significance. In reality, subtle pelvic positioning, foot spacing in the sand, and breath alignment during the kneeling bind set the mechanical foundation for the opening throw.

Myth two is that ssireum is won purely by whichever wrestler weighs more and pushes harder. Because both athletes hold identical waist-and-thigh straps, agile lighter technicians routinely use rapid hip pivots and inside leg hooks to lift and topple much heavier opponents.

Myth three is assuming the circular sand pit is soft like a fluffy beach volleyball court. Championship ssireum sand is finely sifted, slightly dampened, and raked to an exact medium firmness that cushions falls safely while giving bare feet solid push-off traction.
