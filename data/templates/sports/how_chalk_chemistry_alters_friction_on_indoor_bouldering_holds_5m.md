First, the underlying surface-chemistry principle behind moisture absorption and dry skin friction.

Human fingertips naturally secrete microscopic moisture and sebum oils through dense sweat pores—useful for gripping smooth glass, yet slippery against fine-grained sandstone or polyurethane climbing holds. Pure climbing chalk is composed primarily of basic magnesium carbonate, a lightweight, porous mineral powder with an enormous microscopic surface area that rapidly absorbs liquid sweat and neutralizes skin oils into dry, easily shed granules. Second, how chalk chemistry and modern formulations play out in real indoor competition.

Modern indoor competitors choose strategically among loose chunk chalk, fine powdered chalk, and alcohol-suspended liquid chalk—which evaporates quickly upon application to leave a thin, uniform base coat deep inside skin creases before adding a light dusting of loose powder. However, during a four-hour World Cup bouldering round where dozens of finalists attempt the same problem, excess chalk packs tightly into the tiny valleys of textured holds, smoothing out the micro-roughness into a slick white glaze.
