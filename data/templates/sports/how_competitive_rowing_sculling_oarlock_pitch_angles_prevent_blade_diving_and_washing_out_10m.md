Modern composite rigging splits that four-degree angle cleanly between an angled plastic bushing inside the swiveling oarlock and a slightly angled wear sleeve molded onto the carbon oar shaft—allowing boat riggers to swap numbered bushings in seconds to dial pitch from three degrees up to five degrees. Fifth, the common traps or myths surrounding rowing oarlock pitch.

Myth one is that four degrees of pitch means the oarlock tilts sideways away from the boat rather than rotating backward around the vertical pin axis. Pitch refers strictly to the fore-and-aft tilt of the blade face relative to vertical water flow during the drive, whereas lateral pin tilt is a separate adjustment called spread or lateral pitch.

Myth two is that adding extra pitch—say, seven or eight degrees—makes the boat faster by lifting the hull higher out of the water. Excessive pitch causes the blade to plane upward right out of the water halfway through the stroke—washing out and throwing away propulsive power.

Myth three is assuming zero degrees of pitch is the purest, most efficient setting for elite scullers. Because modern curved hatchet and smoothie blades naturally generate slight downward hydrodynamic suction when pulled horizontally, zero degrees of pitch causes blades to dive uncomfortably deep unless offset by roughly four degrees of positive pitch.

Myth four is believing scullers never need to check oarlock pitch once a brand-new boat leaves the factory. Plastic oarlock bushings and oar sleeves wear down gradually over thousands of strokes—reducing pitch by one to two degrees over a season and requiring periodic bushing replacement.

Myth five is thinking port and starboard sculling oars should have different pitch angles because the left hand crosses over the right hand at mid-stroke. Both left and right oarlocks must carry identical pitch angles so symmetrical water pressure keeps the needle-thin shell balanced level on its keel.
