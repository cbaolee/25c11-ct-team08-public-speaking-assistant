Every time a swimmer lifts their chin high out of the water like a water polo player to look for a buoy, their hips and legs sink downward—increasing frontal drag dramatically and breaking stroke rhythm. Master open-water swimmers execute low-profile crocodile sighting: lifting just their goggles barely an inch above the waterline during the front catch phase of a normal stroke, snapping a visual fix in a fraction of a second, and rotating smoothly to the side to breathe without lifting the head higher.

Out on open water with one-meter ocean swells, a small inflatable turn buoy sitting on the surface disappears completely every time the swimmer drops into a wave trough—making direct buoy sighting frustrating and unreliable.

Experienced navigators align the floating buoy during pre-race warm-up with a tall, permanent onshore landmark sitting directly behind it—such as a skyscraper spire, church steeple, coastal lighthouse, or mountain notch—so they can sight effortlessly off the towering background landmark even when waves hide the buoy itself.

Even passionate swimming fans fall into five common blind spots when watching open-water marathon swimming.

Misstep one: assuming open-water swimmers follow a painted line on the bottom or swim inside lane ropes. Open-water courses are marked solely by widely spaced floating buoys—often hundreds of meters apart—requiring swimmers to navigate open water completely unguided between turns.

Misstep two: thinking swimmers finish an open-water marathon by touching a wall flush with the water surface like a pool race. Open-water marathons finish beneath an overhead floating gantry where swimmers must reach upward out of the water to slap a vertical hanging touchpad suspended above the surface.

Misstep three: believing wetsuits are allowed in all Olympic open-water marathon races. World Aquatics permits wetsuits only when water temperature drops below eighteen degrees Celsius—and strictly prohibits them above twenty degrees Celsius, meaning most Olympic marathons are swum in standard textile swimsuits.

Misstep four: assuming drafting behind another swimmer in open water is illegal cheating like drafting in non-drafting triathlons. Drafting behind or beside rivals is completely legal and central to open-water marathon tactics—only physically pulling, grabbing, or obstructing another swimmer is penalized.

Misstep five: thinking open-water races are decided solely by fitness rather than spatial navigation, current reading, feeding execution, and pack positioning.
