Even passionate track-and-field fans fall into five common misconceptions about sprint hurdles and counterweights.

Misstep one: assuming track hurdles are bolted or weighted down so heavily that they never tip over when hit. Sprint hurdles are deliberately engineered to tip forward easily upon horizontal impact so athletes never suffer catastrophic tripping falls at thirty-five kilometers per hour.

Misstep two: thinking knocking down two or three hurdles during a race adds time penalties or lowers your placing. Under World Athletics rules, knocking down any number of hurdles accidentally with your legs or torso carries zero penalty—only knocking a hurdle down deliberately with hands or feet, or trailing a foot outside the vertical plane of the hurdle bar, results in disqualification.

Misstep three: believing heavier counterweights stay fixed permanently at the back of the hurdle base regardless of height. Because taller hurdle heights increase leverage against the base, internal counterweights must slide backward as height rises and forward as height lowers to preserve uniform tip-over force.

Misstep four: assuming steeplechase barriers tip over the exact same way as sprint hurdles. Three-thousand-meter steeplechase barriers are massive, heavy timber beams weighing up to one hundred kilograms designed specifically not to tip when distance runners step directly on top of them.

Misstep five: thinking hurdles face with the long base feet pointing forward down the track. The long horizontal base feet always point backward toward the approaching runners so the pivot hinge sits at the front edge of the frame.
